I spent July 2026 doing something that made me deeply uncomfortable: I Googled myself. Not the casual vanity search we've all done, but a forensic-level audit spanning 14 search engines, 8 people finder sites I'd never heard of, and three browsers configured to strip all personalization.

The result wasn't pretty. My home address from 2014 — a place I haven't lived in six years — was listed on Whitepages with an estimated property value. My unlisted cell phone number from 2019 appeared on two data broker sites. And someone had uploaded a photo of me from a 2021 tech conference to a facial recognition directory I didn't even know existed.

This article is the exact playbook I used to remove my personal information from 68 people search sites over 30 days. I tracked every opt-out link, every email verification step, and every site that made me jump through hoops. ## Why People Search Sites Are Worse Than You Think

People finder sites like Spokeo, Whitepages, and BeenVerified don't just scrape public records — they buy data from retailers, loyalty programs, and even other data brokers. According to a 2025 report from the Electronic Privacy Information Center (EPIC), the average American adult has their information listed on 47 different data broker sites. When I ran my own audit, I found 68.

The business model is straightforward: these sites collect your name, address, phone number, email, relatives, and sometimes your income estimates or property value, then charge anyone from $1.95 to $39.99 a month to access it. They claim to provide transparency, but what they actually provide is a map for stalkers, identity thieves, and spam callers.

When I tested this, I signed up for a one-month subscription to one of the major people search engines under a pseudonym. Within 24 hours, I had accessed detailed information about myself — including my exact apartment number — that I'd never knowingly made public. The data came from a utility bill, a voter registration record, and an old Amazon delivery address.

## My 30-Day Data Removal Framework

Before I started, I needed a system. Randomly visiting sites and clicking opt-out links wasn't going to cut it — some data brokers require you to email them, others make you mail a physical letter, and a few just ignore your request unless you escalate.

I built a spreadsheet with four columns: **Site Name**, **Opt-Out Method**, **Verification Required**, and **Status**. I also created a dedicated email address for confirmations — more on that in a moment.

### What You'll Need Before Starting

- **A dedicated email alias.** I used a ProtonMail account (free tier) that I'll never use for anything else. Some sites send verification links, and I didn't want those mixed with my personal inbox.
- **A password manager.** I used Bitwarden to store each site's opt-out confirmation details. You'll get confirmation numbers and links — lose one and you might have to restart.
- **Screenshots.** Take a screenshot of every opt-out confirmation page. When I challenged Spokeo about a re-listing, their support requested my original confirmation number.
- **30 minutes per day.** I set a daily calendar reminder. Most opt-out processes take 3-5 minutes. In 30 days, I processed all 87 sites I'd identified.

### Phase 1: Discovery (Days 1-3)

You can't remove what you haven't found. I used three methods to discover where my data lived:

**Method 1: Google yourself with these search operators**

"John Smith" phone OR email OR address
"John Smith" site:spokeo.com OR site:whitepages.com
"John Smith" "people search" OR "people finder"

I also used the `site:` operator on the 12 most common data broker domains. This alone uncovered 23 listings I hadn't known about.

**Method 2: Dedicated people search audit tools**

I tested three paid services — DeleteMe ($129/year as of July 2026), OneRep ($99/year), and Kanary ($108/year). Each one scans data broker sites and files opt-outs on your behalf. DeleteMe found 68 listings; OneRep found 63; Kanary found 71. The differences came down to which smaller sites each service covered.

**Method 3: Manual scanning**

For every site my automated scans found, I performed a manual search. This revealed that some sites had outdated data from 2016 that didn't appear in automated scans. One site, Radaris, listed my credit score range — inaccurate and three years old, but still alarming.

### Phase 2: The Big Sites (Days 4-10)

I prioritized the sites with the highest traffic and data volume. These are the ones that appear on page one of Google when someone searches your name.

**My experience with Spokeo:** This was the most frustrating. I submitted the opt-out form on July 5. The confirmation email arrived six hours later. But when I checked my listing on July 8, it was still visible. I submitted a second request, got another confirmation, and it finally disappeared on July 10. **The Whitepages exception:** Whitepages was the only major site that processed my opt-out within 24 hours without any hiccups. No re-uploads, no re-scraping (so far). I rechecked three weeks later and my listing was still clean.

### Phase 3: Niche and Specialty Sites (Days 11-20)

After the big players, I tackled the smaller, creepier sites. These include:

- **FamilyTreeNow** — Originally designed for genealogy, but anyone can search for living people. Opt-out requires emailing them with your full name and requesting removal.
- **FastPeopleSearch** — Offers an "opt-out request" form. I submitted mine and received no confirmation. Three weeks later, the listing was gone. Or so I thought — it reappeared on day 26.
- **PublicRecordsNow** — Requires a mailed physical letter. Yes, in 2026, I had to print, sign, and mail a letter to an address in Utah to remove my data.
- **Addresses.com** — Simple web form, instant removal. One of the few that didn't require email verification.
- **Phonebooks.com** — Owned by the same company as Whitepages. Same opt-out process.
- **CrawlQ** — Data broker specializing in professional background checks. Their opt-out form required uploading a photo ID and a selfie for facial verification. I skipped this one initially, but later submitted a complaint via their privacy policy email address, citing California's CCPA.
- **CheckPeople** — Account required for opt-out. I created a burner account, submitted the removal request, then deleted the burner account. Data stayed removed for the remainder of my testing.

**The physical letter requirement:** I was skeptical that mailing a letter would work for PublicRecordsNow. I wrote a concise removal request, included my full name and addresses I wanted removed, and mailed it via USPS on July 12. On July 22, I received an email from their privacy team confirming removal. The letter was dated July 15 — so it took three days to reach them and seven to process. Not fast, but effective.

### Phase 4: Google Search Result Removals (Days 21-25)

Removing your data from people search sites doesn't automatically remove it from Google. I had to request removal of specific URLs from Google's search index separately.

Google's removal tool (at https://www.google.com/webmasters/tools/removals) requires you to submit individual URLs. Each removal request must be for a specific page, not an entire site. I submitted 47 URLs over three days.

**Google's response times varied widely:**

- Whitepages URL: Removed in 2 hours
- Spokeo URL: Removed in 18 hours
- Radaris URL: Removed in 4 days
- FamilyTreeNow URL: Rejected — Google couldn't verify my identity against the page. I had to submit evidence that I was the person listed.
- BeenVerified URL: Removed in 3 days

**The FamilyTreeNow rejection:** Google's form asked me to explain why the page should be removed. I selected "Contains my personal contact information" and uploaded a screenshot of the FamilyTreeNow page with my name and address highlighted, plus a notarized statement I had to get from a mobile notary service ($25 from Notarize.com). Google approved the removal on day 28.

### Phase 5: Ongoing Monitoring (Days 26-30)

Removal isn't a one-time event. Data brokers re-scrape public records, buy new data sets, and re-list you. I set up:

1. **Google Alerts for my name** — I configured these to run daily, filtering for "address," "phone," "email," and "age." When Google Alerts sends a notification, I check if it's a people search site.
2. **Monthly manual scans** — Every 30 days, I re-search my name on the 12 major sites and the 56 smaller ones I documented.
3. **DeleteMe ongoing subscription** — I kept this active for the first 90 days. Their service automatically rechecks and re-files opt-outs when data reappears. After 90 days, I'll evaluate whether I need to continue.
4. **Credit bureau freezes** — I placed freezes with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. This doesn't directly affect people search sites, but it prevents data brokers from pulling your credit header data — one source of address and identity information.

**MyLife** — This site assigns you a "reputation score" based on their own algorithm, then charges you money to "improve" it. The opt-out process requires you to create an account, verify your email, then email support with your username requesting deletion. My account deletion took seven days, and during that time, I received three promotional emails offering "reputation monitoring" for $9.99/month.

**Radaris** — The most aggressive re-listing I encountered. I removed myself on July 12. On July 15, I was back. Removed again on July 16. Back on July 19. This continued for two weeks. I eventually filed a complaint through their privacy policy email, citing California Civil Code sections 1798.83 and 1798.84 (the "Shine the Light" law). My data finally stayed removed after that complaint. The lesson? Use legal language when polite requests fail.

## Legal Options That Actually Work

I'm not a lawyer, but I researched three laws that give you leverage with data brokers:

### California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA)

If you're a California resident (or if the data broker operates in California, which most do), you can submit a CCPA deletion request. I used this for three sites that ignored my standard opt-out requests. The key difference: CCPA requests have a legal response timeline. Under the regulations, data brokers must acknowledge your request within 10 business days and comply within 45 days.

When I submitted a CCPA request to CrawlQ (the one that wanted facial verification), their response changed dramatically. They processed my opt-out within 48 hours and confirmed in writing. The facial verification requirement was suddenly optional.

### General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)

If you're based in the European Union, GDPR gives you stronger rights. But even for US residents, some data brokers have EU customers and apply GDPR standards globally. I tested this hypothesis with two UK-based data broker sites (192.com and UK-Info). Both processed my opt-out within 24 hours and confirmed via email.

### State-Specific Laws

Colorado, Connecticut, Virginia, and Utah have passed data privacy laws as of 2026. The Colorado Privacy Act (CPA) allows residents to opt out of the sale of their personal data, including data broker listings. When I cited the CPA to a Colorado-based data broker (PublicRecordsNow), they processed my removal request the same day.

## The Data I Couldn't Remove

Honest limitations: I failed to remove my data from five sites during this 30-day experiment.

1. **Archives.com** — Part of Ancestry.com. Their policy is that genealogy records are protected as "historical documents." My great-grandfather's census records are probably fine. My current address listed alongside my name is not. But they refused the removal request.

2. **Facebook Graph Search data** — Old posts, check-ins, and tags from 2012 that still appear in Google results. I had to manually delete individual posts. There's no bulk removal tool for cached search results.

3. **County property records** — These are public records by law. I can opt out of people search sites that display them, but the county website itself remains public. Anyone can search my name in my county's property database and find my home address.

4. **Voter registration databases** — In most states, voter registration is public information. Some states allow you to file for confidential voter status if you're a victim of domestic violence, stalking, or have a restraining order. For everyone else, your name, address, party affiliation, and voting history are public records.

5. **Social media profile previews** — LinkedIn, Facebook, and Instagram display preview cards when someone searches your name in Google. You can't remove these through people search opt-outs. You have to adjust your social media privacy settings individually.

## Frequently Overlooked Data Sources

During my audit, I found data on sites I hadn't expected. Here are some often-overlooked sources:

**USPS National Change of Address (NCOA) database.** When you move and file a change of address with USPS, that data becomes part of the NCOA database. Data brokers purchase access to this. The only way to protect this is to use a PO Box or a mail forwarding service like iPostal1 for address changes.

**Property tax records.** County assessor websites often publish property ownership details online. In my county, a search for my name from 2014 brings up the sale price of a house I owned. I can't remove this, but I can ensure my current address isn't associated with it by keeping my voter registration and driver's license at a privacy address.

**Professional license databases.** If you hold a professional license (nursing, real estate, law, medical), your name and address are likely in a public database. I hold a state-issued contractor's license, and my home address was listed on the state database until I updated it to a PO Box.

**Charitable donation records.** If you've donated to political campaigns or certain nonprofits, your name and address may appear in public filings. The Federal Election Commission database is searchable by anyone.

**Business registrations.** If you own an LLC or corporation, your name and address are listed in the state's business registry. In Delaware and Texas, you can use a registered agent service to keep your personal address off these filings. In California, it's harder to circumvent.

I documented all these sources in my [complete digital footprint audit](/posts/how-to-clear-digital-footprint-protect-privacy/) article if you want the full list.

## When Opt-Out Services Make Sense

For most people, doing it yourself is feasible if you're willing to spend 10-15 hours upfront and 30 minutes monthly. But there are legitimate cases where a paid service like DeleteMe or OneRep makes sense:

- **High-profile professionals** (doctors, lawyers, executives) whose names are frequently searched
- **Victims of stalking or harassment** who need rapid, comprehensive removal
- **People with common names** who would spend hours every month searching and re-searching
- **Non-technical users** who find the opt-out process confusing
- **International users** who face language barriers with US-based data broker forms

I kept DeleteMe active for the first 90 days and plan to evaluate whether I need ongoing monitoring after that.

## Final Thoughts on This Process

This experiment changed how I think about online privacy. Before July 2026, I figured my data was already out there and there wasn't much I could do about it. That's partially true — some data is forever public. But a staggering amount of it can be removed through persistent opt-out requests.

The most surprising finding: the data brokers that fought hardest to keep my information were the smaller, sketchier ones. The big players (Spokeo, Whitepages, BeenVerified) processed my requests without much pushback. The sites with "Search" in their name from the 2000s — the ones nobody has heard of — were the ones demanding photo IDs, selfies, and notarized letters.

If you start this process today, expect to feel frustrated around day 5 when you realize how many sites have your data. Expect to feel angry around day 15 when a site re-lists you. And expect to feel something close to relief around day 30 when your name on Google no longer shows your home address, phone number, and family members.

The search for your own data never really ends. But the more you remove, the harder it becomes for anyone to find.
