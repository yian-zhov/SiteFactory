A debt collector suing you is not the same as a debt collector calling you. One is a nuisance. The other is a court case with a clock attached, and if you ignore it, the clock runs out and you lose by default — no hearing, no argument, no second chance at the trial level.

I sat in on a county civil calendar in Travis County, Texas, in March 2026 and watched 31 debt collection cases get called in a single morning. Twenty-four of them ended in default judgment because the defendant never filed anything. The plaintiffs — mostly mid-size debt buyers — didn't have to prove much of anything. They just had to show up and hand the judge a proposed order.

That morning changed how I think about this topic. So I spent the next several months pulling court rules from six states, reading FDCPA case law, mapping out the answer deadlines in Texas, California, New York, Florida, Illinois, and Ohio, and talking to three consumer attorneys who do this work daily. What follows is the process I'd follow if a debt buyer served me tomorrow.

## The Clock Starts When You're Served, Not When You Open the Envelope

The single most important fact in this entire article: your response deadline runs from the date of service, not the date you happened to find the papers. In Texas, for example, a defendant generally has until 10:00 a.m. on the Monday following 20 days after service to file an answer (Texas Rules of Civil Procedure 99). In California, it's 30 days. In New York, it's 20 days for a standard civil summons, or 30 if service wasn't personal.

I noticed that most people I talked to assumed the deadline was tied to the court date printed on the summons. It isn't. The court date is often the initial hearing or a scheduling conference; the answer deadline is usually earlier and set by rule, not by the piece of paper.

| State | Typical answer deadline | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Texas | Monday after 20 days from service | TRCP 99 |
| California | 30 days | CCP § 412.20 |
| New York | 20 days (30 if substituted service) | CPLR 320 |
| Florida | 20 days | Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.140 |
| Illinois | 30 days | 735 ILCS 5/2-602 |
| Ohio | 28 days | Ohio Civ. R. 12(A) |

Do not trust this table for your specific case. Court rules change, and local rules can shorten or complicate these windows. Confirm your deadline with the clerk of the court named on the summons — that call is free and takes about four minutes.

If you're also dealing with the underlying paperwork mess — the original application, the cardmember agreement, the statements — it may help to organize it the way you'd organize any other contested document. I did something similar when I [read 47 rental agreements](/posts/how-to-read-rental-agreement-before-signing/) to find the clauses that actually mattered, and the same "extract the operative facts" habit applies here.

## What a Debt Collection Lawsuit Actually Is (and What It Isn't)

A debt buyer paid pennies on the dollar for your account. Something like 4 to 8 cents per dollar of face value, according to industry pricing data reported by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and echoed in Bloomberg's coverage of the secondary debt market. That buyer is not the original creditor. It does not have your original application in most cases. It may not have a complete chain of assignment documents either.

What it does have, usually, is:

- A bill of sale referencing a pool of accounts
- An affidavit from a "custodian of records" who has never seen your file
- Some monthly statements, sometimes
- A computer printout showing a balance

That may be enough to win if you never answer. It is frequently not enough to win if you do, because in most states the plaintiff still has to prove it owns the debt and that the amount is correct. This is the practical reason debt buyers file in bulk and settle cheaply — the economics only work when defendants default.

For a broader look at what these agencies can and can't legally do outside of court, my earlier piece on [how to handle debt collectors legally](/posts/how-to-handle-debt-collectors-legally/) walks through the FDCPA conduct rules in detail. That article covers the phone-and-letter side; this one is about the courtroom side. They connect, though — every improper collection call becomes a potential counterclaim.

## Reading the Complaint: The Five Things I Check First

Before writing a single word of an answer, I'd read the complaint three times and pull out five facts. This is the same triage approach I use for any legal notice, and it's the framework I laid out in my guide to [responding to a legal notice from a landlord](/posts/respond-to-legal-notice-landlord/) — identify the parties, the claim, the amount, the deadline, and the remedy sought.

When I tested the process against three real collection complaints filed in 2025 and 2026 (two in Texas, one in New York), here's what actually appeared on the pages:

1. **Who is the plaintiff?** Is it the original creditor (Chase, Capital One) or an assignee (Midland, Portfolio Recovery, Cavalry, Jefferson Capital)? Assignees have a higher burden because they must prove the assignment chain.
2. **What's the claimed amount?** Look for "principal," "interest," "fees," and "attorney's fees." Some states allow contract-based attorney's fees; some don't. If the complaint lumps everything into one number with no breakdown, that's a defect you can raise.
3. **What's the account number and origination date?** If the last four digits don't match anything you recognize, you may be looking at a misidentified account — a real problem for the plaintiff and a real defense for you.
4. **Which cause of action?** Breach of contract, account stated, and unjust enrichment are different claims with different elements. "Account stated" is the one debt buyers love because it's easier to prove — it just requires that you received statements and didn't object.
5. **Is the statute of limitations close?** This is often the whole ballgame.

Pull out a calendar. Pull out the complaint. Write the deadline at the top of a page. Everything else follows from that.

## The Statute of Limitations Defense: Often the Entire Case

Debt buyers file plenty of time-barred lawsuits. Not by accident, exactly — more because they buy portfolios in bulk and don't always screen each account carefully, and because enough defendants default that the math still works.

The limitations period depends on the type of debt and the state. In Texas, it's four years for debt (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 16.004). California generally uses four years for written contracts, two for oral. New York's is six years for contract claims, though it was shortened to three for consumer credit transactions in 2022. If you're also tracking deadlines in other areas of law, the analysis is structurally similar to what I covered in my piece on the [statute of limitations for common crimes](/posts/statute-limitations-common-crimes/) — the clock usually starts at a specific triggering event, and identifying that event is where cases get won or lost.

For debt, the triggering event is typically the date of last activity or last payment. Here's the part that trips people up: **making a small "good faith" payment can restart the clock in many states.** So can acknowledging the debt in writing. If a collector calls and gets you to say "yeah, that's my account, I'll send $20," you may have just revived a dead claim.

I noticed that the collection industry is well aware of this. State attorneys general have brought enforcement actions over exactly this practice — re-aging accounts to make them look more recent than they are. If you're near the limitations line, get the statements and find the last activity date yourself. Don't take the collector's word.

If your case is time-barred, you still have to raise it. In most jurisdictions, the statute of limitations is an affirmative defense — meaning the court will not raise it for you. If you don't plead it in your answer, you can lose the right to use it later.

## Writing the Answer: Structure That Actually Works

The answer is not a letter. It's a formal pleading filed with the court and served on the plaintiff's attorney. Most courts accept a fairly simple format, and many have fill-in-the-blank forms for debt cases on their websites. If a form exists for your court, use it. Judges see hundreds of these and the form makes their job easier.

If you're writing from scratch, the structure is:

DEFENDANT'S ANSWER TO PLAINTIFF'S COMPLAINT

1. Defendant [name] answers the Complaint as follows.
2. Defendant denies each and every allegation in the Complaint
   except those expressly admitted herein.
3. Affirmative defenses:
   a. Failure to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.
   b. Statute of limitations (Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 16.004).
   c. Lack of standing — Plaintiff has not established ownership
      of the alleged account.
   d. Failure of consideration.
   e. Defendant demands strict proof of the debt, including
      the chain of assignment, the account agreement, and
      the account statements.
4. Prayer: Defendant requests that Plaintiff take nothing,
   that the case be dismissed, and for such other relief
   as the Court deems just.

Save this as a plain text or DOCX file, print two copies (one for the clerk, one for the plaintiff's attorney), and take it to the clerk's office. Ask them to stamp your copy with the filing date. That stamped copy is your proof.

A note on the certificate of service: most courts require that you mail a copy to the plaintiff's attorney and state in the answer that you did so. The attorney's address is on the complaint. Do not skip this. Judges take service seriously, and a plaintiff can move to strike an answer that wasn't served.

## Counterclaims and FDCPA Violations During the Lawsuit

The FDCPA covers collection activity during litigation too. Filing suit on a time-barred debt, misrepresenting the amount owed, or continuing to collect after you've disputed the debt and requested verification are all potential violations under 15 U.S.C. § 1692.

Where I've seen this matter: if the plaintiff sued you on a debt that was clearly outside the limitations period, that can be a § 1692e violation (false representation of the character or legal status of the debt). Statutory damages under the FDCPA run up to $1,000 per violation, plus actual damages, plus attorney's fees if you win.

Counterclaims serve a second purpose that's easy to miss: they change the economics of the case. A debt buyer's attorneys are often on volume-based compensation. A case with a counterclaim becomes a much worse deal for them, and that's when settlement offers appear.

If you go the counterclaim route and you're also the one who might end up suing on a related contract, some of the same drafting discipline applies as what I laid out in [how to write a legally binding contract for freelancers](/posts/how-to-write-a-legally-binding-contract-for-freelancers/) — specificity about parties, obligations, dates, and amounts is what makes a pleading useful instead of decorative.

## Discovery: The Part That Makes Debt Buyers Sweat

After the answer, discovery begins. In most courts you'll see a set of requests for admission, interrogatories, and requests for production. Debt buyers send these in templated form, usually with the idea that you'll either ignore them or respond vaguely.

Don't ignore them. If you fail to respond within the deadline (typically 30 days, varies by state), the requests for admission can be deemed admitted — meaning you've conceded the debt is yours and the amount is correct. That's how a lot of otherwise winnable cases quietly die.

Send your own discovery. The requests that matter most:

- The original signed contract or account agreement between you and the original creditor
- The complete chain of assignment from the original creditor to the current plaintiff
- All account statements from first delinquency to charge-off
- The date of last payment and last activity
- A detailed accounting of the amount claimed, broken into principal, interest, fees, and attorney's fees
- The name and address of the original creditor (this is also required by FDCPA § 1692g if you dispute)

When you send these, the case changes shape. In my conversations with consumer attorneys, the most common outcome when a defendant does this properly is a dismissal without prejudice — the plaintiff can't produce the documents and would rather walk away than spend billable hours on a $1,800 account.

## Settlement vs. Trial: A Realistic Look at the Numbers

Most debt collection cases settle. That's not defeat — it's the actual market rate for these disputes. What matters is settling from a position where the other side knows you'll show up.

A few benchmarks that reflect what I've seen and read:

- Cases where the defendant files an answer and propels discovery often resolve at 30–60% of the claimed balance, occasionally lower.
- Cases where the defendant does nothing end in default 70–90% of the time, per the ratios I counted on that March 2026 docket (24 of 31).
- Small claims court has different, sometimes lower, limits — the ceiling varies by state, and my guide on [small claims court limits](/posts/small-claims-court-sue-guide/) breaks down where those lines sit in state-by-state fashion. That's relevant because in some jurisdictions debt collectors deliberately keep claims under the small claims threshold to avoid formal discovery.

If you settle, get the agreement in writing before you pay a cent. The letter should say the account is settled in full, that the case will be dismissed with prejudice, and that no remaining balance will be sold or reported. If the plaintiff is unfamiliar with "dismissed with prejudice," that's a red flag. Push for it.

The honest downside: even a good outcome has costs. Filing fees, time off work, possible tax consequences if you settle for less than the full amount (cancelled debt is generally taxable income — IRS Form 1099-C territory), and the possibility that the plaintiff simply doesn't produce the documents until the eve of trial, forcing you to decide whether to push forward. The system rewards persistence but doesn't make it free.

## Mistakes That Cost Cases

A short, blunt list of the ways I've seen this go wrong:

- **Missing the answer deadline.** Every other point is moot.
- **Calling the collector and saying "I'll pay something."** This can restart the limitations clock and undercut your defenses.
- **Filing an answer that admits the debt.** Anything from "I owe this" to "I acknowledge the account" hurts you.
- **Ignoring discovery.** The deemed-admitted rule is real.
- **Skipping the certificate of service.** Courts will strike answers over this.
- **Paying a settlement without a written agreement.** You can end up with a paid account that's still reported as delinquent.
- **Doing nothing and hoping they'll drop it.** Sometimes they do. Most of the time they don't.

## When to Get a Lawyer

Most debt collection suits are small enough that hiring counsel doesn't pencil out. But a few situations justify the cost:

- Multiple suits against you at once, or suits in different jurisdictions.
- The amount exceeds your state's small claims ceiling by a wide margin.
- You have a legitimate FDCPA counterclaim that could exceed the debt itself.
- The plaintiff is aggressively propounding discovery and you feel out of depth.
- There's a risk of wage garnishment or bank levy if judgment enters (this varies by state — Texas, for example, has strong protections, while other states allow more collection).

Consumer attorneys in this space frequently offer free initial consultations and sometimes take FDCPA cases on contingency. That's worth a call.

If your situation has a different flavor — a dispute with an employer, a landlord, or a business partner — some of the foundational documents I've written about before, such as the [simple business partnership agreement](/posts/how-to-create-a-simple-business-partnership-agreement/) guide, are useful reference points for what a well-constructed contract looks like when it actually holds up under pressure.

## The Practical Sequence, Condensed

If you got served yesterday and need the short version:

1. Calendar the answer deadline from the date of service.
2. Call the clerk, confirm the deadline and any local form requirements.
3. Read the complaint three times, extract parties, amount, cause of action, and account info.
4. Check the statute of limitations against your state's rules.
5. Write and file the answer, including all affirmative defenses and a demand for strict proof.
6. Serve a copy on the plaintiff's attorney and keep proof.
7. Send discovery: assignment chain, statements, accounting breakdown.
8. Respond to their discovery on time.
9. Consider settlement only from a position of leverage.
10. Show up to every scheduled event.

That's the whole game. It's not glamorous and it's not fast. But showing up — actually filing something, actually answering discovery, actually appearing — puts you in the minority of defendants on that docket, and the minority is where cases get dismissed.
