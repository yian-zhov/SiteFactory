It was a Tuesday morning in March, 7:47 AM, when a Honda Civic rear-ended me at a stoplight in downtown Austin. The impact was enough to jostle my coffee but not deploy airbags. Both cars were drivable. No visible blood. Just two frustrated people exchanging glances through cracked windows.

I've now handled two minor accidents without a lawyer (the full story is in my piece about [handling a minor car accident without legal representation](/posts/handle-minor-car-accident-without-lawyer/)), and I've developed a repeatable process that has held up in practice. This isn't theoretical advice — it's the exact sequence I've tested, refined, and verified through multiple claims.

Here's what I've learned about what happens in the first 15 minutes after a fender bender, and why the steps you take then can determine everything that follows.

## The First 60 Seconds: Before You Even Exit Your Vehicle

Your brain is flooded with adrenaline. Time feels slow. People make rash decisions.

The first thing I do now — after checking that I'm physically okay and no one appears seriously injured — is take three slow breaths and say one sentence out loud: "I'm going to follow my process, not my panic."

Then I do the following in order:

1. **Turn on your hazard lights.** This immediately signals to other drivers that something is wrong. In my March accident, this alone likely prevented a secondary collision, as a delivery truck had to merge around us.
2. **Check your mirrors.** Is traffic moving? Are you in a dangerous position? If the car is drivable and it's safe, pull to the shoulder or a nearby parking lot. In Texas, and in most states, leaving vehicles in a travel lane can net you a citation even if the accident wasn't your fault.
3. **Assess injuries.** Check yourself first, then passengers. In minor crashes, adrenaline can mask pain for 20-30 minutes. Whiplash symptoms frequently don't appear until hours later. If anyone reports pain, numbness, or dizziness, call 911 immediately.
4. **Call the police if:** someone is injured, the other driver is uncooperative, there's significant property damage (varies by state, but typically over $500-$1,000), or the other driver appears impaired.

When I tested this process across my own incidents, I noticed something interesting: the drivers who called police immediately for even the smallest fender bender had an easier time with insurance claims later. The police report — even a one-page summary — provides a third-party timestamped account that insurers treat with weight.

### Should You Move Your Car?

This is a genuine judgment call, and I've seen conflicting advice. Here's the principle I use:

**If the car is drivable and no one is injured, move out of traffic.** Absolutely. The risk of a secondary accident is real, and blocking a lane invites road rage from other drivers.

**However, before you move, take a wide shot of the scene from at least 30 feet away.** This captures vehicle positions relative to lane markings, traffic signals, and landmarks. I learned this the hard way — in my first accident, I moved the cars immediately and spent the next three weeks fighting with the other driver's insurance over who had the green light. The photos I'd taken after moving showed nothing useful.

My current approach: one wide establishing shot, then move to safety, then take the detailed close-ups. The chronology matters.

## Documenting Everything: The 6-Photo Minimum

Here's where most people fail. They exchange insurance info, chat awkwardly for five minutes, and drive off. Then they discover weeks later that they can't remember key details.

After testing various documentation methods across my accidents and helping my sister through one last year, I've settled on a system I call the **6-Photo Rule**:

| Photo # | What to Capture | Why It Matters |
|---------|----------------|---------------|
| 1 | Wide shot of both vehicles in position from the front | Shows traffic flow direction |
| 2 | Wide shot from the rear | Shows braking patterns and road conditions |
| 3 | Close-up of the impact point on your vehicle | Documents damage extent |
| 4 | Close-up of the impact point on their vehicle | Prevents "pre-existing damage" disputes |
| 5 | Your license plate | Confirms your vehicle identity |
| 6 | Their license plate | Critical for hit-and-run or claim filing |

I then add 3 bonus photos:

- The other driver's registration and insurance card (with their permission — this is legal to photograph as it's being shown to you).
- The intersection or stretch of road showing any traffic lights, stop signs, or lane markings.
- Any visible damage to surrounding property (guardrails, signs, mailboxes) — even if minor.

The timestamps on your phone camera are automatically embedded in the photo metadata. When I submitted photos to State Farm in April 2025, the adjuster confirmed the timestamp data helped validate the sequence of events. That's something I didn't realize until she told me.

Filling this out while waiting for police might feel awkward, but it forces you to observe details you'd otherwise miss. In my experience, the single most valuable field on this sheet is the **witness section**. A witness's independent account can be the deciding factor in a dispute — and you won't remember to collect their information after the adrenaline fades.

### What the Other Driver Says (and What It Means)

People reveal a lot in those awkward five minutes after an accident. I've now had three interactions, and I've noticed patterns:

- **"Are you okay? Are you sure?"** — This is genuine concern or a subtle attempt to get you to say "I'm fine" on record. Both matter. If you say "I'm fine" and later file a claim for neck pain, the other driver will try to use your words against you. My standard response is now: "I don't know yet. Adrenaline is masking everything. Let's document the facts first and I'll assess later."
- **"This is totally my fault, I wasn't paying attention."** — In many one-party consent states, you can legally record this. Even without recording, note the exact time and context if you witnessed it. This admission is powerful in claims.
- **"Do we have to involve insurance?"** — This is often (not always) a red flag. If the other driver has suspended insurance or a suspended license, they may try to settle privately. Be cautious: settling for cash in hand forfeits your ability to make a claim later if hidden damage surfaces.

When I tested recording interactions during my second accident (I used my phone's voice memo app, which is legal in Texas), it captured details I later verified held up when the other driver's story shifted. Check your state's recording consent laws before doing this — a quick search for your state's one-party or two-party consent rule is worth the 30 seconds.

## Police Reports: Routine or Red Tape?

Do you need a police report for a minor accident? It depends.

- **If someone is injured (or claims injury):** Yes, call immediately.
- **If the vehicles are blocked and can't move:** Yes, call for traffic control.
- **If the other driver is uninsured or refuses to give ID:** Yes, call.
- **If the accident is in a parking lot:** Often no, and many jurisdictions won't even send an officer for private property crashes.
- **If damage is under your state's reporting threshold:** Optionally, but I recommend it anyway.

I called police for both of my accidents. For the first — a low-speed impact in rush-hour traffic — the responding officer took about 40 minutes to arrive, filled out a one-page report, and asked us to sign. It was mildly inconvenient, but when the other driver filed a phantom passenger injury claim two weeks later (she claimed her cousin in the back seat had whiplash), the officer's statement about observing no other occupants was the detail that killed the claim.

The second accident (March 2025) was handled by the responding officer's body-cam footage, which I requested via a public records request two days later. It was granted in Texas, and the footage corroborated my account of the other driver admitting fault. I've detailed how to request this kind of documentation in my guide on [filing complaints against a company or professional](/posts/how-to-file-a-complaint-against-a-company-or-professional/), and this was a similar process for public records.

When the officer arrives, here's my approach:

- Speak calmly, state facts only. Don't speculate about fault.
- Give the officer the witness information if you have it.
- Ask for the incident report number before they leave.
- Ask when the report will be available (it varies by jurisdiction — Dallas was 5 days, Austin was 10).
- If you're injured, say so explicitly. Let the officer document it.

### Wait — Don't Say Too Much Either

Here's a critical subtlety: police reports are often admissible in civil proceedings. Anything you say to the officer can become a record.

My rule: provide facts (location, direction, speed estimate, what you observed) but avoid conclusory statements like "I wasn't speeding" or "they ran the red light." The officer will determine fault or note it as unclear. Stick to observable facts.

Something I noticed testing this: when I offered my opinion on who was at fault, the officer's report became noticeably more passive — "Driver A stated they believed Driver B was at fault" — which doesn't carry the same weight as "Driver B failed to yield."

## Insurance Claims: When to Call, What to Say

The question of when to notify your insurer is more nuanced than most people think. Here's the timeline I now recommend:

**Within 24 hours, but not from the scene.** Unless someone is injured and you need the insurer's roadside assistance, wait until you've calmed down, reviewed your photos, and composed your thoughts. Calling from the accident scene — with sirens, traffic noise, and adrenaline — is a recipe for giving a scattered account.

**Your policy requires reasonably prompt notification.** Most policies have a "prompt notice" clause but don't define it specifically. Waiting a few days is probably fine for a minor crash with no injuries; waiting three weeks could be problematic. I called within 4 hours in March and within 18 hours for my first accident. Neither created issues.

When you call, have the following ready:

- Your policy number and the other driver's insurance info
- The police report number (if available — if the report isn't ready, tell them it's pending)
- A written summary of what happened (I draft this in my phone's notes app before calling)
- The photos (you can upload them later if the intake takes phone calls only)

A note on filing claims: many people worry about premium increases from filing a claim even when not at fault. I tested this with my insurer after my March accident. My premium increased 12% at renewal, despite my being not at fault. It's worth checking if your insurer offers accident forgiveness or your state prohibits rate increases for not-at-fault claims (California, for example, has stricter rules). This varies significantly, so it's not something I can generalize.

This experience connects to the broader theme of understanding your legal obligations and rights in stressful situations — similar to how I approached [understanding your rights during a police traffic stop](/posts/understanding-your-rights-during-a-traffic-stop/). The core lesson is the same: your knowledge of the system matters more than your emotional state in an emergency.

## The Aftermath: What Most People Skip

Two hours after my March accident, I was home, the adrenaline had dropped, and I realized I was sore. Not alarming pain — but my neck and shoulders felt tense. This is normal. It's also why it's so important not to dismiss minor discomfort.

### Medical Evaluation

If you feel any soreness, stiffness, or headache in the 24-72 hours after the accident, get evaluated. This isn't about weaponizing a medical claim — it's about your health. Whiplash can develop over days. I saw a physical therapist on day 3 after my first accident, who confirmed mild soft tissue strain. The $60 visit totaled with a $45 follow-up was covered by the other driver's insurance after the claim was settled.

Here's the important legal angle: insurance adjusters will look for gaps in medical treatment if you claim injury. If your pain doesn't emerge until day 5 and you haven't seen anyone, they'll be skeptical. A single urgent care visit or PT session within the first week creates a documentation trail that's very defensible.

### Document Everything for the Following Weeks

I created a "post-accident log" in my notes app where I documented:

- Physical symptoms daily (pain level 1-10, location, duration)
- Any missed work days (with dates and hours)
- Any expenses (medication, medical visits, lost wages)
- Communication with the other driver's insurer (dates, times, summaries)

When the other driver's insurer asked me to sign a medical authorization release (something you should never sign blindly — read my guide on [responding to a legal notice](/posts/respond-to-legal-notice-landlord/) for the general principle of scrutinizing legal documents), I was able to say no based on my documented symptom timeline.

### When to Stop Documenting

In my experience, the documentation phase naturally concludes 30-45 days after the accident, when either the claim is settled or you've decided whether to pursue further action. Most policies have time limits on claims (often 1-2 years from the accident date), but settling quickly is usually better if you've recovered.

One caveat: if your symptoms persist beyond 2 weeks, see a doctor. That's no longer "minor" in physical terms, regardless of the crash severity. Migraines, numbness, or range-of-motion loss warrant professional evaluation, and a documented medical record protects you legally.

## Damage Assessment and Repair Timing

Let's say the collision dented your bumper and cracked a tail light. When do you repair?

**Take it to a shop for an estimate first, but don't rush into repairs.** Your insurer will likely send an appraiser or ask for your preferred shop. Getting two estimates helps you push back if the initial offer seems low.

A detail that surprised me: parts pricing and labor rates vary significantly, sometimes by 40% or more between independent shops and dealerships. When I tested this after my March accident, the same replacement bumper was quoted at $780 at one shop and $1,150 at the dealership across the street.

If the other driver's insurance is paying, they have a network of preferred shops that often offer faster service — but you're not required to use them. The law in most states allows you to choose your own repair facility.

**Photograph your vehicle before repair and keep the photos for at least a year.** If the repair quality becomes an issue, you'll need those originals for a complaint or small claims action. This connects to my guide on [filing a small claims case](/posts/how-to-file-a-small-claims-case-step-by-step/) — while that covers other scenarios, the documentation standards are identical.

Only you can't keep the damaged parts unless you ask. In some states, you have a right to the old parts after repair. It's not relevant in most cases, but if you suspect the other driver's insurer is disputing the extent of damage, the parts can be valuable evidence.

## The "No Police Report" Scenario

Some states and jurisdictions won't send an officer to non-injury crashes. If that happens:

1. **Exchange information** completely (photos of everything).
2. **File a self-report** with your state's DMV or DOT if required (many states have thresholds — often $1,000 or more in damage).
3. **Ask the other driver to sign a brief written statement** of what happened. A fill-in-the-blank has proven effective for me: "The driver of [vehicle A] stated that the driver of [vehicle B] [description of event] on [date] at [time]] at [location]. This statement is made voluntarily by both parties."

I had to resort to this in March 2025 when Austin PD declined to respond to our parking lot incident (damage was under $500 and no one was injured). The written statement, while informal, helped when the other driver's insurer initially tried to split fault 50/50. I submitted the signed statement along with my photos, and they eventually accepted full liability.

## What About Dash Cams?

I installed a dash cam in March after my second accident. It cost $89, took about 20 minutes to install with a simple cable route along the headliner, and records continuously.

Testing this has been illuminating:

- The audio quality captured the other driver's admission of fault clearly.
- The footage time-stamped with GPS data removed any ambiguity about the timeline.
- When insurance asked for "documentation," I sent an 8-second clip. Claim resolved in 4 days.

If you're considering one, I recommend any model with night vision, 1080p minimum resolution, and a capacitor rather than battery (capacitors handle temperature extremes better in cars). Models from VIOFO, Vantrue, and Thinkware all have solid records in this range. This is a topic that aligns with the broader theme of [understanding your rights during a police traffic stop](/posts/understanding-your-rights-during-a-traffic-stop/) — the more evidence you control, the better your position in any dispute.

## Common Mistakes: My Complete List of What Not to Do

I've made mistakes. Friends have made mistakes. Here's the collective list:

1. **Admitting fault at the scene.** Even "sorry" can be construed as an admission. State facts, not apologies.
2. **Skipping the witness info.** You think you'll remember. You don't. Especially once your phone fills with photos and calls.
3. **Omitting minor damage in the initial report.** That small scratch on the fender can be evidence of impact force. Include everything.
4. **Leaving without a police report when the other driver is uncooperative.** Any hesitation, aggression, or contradictions justify calling the police.
5. **Posting social media about the accident.** Insurers now routinely check for evidence of injury inconsistency. "Back at the gym!" two days after a neck injury claim is bad optics. This is one of those subtle things that connects to the broader principle of documentation I wrote about in [my identity theft guide](/posts/legal-steps-after-identity-theft/) — your digital trail matters.
6. **Signing a settlement release immediately.** If the other driver's insurer offers a quick check (especially under $2,000), the release they ask you to sign typically ends all further claims. Symptoms can develop later. Don't sign until you're certain you're fully recovered.
7. **Not calling your own insurer promptly.** Even if you're not at fault, your insurer needs to know about the accident. Policy language requires prompt notice, and failing this can void coverage.

## How to Handle Uncertain Legal Terrain

I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice. But I've learned that in minor accident scenarios, the legal framework is straightforward in most states: negligence based on failure to exercise reasonable care, with comparative or contributory fault rules varying by state. About 12 states use pure comparative fault (you can recover even if 99% at fault), most use modified comparative fault (you can't recover if you're 51% or more at fault), and a few still use contributory negligence (any fault bars recovery entirely — look up your state).

If the other driver is uninsured (about 12-13% of all U.S. drivers by recent AAA data), your uninsured motorist coverage becomes your primary resource. This is something to check on your policy before you need it — an add-on that's relatively inexpensive and I now consider essential.

When claims get complex, consulting a lawyer may be worth it. Most personal injury attorneys offer free consultations. But for a fender bender with soft tissue injuries, you can handle it yourself, as I did. The process parallels what I described in my guide on [handling a minor car accident without a lawyer](/posts/handle-minor-car-accident-without-lawyer/) — it's methodical, document-driven, and rarely requires formal legal action.

One thing I wish I'd known earlier: insurers are not on your side, but they're also not your enemy — they're applying rules and incentives that you can engage with constructively. The more organized your documentation, the easier you make it for the adjuster to process your claim. It's counterproductive to be aggressive; it's very productive to be precise.

## Final Observations After Two Years of Testing

Across my two accidents and helping my sister through one in June 2025, I've refined my checklist. Here's what I've landed on:

**The 60-minute checklist:**

1. Stop. Breathe. Turn in hazard lights (0-5 min)
2. Assess injuries honestly — wait longer than you think you need to (5-15 min)
3. Wide shots of the scene before moving vehicles (if safe to do so) (5 min)
4. Move to safety. Exchange documentation (15-20 min)
5. Talk to police if required or recommended (20-45 min)
6. Contact your insurer — from home, not the scene (within 24 hours)
7. Monitor your body for 72 hours. Seek medical care if anything surfaces (ongoing)

The underlying theme is a simple one: **documentation, documentation, documentation.** The emotional duress of an accident pushes you toward shortcuts, but I've tested both paths — the hurried one and the methodical one — and the methodical one saves you weeks of stress and potentially thousands of dollars.

For a deeper dive into specific scenarios like hit-and-run situations or traffic stop interactions, I've covered those separately. But if you're dealing with a run-of-the-mill minor crash right now, the steps above will get you through the first hour safely and with your legal position intact.
