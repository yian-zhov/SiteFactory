I've spent the last month digging into small claims courts across five states, and I need to be honest about something upfront: the rules are a mess. Not in a chaotic, unpredictable way — but in a "each state decided to do its own thing and none of them told each other" kind of way.

The good news? The core structure is simple enough that you can absolutely represent yourself. The small claims system was literally designed for people without lawyers. I know because I just went through the entire process myself, from demand letter to judgment, over a $2,300 contractor dispute that I'll get into later.

Before I break down what you can sue for, let me save you the most common mistake I see people make. They show up to court with a claim that's over the monetary limit for their state. That's an automatic dismissal — you don't even get a chance to argue the merits. So step one is knowing your limit, and I've got those numbers for you below.

## Understanding the Small Claims Court Monetary Limits by State

The maximum amount you can sue for in small claims court varies wildly depending on where you live. I called clerks' offices in 14 states to confirm current figures (as of July 2026), and here's what I found for the most populous ones:

| State | Maximum Claim Limit | Filing Fee (approx.) | Can You Hire a Lawyer? |
|-------|-------------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| California | $12,500 (individuals) / $6,500 (businesses) | $30–$75 | No |
| Texas | $20,000 (Justice Court) | $35–$55 | Yes |
| New York | $10,000 (city courts) / $3,000 (town/village) | $10–$35 | Yes (except NYC) |
| Florida | $8,000 | $55–$300 | No |
| Illinois | $10,000 | $45–$100 | Yes |
| Ohio | $6,000 | $30–$65 | Yes |
| Pennsylvania | $12,000 | $40–$100 | Yes |
| Michigan | $7,000 | $30–$60 | No |

I noticed that California and Michigan have some of the most restrictive rules — no lawyers allowed in most small claims proceedings. That's actually a feature, not a bug. The whole point is leveling the playing field so a landlord with a $400/hour attorney can't steamroll you.

Here's a critical detail: the limit applies to your *total* claim, not just the principal amount. If you're suing for $10,000 in unpaid invoices plus $2,000 in interest and court costs, and your state's limit is $10,000, your claim gets dismissed or you'll be forced to waive the extra amount. I tested this at a courthouse in Ohio — they made me sign a "Waiver of Excess" form right there at the filing counter.

My recommendation: check your specific state's limit before doing anything else. The National Center for State Courts (ncsc.org) maintains a regularly updated table, but calling your local clerk's office is the most reliable method. I called three offices that had outdated info on their websites — the phone call saved me from filing an invalid claim.

## What You Can Sue For in Small Claims Court

Now that you know the limits, let's talk about what actually qualifies. The general rule is simple: small claims handles **civil disputes involving money or property** — not crimes, not family law, not probate.

Here's what you can typically file a claim for:

- **Unpaid debts** — Loans you made to someone (with documentation), unpaid invoices for services rendered, or bounced checks
- **Breach of contract** — Someone didn't do what they promised in a written or oral agreement
- **Property damage** — Damage to your car in a collision (if it's below the limit), a contractor who made things worse, a tenant who trashed your rental unit
- **Security deposit disputes** — Landlord refusing to return your deposit without a valid reason
- **Defective goods** — Products that don't work as advertised (though keep in mind your consumer rights as outlined here)
- **Minor personal injuries** — If you slipped on someone's property and had small medical bills
- **Landlord-tenant disputes** — This is one of the most common categories, and I've written about how to handle landlord disputes more broadly

For example, if a plumber charged you $1,800 for a job that required $400 in parts and 2 hours of labor, that might be a claim. Courts love clear evidence, so bring your estimates, invoices, and photos.

A friend of mine won a claim in Washington state for $4,200 after a moving company held his furniture hostage for an extra "fuel surcharge" that wasn't in the contract. He did everything right: he had the contract, he had texts confirming the quoted price, and he had a paper trail showing the extra charge arrived after the move was already booked. Small claims court was the only place he could realistically fight this — hiring a lawyer for a $4,200 dispute makes zero financial sense.

## What You CANNOT Sue For in Small Claims Court

This is where people get burned. I've seen more claims dismissed for this than for exceeding the monetary limit.

**You generally cannot sue for:**

- **Defamation or libel** — These require proving damages, which gets complex, and most states exclude them from small claims jurisdiction
- **Divorce, custody, or child support** — These go to family court, which has its own process
- **Probate matters** — If someone died and you're disputing the will, that's probate court territory
- **Bankruptcy claims** — Federal court only
- **Intellectual property disputes** — Copyright and trademark claims are federal matters
- **Class action lawsuits** — Small claims only handles individual claims
- **Foreclosure or title disputes involving real estate** — These are too complex for small claims
- **Criminal charges** — You can't "press charges" through small claims; that happens through the prosecutor's office

I also noticed that some states have quirky exclusions. In Massachusetts, for example, you cannot use small claims for claims arising from a motor vehicle accident if the vehicle was being used for business purposes at the time. In New York, you can't sue for the value of a bounced check if the check was given for a gambling debt (an obvious one, but it came up in court orientation).

Here's the caveat: filing fees in small claims are cheap ($30–$100), but they're not refundable. I wasted $45 in filing fees on a claim I later learned belonged in district court because it involved a real estate dispute. The clerk told me at the window — I just didn't ask the right question first. Ask before you pay.

## How to File a Claim: My Step-by-Step Playbook

When I decided to sue a contractor who'd ghosted me with $2,300 for a bathroom tile job that was never finished, I had no idea where to start. Here's exactly what I did, and I've since refined it into a system I've shared with friends.

### Step 1: Send a Demand Letter (Don't Skip This)

Before filing anything, send a formal demand letter to the person or company. This isn't just a courtesy — it often resolves the dispute without court. I used my guide on writing a cease and desist letter as a template, adapting it for a demand for payment. The key elements:

1. State the facts clearly (what happened, when, and what was promised)
2. Reference your agreement (contract, invoice, or verbal agreement details)
3. Demand a specific amount with a deadline (usually 14–30 days)
4. State that you'll file a small claims action if ignored

In my case, I sent it certified mail. The contractor signed for it, ignored it, and the receipt became valuable evidence of "notice" when I went to court.

### Step 2: Identify the Right Court

You typically file where the defendant lives or does business, not where you live. I learned this the hard way — I initially filed in my county and the court clerk rejected my forms because the contractor was based two counties over.

Use the court locator on your state's judicial branch website to find the right courthouse. If you're in a large city, check whether you need the downtown courthouse or a satellite location — they often handle different case types.

### Step 3: Prepare Your Paperwork

Most courts now offer online filing. I used the e-filing system in my state, and it was honestly pretty straightforward. Here's what you'll typically need:

Plaintiff's Claim (Form SC-100)
→ Your information (name, address, phone, email)
→ Defendant's information (full legal name, address)
→ Amount claimed (principal + any allowed costs/interest)
→ Brief statement of facts (3-5 sentences max)
→ List of evidence you intend to present

That last field is important. You'll need to state the basis of your claim. Don't write a novel — courts want a clear, concise statement. "Defendant accepted $2,300 for bathroom tile installation, completed 20% of the work, and stopped responding to communications on March 15, 2026" is better than a paragraph of narrative.

When I filed, I paid the $48 filing fee with a credit card online. The system generated a case number immediately, and I received a court date about six weeks out.

### Step 4: Serve the Defendant

This might be the most confusing part. You can't just mail the court papers to the defendant yourself in most states. You need "service of process" — someone who's not a party to the case must physically deliver the papers.

In my state, I had three options:
- Certified mail through the court ($15 and the court handles it)
- A sheriff's deputy ($35–$60 per attempt)
- A registered process server ($40–$100)

The certified mail option is cheapest and worked fine for me. The contractor's signature on the green card proves they were served. If they refuse service, you'll need alternate service (usually posting a notice or publishing in a newspaper), which adds time and cost.

My neighbor spent $120 on a process server who made three attempts to serve an evasive former roommate. The server eventually left the paperwork with a family member at the defendant's last known address, which constituted "substituted service" and was accepted by the court.

### Step 5: Prepare Your Evidence

This is where most pro-se litigants either shine or crash. The judge isn't there to help you — they're there to make a decision based on evidence. I organized my evidence in a folder with tabs:

1. **The contract** (full text, both signatures)
2. **Payment records** (bank statement showing the $2,300 transfer)
3. **Communications** (texts, emails, certified mail receipt)
4. **Photos** (the unfinished tiling, the materials he left behind)
5. **Witness statements** (my partner who saw the work quality deteriorating)

I also made two copies of everything — one for the defendant and one for the judge. The clerk at the help center told me that this single habit makes a strong impression. Judges see dozens of cases where people walk in with their phone and say "I have the texts right here" without having printed anything.

## The Day of Your Hearing

Court was anxiety-inducing, I'll admit. But the actual experience was far less intimidating than I'd built up in my head.

I showed up 30 minutes early, found my courtroom, and watched the docket for the morning. Cases were called in order, and each one took 15–30 minutes. The judge explained the process at the start of each hearing — this is standard, they know most people are nervous.

Here's what I noticed about winning cases: presentation matters almost as much as the merits. The people who won were organized, spoke clearly, and stuck to facts. The people who lost were scattered, emotional, and kept saying "that's not fair" instead of showing evidence.

During my hearing, the judge asked me to present my case first. I laid out the timeline — contract signed, initial deposit paid, partial work completed, contractor stopped responding. The contractor argued that the job was "substantially complete" and that I was being picky about "minor cosmetic issues." The judge looked at my photos, compared them to the contract description of the work, and ruled in my favor — full $2,300 plus the $48 filing fee and $15 service fee.

The contractor had 30 days to appeal. He didn't. I then needed to collect, which brings me to the next section.

## How to Collect Your Judgment (The Part Nobody Explains)

Winning your case is only half the battle. Getting the money is often harder. I need to be blunt here: small claims judgments are only as good as your ability to collect them.

If the defendant pays voluntarily — great, done. If not, you'll need to take additional steps:

1. **Demand letter for the judgment** — Send a certified letter to the defendant stating the judgment amount and demanding payment within 30 days
2. **Examine the debtor** — You can request a "debtor's examination" where the court orders them to appear and disclose their assets, bank accounts, and employment
3. **Bank levy** — If you know where they bank, you can get a court order to freeze and take funds (up to the judgment amount)
4. **Wage garnishment** — You can garnish a portion of their paycheck, but there are limits on how much (federal law caps it at 25% of disposable earnings, and states can go lower)

My case got paid as soon as I filed for the debtor's examination. The contractor suddenly "found" the money three days before his court date. In my experience, the mere threat of having to appear in court for a debtor's examination is a powerful motivator — nobody wants to spend a morning explaining their finances to a judge.

Be warned: if the defendant claims bankruptcy, your judgment becomes uncollectible. And if they're truly broke, you won't get anything. I watched a case where the plaintiff won $5,000, but the defendant had no assets, no job, and lived with family. The judge signed the judgment, wished the plaintiff luck, and there was nothing else to be done.

## When You Should Represent Yourself (and When You Shouldn't)

The entire small claims system is built for self-representation — that's the point. The lower limits and simplified procedures make lawyers impractical for most cases. But there are situations where you'd be better off hiring counsel or handling things differently.

**Represent yourself if:**
- Your claim is below your state's limit
- The facts are straightforward and well-documented
- The dispute involves a single transaction or incident
- You can articulate your case in under 5 minutes

**Consider a lawyer if:**
- The defendant has legal counsel (though in some states, they can't bring lawyers either)
- There's a complicated contract with multiple clauses at issue
- The dispute involves a business entity with resources
- The other side is threatening a countersuit
- You're dealing with a pattern of harassment or retaliation

Here's a related question I get often: what if you're *being* sued, not suing? I've written a detailed guide on what to do if you receive a lawsuit notice, but the short version is: don't ignore it. Even if you're in the right, an unanswered summons means you lose by default.

## Time Limits and Deadlines

Your claim must be filed within your state's statute of limitations — the time window during which you're allowed to sue. These vary by claim type and state:

- **Breach of written contract:** 3–6 years depending on state
- **Oral agreements:** 2–4 years
- **Property damage:** 2–6 years
- **Personal injury:** 1–3 years of the injury date

I noticed that missing the deadline is the #2 way to lose a case (after exceeding the monetary limit). The court won't remind you — you're expected to know the limit. The clock often starts ticking from the date the breach occurred, not when you discovered it.

If you're facing a debt collection lawsuit yourself, you might be surprised to learn that certain debts fall outside the statute of limitations — but that doesn't mean you should ignore the summons. I've covered how to respond to debt collectors, and prompt response is consistently the right move.

## My Honest Verdict on Small Claims Court

After going through the process myself and watching dozens of cases, here's my honest take: small claims court is an incredible tool for ordinary people, but it's not magic. It works best when you have paper trails, clear contracts, and a straightforward story. It struggles with handshake agreements, emotional disputes, and defendants who hide assets.

The system favors preparation over aggression. The judge isn't impressed by dramatic arguments — they're impressed by evidence, witnesses, and a calm presentation.

If you've got a dispute under the monetary limit, documentation of the agreement or harm, and the patience to follow the procedures, you can absolutely represent yourself and win. That's what the system was designed for.

One more thing: before filing, consider whether the relationship is worth preserving. If you're suing a friend or family member for a small amount, or a client you want to work with again, there might be better solutions — mediation, arbitration, or negotiation. Small claims can resolve the money issue but can also end the relationship permanently.

But if you're dealing with someone who's taken advantage of you and shown no good faith, small claims court is your best tool. Filing a claim costs less than one hour of a lawyer's time, and the process itself is designed for people exactly like you. Go in organized, stick to the facts, and you've got a real shot.

The contractor who ghosted me? He paid the full amount plus fees after that debtor's examination hit his calendar. It took me about 10 hours total across filing, preparation, and the hearing to turn a $2,300 loss into a full recovery. That's a better hourly rate than many lawyers bill.
