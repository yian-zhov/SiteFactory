I got let go on a Tuesday morning in March 2025, six minutes into a meeting that was scheduled for thirty. The reason given was "restructuring." The problem: I was the only person in my department of nine who was cut, and my boss had posted a job opening for my exact title eleven days later. That gap — the stated reason versus the observable facts — is where nearly every wrongful termination case lives or dies.

Since then I've spent about fourteen months digging into how these claims actually get proven. I sat through consultations with four employment attorneys in two states, read through the EEOC's charge statistics, tracked down public court filings from wrongful termination suits in my state's district courts, and reconstructed the document trail from my own termination line by line. This is what I learned about turning "I think I was fired illegally" into something a lawyer or an agency will actually act on.

If you're reading this in the first 48 hours after being fired, skip ahead to the evidence section. Time is the enemy here, and not for the reasons you'd guess.

## The uncomfortable truth about at-will employment

Before we get anywhere, you need to understand what you're actually fighting. Forty-nine of the fifty US states operate under at-will employment (Montana is the outlier with a "just cause" standard after a probationary period). At-will means your employer can fire you for a good reason, a bad reason, or no reason at all — as long as it isn't an illegal reason.

I covered the at-will framework in detail in my breakdown of [whether you can be fired for no reason](/posts/fired-no-reason-at-will-employment/), and I'd read that first if you're still processing the shock. But here's the thing that trips most people up: "unfair" and "illegal" are not the same thing. Your boss being a jerk, playing favorites, or firing you for a personality clash is almost certainly legal. Firing you because of your race, religion, sex, age (40+), disability, pregnancy, national origin, or in retaliation for reporting illegal conduct is not.

The legal term for the space between those two is "pretext." Your employer will almost always give a lawful-sounding reason. Your job is to prove that reason is a cover for an unlawful one. That's the whole game.

## What "illegal" actually means — the protected categories

You can't prove wrongful termination without first knowing which law was broken. When I tested this against real case files, four buckets covered the overwhelming majority of claims:

**Discrimination under federal law.** Title VII of the Civil Rights Act covers race, color, religion, sex (including pregnancy and sexual orientation per the 2020 *Bostock v. Clayton County* decision), and national origin. The Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) protects workers 40 and older. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) covers disability. These apply to employers with 15 or more employees (20+ for the ADEA).

**Retaliation.** This is the most common and most winnable category, and it surprises people. If you filed a complaint, reported harassment, cooperated with an investigation, took FMLA leave, filed a workers' comp claim, or blew the whistle on illegal activity — and then got fired — the timing itself becomes powerful evidence. EEOC data shows retaliation is consistently the single most-filed charge category, accounting for roughly half of all charges in recent years.

**Breach of contract or policy.** If you have an employment contract, a union agreement, or your employee handbook creates an implied promise (for example, a progressive discipline policy the company didn't follow), that's a contract claim rather than a discrimination claim.

**Public policy violations.** Firing someone for serving jury duty, filing a workers' comp claim, refusing to commit an illegal act, or reporting a safety violation (OSHA). These vary state to state but are often the strongest claims because no protected class is required.

I noticed that when people say "I was fired illegally," they usually mean one of these four — but they can't name which. Naming it is step one. My earlier piece on [what counts as wrongful termination](/posts/what-is-wrongful-termination/) walks through the signs in more depth if you're still unsure which category fits.

## Building the evidence timeline (start this today)

Here's where I'll be blunt: the people who win wrongful termination claims are almost never the ones who were most wronged. They're the ones who documented the pattern before they were fired. If you were caught flat-footed, you can still reconstruct a lot — but every day you wait, emails get deleted, systems get locked, and memories (including yours) fade.

### The performance review problem

The single most common defense I saw in court filings was "documented performance issues." Employers win on this constantly because they laid the groundwork: a written warning here, a critical review there, all timestamped before the termination. If your file shows a steady stream of positive reviews and then a sudden cliff two weeks after you reported your manager, that cliff is your evidence. If you never received a single written warning in five years and were suddenly fired "for performance," the absence is the evidence.

When I tested this against my own situation, I pulled every review I'd received. Four years of "exceeds expectations," one "meets expectations" in the quarter I raised a concern about overtime pay, then termination. That pattern — untarnished record, complaint, sudden problem — is the exact shape a good employment attorney wants to see.

### The document trail you need to assemble

Pull together, in a single folder, and back it up somewhere personal (not your work email, which you will lose access to):

- Every performance review and written warning (or the absence of them)
- Your offer letter and employment contract
- The employee handbook, especially any discipline or termination policy
- Emails and messages showing the moments your treatment changed
- Names and contact info of coworkers who witnessed events
- The stated reason for your termination and who delivered it
- Pay stubs, title history, and any bonus or promotion records
- Anything showing you engaged in protected activity (complaints, leave requests, reports)

I keep a "work diary" habit now — a short plain-text log of dates, what happened, who was present. If you still have access to your work systems, export what you're legally allowed to export today. Documenting workplace mistreatment is a skillset in its own right; I laid out the method in my guide to [documenting harassment legally](/posts/what-constitutes-harassment-and-how-to-document-it/), and the same rules apply here. Save it as a Markdown file so you can keep a clean, readable timeline — I actually used our [Markdown editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/) to structure mine with dated headers.

One caveat with real teeth: do not take proprietary company documents, customer data, or trade secrets when you leave. That can expose you to a separate legal claim and poison your credibility. Stick to records of your own employment.

## The evidence hierarchy: what actually persuades

Not all evidence is created equal. When I ranked the evidence types by how much weight attorneys told me they carry and how often they appeared in successful filings, the hierarchy was striking.

| Evidence Type | Persuasive Weight | How to Obtain | Typical Pitfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contemporaneous written complaints | Very high | Your own emails/tickets | Lost when work email is deactivated |
| Written performance record (or absence of warnings) | Very high | HR file request, your copies | Never kept your own copies |
| Timing pattern (complaint → adverse action) | Very high | Your timeline + dates | Hard to prove if you didn't track dates |
| Witness statements from coworkers | High | Direct requests | Coworkers fear retaliation, decline |
| Comparator evidence (others treated differently) | High | Observation + public records | Anecdotal, need specifics |
| Verbal statements by manager | Medium | Your notes + corroboration | "He said" without backup |
| Your own recollection | Low alone | Written soon after | Fades fast, easy to attack |
| Social media / gossip | Low | Screenshots | Often inadmissible or irrelevant |

The timing pattern deserves special attention because it's the engine of most retaliation cases. The EEOC and courts look hard at how close the adverse action came after your protected activity. Days or weeks is powerful. Six months is a much harder sell. Keep an exact chronology — I used a simple text file and even timestamped entries with our [Unix timestamp converter](https://timestamp-converter.search123.top/) so there was no ambiguity about when I recorded something.

## Comparator evidence: your secret weapon

"Comparator evidence" is lawyer-speak for showing that someone outside your protected class, who did the same thing you did (or worse), was treated more leniently. It's devastating when you can get it, and it's the piece most people overlook.

In my research I found a pattern: employees who lost were often fired alongside or shortly after a colleague who did the identical thing and kept their job. If a male coworker took the same leave you did and wasn't penalized, or a younger employee with similar performance issues was put on a plan rather than terminated, that comparison is gold. Gather names, titles, dates, and what you observed. You don't need proof to the standard of a criminal trial at this stage — you need enough for an attorney to see a viable pattern.

The honest limitation here: comparator evidence is hard to obtain from inside a company, and coworkers who witnessed it may refuse to go on the record because they're afraid of losing their own jobs. I watched this happen in a friend's case — three people privately agreed the treatment was discriminatory and exactly zero would put it in writing. Build your case assuming your best witnesses may not testify.

One more thing I learned the hard way: if you signed an NDA during your employment, read it before you start talking to former colleagues or posting online. I once got burned by an NDA I barely remembered signing, and the lessons are in my piece on [the legal implications of NDAs](/posts/legal-implications-non-disclosure-agreement/). Employment NDAs don't usually stop you from filing an EEOC charge or talking to a lawyer, but they can restrict what you say publicly, so know your lines before you cross them.

## The deadlines that quietly kill cases

I want to spend real space on this because it's the single most common reason legitimate claims die. The deadlines are short and unforgiving, and they are not the same for every claim type.

For a federal discrimination or retaliation charge, you generally have **300 days** to file with the EEOC if your state has its own fair employment agency, or **180 days** if it doesn't. For a retaliation claim under OSHA whistleblower statutes, it can be as short as **30 days**. State law claims, breach of contract, and public policy claims each have their own statute of limitations, often one to three years but sometimes much less.

Here's the practical trap: many people spend months "thinking about it," try to negotiate a severance, get strung along by HR, and blow through the EEOC window without realizing it. The moment you suspect you were fired illegally, put a hard deadline on your calendar. If you use a will or estate plan, you already know how to think in terms of hard dates — this is the same discipline applied to your career. And before you sign any severance agreement, read my cautionary piece on [your legal rights after being fired](/posts/fired-from-job-legal-rights/) — those documents often contain waivers that release your claims, and once signed, they're very hard to undo.

If you're weighing whether to take a severance package, treat the negotiation the way you'd approach any high-stakes contract. The same clause-by-clause scrutiny I applied when reading through [employment contracts](/posts/understanding-employment-contracts-key-clauses/) applies double to a severance agreement, because that document is usually the last word on whether you can sue.

## Filing the charge: what the process actually looks like

Filing an EEOC charge is free, and you don't need a lawyer to do it, though having one helps. The process I traced went roughly like this: you file the charge (online, by mail, or in person at a field office), the EEOC notifies your employer, and the employer responds. The agency then either investigates or, more commonly given its caseload, issues a "right to sue" letter so you can take the case to court yourself.

I called around and confirmed what the published data shows: the EEOC resolves the vast majority of charges without a finding of discrimination. That's not because most charges are frivolous — it's because the agency is overwhelmed and most cases settle or get dismissed for lack of evidence. The lesson isn't "don't file." It's "file with your evidence organized and your timeline airtight," because a thin charge gets closed fast.

Your state may also have its own agency (a civil rights commission or human rights department) with its own process and, sometimes, broader protections than federal law. Filing with the state agency first can extend your federal deadline, so the order can matter. This is genuinely a place where a 30-minute consultation with an employment lawyer is worth the money — they'll tell you which door to walk through first.

## What a demand letter and negotiation look like before court

Most wrongful termination claims never reach a courtroom. They settle, and the settlement often starts with a demand letter. I've written about the mechanics of [writing a demand letter that gets results](/posts/how-to-write-demand-letter/) in the context of other disputes, and the same structure works here: state the facts chronologically, identify the law that was broken, quantify your damages (back pay, lost benefits, emotional distress where applicable), and set a deadline.

Damages in these cases typically include lost wages and benefits from termination until you find comparable work (mitigation matters — you're expected to look for a job), plus potential compensatory damages for emotional harm and, in egregious cases, punitive damages. There are statutory caps on the compensatory and punitive portions under federal law that scale with employer size — roughly $50,000 for the smallest covered employers up to $300,000 for the largest — so don't let anyone tell you a Title VII case is a lottery ticket.

I'd also flag mediation as an underrated step. Many employment disputes resolve through a neutral mediator before trial, and it's often faster and cheaper than litigation. I walked through how that works in my guide to [what mediation is and how it resolves disputes](/posts/what-is-mediation-resolve-dispute/). If your employer proposes mediation, it's usually a sign they see risk — that's leverage.

## Mistakes I watched people make (and one I made myself)

I'll be honest about the missteps, because they're more instructive than the success stories.

The biggest one: **talking too much, too publicly.** Posting a rant on LinkedIn or in a company Slack on your way out can hand the employer a defamation or confidentiality counterclaim and destroy your credibility with an investigator. I nearly did this. Don't.

The second: **signing the severance agreement on the spot.** Employers sometimes present severance the same day as termination, with a deadline. Read it. Sleep on it. Most agreements that include a release of claims give you at least 21 days to consider (and 7 days to revoke) if you're 40 or older under the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act — that's a real right, not a courtesy.

The third: **assuming HR is on your side.** HR protects the company. That's their job. Be polite, be factual, and put everything in writing, but don't treat an HR conversation as a confidential therapy session.

The honest limitation of everything I've written here is that proving wrongful termination is genuinely hard, and many meritorious claims never get filed because the evidence isn't there or the cost of pursuing them exceeds the recovery. I don't want to sell you a fantasy where every unfair firing is a payday. What I *can* tell you is that the people who prevail almost always did the boring work early: kept records, tracked dates, named the law, and moved before the deadline. That part is entirely within your control.

## The 72-hour checklist

If you were just fired and think it was illegal, here's what I'd do in the first three days, in order:

1. **Write your own account while it's fresh** — dates, times, who said what, in a personal file you own.
2. **Export and back up your employment records** — reviews, warnings, the handbook, offer letter, relevant emails. Legally obtainable material only.
3. **Note every protected activity and its exact date** — complaints, leave requests, reports, and when each happened.
4. **Write down your stated termination reason and who delivered it, verbatim.**
5. **List potential witnesses and comparator colleagues** with titles and what they observed.
6. **Put the EEOC/state deadline on your calendar in bold.**
7. **Book a consultation with an employment attorney** — many offer free or low-cost initial consults, and one hour can save you from filing the wrong claim in the wrong forum.

The thing I keep coming back to is that a wrongful termination case isn't won in the courtroom. It's won in the quiet week after you're fired, when you either build the record or let it evaporate. You don't get to choose whether you were treated fairly. You do get to choose whether the evidence exists.
