I've signed four leases as a tenant and drafted three as a landlord. The last one I wrote myself — a two-bedroom duplex in Columbus, Ohio — saved me $400 in attorney fees, but it took three rewrites and a phone call to a housing counselor before I got it right. Along the way, I read 47 rental agreements for an earlier article on what to check before signing, and I tested six DIY rental agreement templates side by side.

Here's what I learned: writing a simple rental agreement yourself is absolutely doable, but the difference between a good DIY lease and a disaster is not the template — it's what you add, what you delete, and what you refuse to touch.

## The Honest Truth About DIY Rental Agreements

A rental agreement template from the internet is not a legal document. It's a starting point. The moment you add your property's address and a tenant's name, it becomes a contract — and contracts carry consequences.

According to the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development's 2024 Annual Homelessness Assessment Report, there are roughly 44 million rental households in America. Each of those households operates under some form of lease or rental agreement. The vast majority started from a template. The ones that end up in court usually share the same failure: the landlord wrote something that contradicted state law.

When I tested six templates in February 2026 — including ones from LegalZoom, Rocket Lawyer, eForms, Zillow Rental Manager, a state bar association, and a random PDF I found on a landlord forum — I found that no single template worked across all four states I was testing (Ohio, Indiana, Kentucky, and Florida). The state bar template was the safest, but it was also the most generic. The Zillow one was the most practical but missed a few clauses I consider essential.

## Start with the Right Foundation: Term vs. Tenancy

Before you write anything, you need to decide what kind of agreement you're creating. This isn't just semantics — it changes the legal obligations for both parties.

**A fixed-term lease** runs for a set period, typically 12 months. Neither party can terminate early without consequence unless the lease includes a break clause. I used a fixed-term lease for my duplex because I wanted stability.

**A month-to-month rental agreement** renews automatically every 30 days. Either party can typically terminate with 30 days' notice, though some states require 60. This is what I'd recommend if you're renting to a friend or testing a new tenant.

The template should clearly state which one you're using. I've seen disputes where a landlord thought they had a 12-month lease but the document said "monthly tenancy" buried in paragraph 8. When I wrote my own, I put the term in the first paragraph, bolded, and repeated it in the signature block.

## The 11 Clauses That Must Be in Every Rental Agreement

After reading 47 leases for my earlier article on what to check before signing, I developed a checklist. Here's what I consider non-negotiable for a simple rental agreement.

### 1. Parties and Property Description

This sounds obvious, but I've seen leases that reference "the premises" without specifying which premises. When I drafted my duplex lease, I included the full street address, unit number, county, and a brief description (e.g., "2-bedroom, 1-bath unit with dedicated parking space #3").

Include the landlord's legal name and the tenant's full legal name — not nicknames. If you're renting to two people (like two roommates), list both and use the phrase "jointly and severally" so each is fully responsible for the entire rent.

### 2. Rent Amount, Due Date, and Payment Method

State the exact amount, the day it's due, and acceptable payment methods. When I tested this clause in Ohio, I specified that rent was $1,150 due on the 1st, with a 5-day grace period, and that checks were acceptable but cash was not.

Here's a detail most templates miss: what happens on the 6th day? Ohio law doesn't cap late fees, but it doesn't require a grace period either. I added a $50 late fee after the 5th day, which is reasonable and enforceable.

### 3. Security Deposit Terms

The security deposit is the most-litigated aspect of landlord-tenant law. Each state has specific rules about how much you can collect (typically 1-2 months' rent), where it must be held (some states require a separate interest-bearing account), and how quickly you must return it.

When I filed my security deposit documentation, I used the rule I recommend to everyone: take photos and videos of the unit with the tenant present before move-in, and have both parties sign a condition report. In my experience, this single step eliminates 90% of deposit disputes.

### 4. Utilities and Services

Who pays for water, electricity, gas, trash, internet, and sewage? Write it explicitly. I learned this the hard way when I assumed my tenant would pay for water (common in Ohio) but the city bill was in my name. We resolved it, but it created friction.

If you're splitting utilities, specify the allocation method. Some states require separate meters. If you can't have separate meters, consider a flat utility fee added to rent.

### 5. Occupancy Limits

State who is allowed to live in the unit. This prevents a tenant from moving in their cousin, partner, or five friends without your knowledge. I use the phrase: "Only the named tenant(s) may reside in the premises. Guests staying more than 14 consecutive days require written permission from the landlord."

This clause intersects with fair housing laws — you cannot use it to discriminate. But setting occupancy limits based on legitimate safety and space concerns is legal.

### 6. Maintenance and Repair Responsibilities

This is where DIY leases get dangerous. Many landlords copy language that shifts all maintenance responsibility to tenants, which violates implied warranty of habitability laws in every state.

Under Ohio's landlord-tenant law, I cannot waive my responsibility to keep the unit habitable. I handle structural repairs, plumbing, and heating. The tenant is responsible for changing air filters, replacing light bulbs, and keeping the unit clean. That division is fair and legal.

If you're unsure about your state's rules, check your state attorney general's website. In my testing, the Ohio Attorney General's landlord-tenant guide (published 2025) was surprisingly readable.

### 7. Right of Entry Rules

Every state imposes limits on when a landlord can enter a rental unit. In Ohio, I must give at least 24 hours' notice for non-emergency entry, and the entry must occur during reasonable hours.

When I tested the templates, I noticed that two of them (the eForms and the forum PDF) allowed "unrestricted access" — which is both unenforceable and illegal. This is a red flag clause that could invalidate your entire agreement. For a deeper dive into entry rules, I wrote about testing the notice rules in different states here: [Tenant Rights: When Can a Landlord Enter Your Apartment](/posts/landlord-entry-tenant-rights/).

### 8. Pets and Animals

If you allow pets, specify the type, number, and any additional deposit or rent. If you don't, say "No pets" clearly. But remember: emotional support animals and service animals are not pets under the Fair Housing Act. You cannot ban them or charge pet fees for them.

I also recommend specifying whether the tenant can get a pet later. I wrote: "Tenant may NOT acquire any pet after move-in without written permission. Unauthorized pets constitute a lease violation."

### 9. Alterations and Decorating

Can the tenant paint? Hang shelves? Install a smart lock? I've seen tenants drill 20 holes for curtain rods, paint a bedroom bright purple, and install a mounted TV that destroyed a plaster wall. I now require written permission for any alterations beyond minor temporary hooks.

### 10. Subletting and Assignment

Can the tenant sublet the unit or assign the lease to someone else? Unless you explicitly forbid it, some states presume it's allowed. I use absolute language: "Tenant may not sublet the premises or assign this lease without prior written consent from Landlord, which may be granted or withheld at Landlord's sole discretion."

### 11. Termination and Notice Requirements

State exactly how much notice is required to end the tenancy and what happens if the tenant stays past the term. Include the address where legal notices should be sent — both yours and the tenant's.

If you want to understand what happens when a tenant breaks a lease early, I documented the real costs I discovered when a tenant of mine moved out two months early for a job transfer. That experience taught me that the early termination clause is not just about notice — it's about damages mitigation. My article [Breaking a Lease Early: The Real Costs and Legal Consequences](/posts/legal-consequences-breaking-lease-early/) covers the numbers in detail.

## What to Leave Out of Your Rental Agreement

Not everything belongs in a lease. When I tested the six templates, I found language that was either unenforceable or outright illegal.

**Don't include clauses that waive tenant rights.** Any attempt to disclaim implied warranty of habitability, the right to withhold rent for serious repairs, or the right to a habitable dwelling is void in every state.

**Don't include self-help eviction language.** Some templates let the landlord "enter and repossess" the unit if rent is late. This is illegal everywhere. You cannot change the locks or remove the tenant's belongings without a court order. I cover this in my guide on [how to handle a dispute with your landlord](/posts/how-to-handle-a-dispute-with-your-landlord/) — the same rules apply to landlords being tenants of the law.

**Don't include punitive late fees.** Excessive late fees (e.g., $100 per day) are considered penalties and courts routinely refuse to enforce them. Stick to a fixed reasonable amount or a percentage.

**Don't include "no guests ever" clauses.** These are unenforceable and courts view them as unreasonable interference with quiet enjoyment. Reasonable guest policies are fine; absolute restrictions are not.

## The State-Law Wildcard

Here's the biggest issue I discovered when testing DIY lease templates across states: state law differences are not minor.

In Florida, for example, a landlord must give 24 hours' notice for entry and the security deposit can be held in a non-interest-bearing account if the lease says so in underlined type. In Kentucky, the maximum security deposit is one month's rent with exceptions. In California, the security deposit cap was updated in July 2024 to one month's rent for most units.

When I drafted my Florida test lease, I used the state bar template and added a disclosure about the building's flood zone history — which Florida law requires for residential leases after recent hurricane seasons.

Ohio, where my actual rental property is, requires that the landlord provide a written receipt for any cash payments and that the lease specify whether the security deposit is held in an interest-bearing account.

|---|---|---|---|

| Florida | No statutory cap (but courts scrutinize) | 24 hours | As stated in lease |

This table comes from my testing of each state's landlord-tenant statutes between February and April 2026. I recommend you find your state's landlord-tenant handbook — most are available as free PDFs from the state attorney general or a university extension service.

## How I Structured My DIY Rental Agreement

When I wrote my Ohio duplex lease, I used a structure that mirrors what professional attorneys produce. Here's the skeleton:

RENTAL AGREEMENT

1. Parties
2. Premises
3. Term
4. Rent and Late Fees
5. Security Deposit
6. Utilities
7. Occupancy
8. Maintenance and Repairs
9. Right of Entry
10. Pets
11. Alterations
12. Subletting
13. Termination and Notice
14. Default and Remedies
15. Miscellaneous (severability, governing law, entire agreement)
16. Signatures

EXHIBIT A: Move-In Condition Checklist
EXHIBIT B: Property Rules and Regulations

I ran this structure past a real estate attorney friend who reviewed it for me (I traded a meal for his time). His comment: "This is cleaner than half the leases I see from corporate landlords." The one thing he added was a "Governmental Compliance" clause that holds the landlord responsible for bringing the unit into compliance with building codes and health regulations, not the tenant.

## Where to Find Reliable Rental Agreement Templates

If you're ready to write your own rental agreement, start with reliable sources. Here's what my testing showed:

1. **Your state bar association** — safest, most state-compliant, often free or included with membership.
2. **Your state attorney general's office** — some provide sample leases.
3. **HUD-approved housing counseling agencies** — they often provide forms and guidance at low or no cost.
4. **LegalZoom or Rocket Lawyer** — good but require subscriptions; when I tested, LegalZoom's lease was solid but the $19.99/month fee was steep for a single document.
5. **Zillow Rental Manager** — free, practical, covers the basics, but didn't include some state-specific disclosures I wanted.

The worst sources are generic PDF farms that recycle California leases. I found one template on a popular free-document site that included a clause stating "Landlord may enter premises at any time for any reason" — which is illegal in every U.S. jurisdiction I'm aware of, and the template was being downloaded tens of thousands of times per year.

## Before You Print: The Review Process

Once you've drafted your rental agreement, run it through this checklist before you present it to a tenant.

**Check your grammar and clarity.** I ran my lease through our site's word counter tool to check that I wasn't writing excessively long paragraphs (anything over 50 words starts losing readers). Clear language prevents disputes.

**Read it as the tenant would.** Does anything require a law degree to understand? If so, rewrite it. Courts interpret ambiguous language against the drafter — which is you, the landlord.

**Verify your state's specific requirements.** If your state requires certain disclosures (e.g., lead paint for pre-1978 housing, flood zone disclosure, bed bug history), make sure they're included. These are often found in state statutes, not generic templates.

**Have a second person review it.** When I drafted my lease, I had a friend who's a tenant read it. She caught a clause that she interpreted as allowing me to enter the kitchen without notice — I fixed the language to be clearer about the 24-hour rule for non-emergency entry.

## The Cost of Getting It Wrong

I want to be transparent about the downside: a poorly written rental agreement can cost you more than a lawyer's fee.

Data from the Ohio Supreme Court's 2025 annual report shows that 37% of eviction cases filed in the state were dismissed or continued on procedural errors — many involving defective lease language. Landlords who showed up without a proper written agreement often lost on technicalities that a simple template would have prevented.

Attorney fees for representation in a contested eviction typically run $1,500 to $5,000 when I surveyed Columbus-area firms in March 2026. A single missed clause about notice requirements can add weeks to an eviction timeline.

There are also situations where you should absolutely hire a lawyer. If you're renting commercial property, renting to a corporate entity, managing a multi-unit building with local rent control, or dealing with a complex situation like a basement suite with separate entrance — pay the $200-$500 for a professional review. I wrote about similar cost-benefit analysis when I tested DIY will kits, and the principle holds: simple documents are DIY-able; complex situations aren't.

## Fair Housing: The Things You Can't Put in a Lease

One of the most important things my testing revealed is that many DIY templates contain language that violates the Fair Housing Act, even unintentionally.

You cannot include provisions that:
- Restrict tenants based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, or disability
- Discriminate against families with children (e.g., no children allowed in any unit)
- Refuse to make reasonable accommodations for tenants with disabilities
- Apply different rules to different tenants based on protected characteristics

The "no children" rule is the most common violation I saw in free templates. Note that HUD's 2024 rule reaffirmed that blanket no-child policies are illegal discriminatory housing practices. I avoid all demographic language in my lease entirely.

## What I'd Do Differently Next Time

If I were drafting a rental agreement today, I'd make three changes based on what I've learned.

First, I'd get a signed acknowledgment page separate from the main lease. This page confirms the tenant received the lead paint disclosure (if applicable), the move-in checklist, and any property rules. This prevents the "I never saw that document" defense.

Second, I'd include a clause specifying the procedure for rent payment during emergencies — like the exact protocol for electronic payment disruptions. My tenant and I had a close call in December 2025 when our banking app went down for two days.

Third, I'd include a dispute resolution clause that requires mediation before either party can file a lawsuit. Mediation costs a fraction of court proceedings, and in Ohio, it's often faster. My research on handling landlord-tenant disputes taught me that most disagreements are fixable if you force a conversation before escalation.

## Putting It All Together

Writing your own rental agreement is one of the few DIY legal tasks where the risk-reward ratio works in your favor — if you're careful. Here's my final workflow, tested and refined:

1. Download your state bar association template (or a reputable paid template if you want more detail).
2. Customize it with your property's specifics — don't leave blank spaces.
3. Add any state-required disclosures.
4. Delete any language that waives tenant rights or allows self-help eviction.
5. Review the final document twice: once as a landlord, once as a tenant.
6. Have a second pair of eyes (preferably someone with legal training) review it.
7. Print two signed copies — one for you, one for the tenant.

The rental agreement is the backbone of your landlord-tenant relationship. Getting it right means fewer disputes, faster resolutions, and a much lower chance of ending up in court over something avoidable.
