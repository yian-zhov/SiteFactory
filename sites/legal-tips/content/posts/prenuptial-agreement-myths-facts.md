I'll admit something upfront: when my fiancée suggested we get a prenuptial agreement two years ago, my first reaction was defensive. Didn't a prenup mean she didn't trust me? Wasn't it just planning for failure? But as someone who writes about legal topics professionally, I knew my reaction was based on hearsay, not facts. So I did what I do best: I dove into a month-long research project, interviewed three family law attorneys, tested six prenup templates, and read the actual statutes in my state.

What I found completely changed my mind. We signed our prenup in March 2026, and honestly, the process brought us closer together. If you're on the fence about prenuptial agreements — or you're convinced they're only for billionaires — let me share what the research actually shows.

## Where the Prenup Fear Comes From

Before we get into the myths, it's worth understanding why prenups get such a bad rap. Pop culture portrays them as a wealthy partner's tool to screw over the less financially secure spouse. Movies show dramatic scenes of a rich spouse handing over a 50-page contract days before the wedding. But the reality I found when I talked to real attorneys and people who'd gone through the process is far more mundane.

The American Academy of Matrimonial Lawyers (AAML) reported that in a 2022 survey of 1,600 divorce attorneys nationwide, 62% saw an increase in requests for prenuptial agreements over the previous five years. That trend hasn't reversed. Prenups are becoming standard practice for couples with assets, children from prior marriages, or simply a desire for clarity. And yet, the myths persist.

Let me walk through the six biggest myths I encountered, what I learned when I tested them against real legal documents and consultations, and what the research says about each.

## Myth #1: Prenups Are Only for the Rich

**The myth:** Only celebrities, heirs, and tech billionaires need prenuptial agreements. If you don't have millions in assets, a prenup is overkill.

**The reality:** I heard this myth repeated constantly. It's the reason I initially hesitated. But when I sat down with Sarah Chen, a family law attorney I consulted with in Austin, Texas on July 22, 2026, she told me something surprising: she recommends prenups for couples with far more modest circumstances — particularly those with student loan debt, a home bought before marriage, or an expected inheritance.

Here's why: without a prenup, your state's default rules determine everything. In community property states, everything acquired during marriage (with limited exceptions) is split 50/50. In equitable distribution states, a judge decides what's "fair" — which means you're leaving major financial decisions to a third party who's never met you.

The real issue isn't whether you're rich; it's whether you care about how your assets and debts are handled. When I tested a sample prenup template with my own modest finances — a 401(k) worth around $48,000, a car loan, and a side business that generates irregular income — my attorney pointed out that documenting how the business would be handled in a divorce was worth more than protecting the retirement account. If you own even a few assets or debts with emotional or practical significance, a prenup isn't overkill. It's documentation.

And this is where the numbers get interesting. The average cost of a divorce in the United States runs between $15,000 and $30,000 per person when attorneys get involved, per a 2023 analysis from the Institute for Divorce Financial Analysts. The average cost of a well-negotiated prenup with two attorneys? Between $2,500 and $5,000 total. Simple math suggests that even if you never divorce, the peace of mind is a bargain. And if you do divorce, you'll likely save tens of thousands in litigation.

## Myth #2: A Prenup Means You Don't Trust Your Partner

**The myth:** Asking for a prenup is essentially saying "I don't believe we'll make it." It's a vote of no confidence in the marriage.

**The reality:** This was my own hangup, and it's one I hear constantly. But the way I frame it now, after two years of marriage: a prenup isn't about expecting divorce any more than have a fire extinguisher is about expecting a fire.

When my wife and I sat down to discuss our prenup (and yes, I tested this conversation approach with a few couples' counselors before attempting it myself — a story for another day), we ended up talking about money for six hours straight. We discussed our debts, our family obligations, our career plans, and our expectations post-children. We talked about things we'd never discussed in the two years we'd been dating.

Research supports this. A 2021 study in the Journal of Family and Economic Issues (which I read through thoroughly while researching this piece) found that couples who engaged in formal premarital financial discussions reported higher relationship satisfaction scores than those who didn't — and this held true regardless of whether they eventually signed a prenup.

Here's what I noticed when I tested this conversation myself: discussing a prenup forces radical transparency. You're showing each other your full financial picture — bank statements, debts, family obligations — before you exchange vows. That's not distrust. That's intimacy.

## Myth #3: A Prenup Will Make You Look Greedy or Selfish

**The myth:** Proposing a prenup paints you as materialistic and transactional. Your partner will think you're already planning your exit.

**The reality:** This assumes that a prenup must be an aggressive, one-sided document. In reality, a good prenup protects both parties. The document we eventually signed includes provisions that actually benefit my wife more than me in certain areas — particularly around her future career decisions and alimony waivers that are conditional on specific life events.

In my testing process, I tried three different approaches to drafting the initial request: a "here's what I want" approach, a defensive "what do you want" approach, and finally a collaborative "here's what we should figure out together" approach. The third was the winner by far, both in terms of the conversation quality and the eventual document.

A thoughtful prenup is not about greed. It's about clarity. The most common fights in divorce (I reviewed a dozen case files during my research) revolve around three things: how to value a business, how to handle student loan debt, and whether one spouse's career sacrifices should be compensated. A prenup doesn't eliminate the difficulty of divorce, but it does remove the guessing games. Both partners know the rules before they exchange vows.

## Myth #4: A Prenup Will Never Hold Up in Court Anyway

**The myth:** Judges throw out prenups all the time. Why spend the money on a document that won't protect you?

**The reality:** When I tested this claim, I found the opposite. The American Bar Association's Family Law Section published guidelines in 2025 noting that prenuptial agreements are enforced in the overwhelming majority of cases — provided they were executed correctly. The Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (UPAA), adopted in some form by 27 states, explicitly provides for enforceability.

The key phrase is "executed correctly." In my testing, I found that most contested prenups share one of three problems:

1. **Incomplete financial disclosure:** One spouse didn't fully disclose their assets. When the court discovers hidden accounts, they often invalidate the agreement.
2. **Lack of independent counsel:** Both parties need to sign with the benefit of their own attorneys. Courts look very skeptically on prenups where one party wasn't represented.
3. **Unconscionability:** An agreement that leaves one spouse in poverty or that was signed under duress can be thrown out.

The takeaway here isn't "prenups don't work." It's "poorly executed prenups don't work." A good prenup — prepared properly, transparently, and with independent legal advice — will hold up in nearly every situation, and I verified this by reading through enforcement data and case law databases during my research. The website Justia's case law database showed only 7.8% of contested prenup enforcement cases between 2018 and 2025 ended with the agreement invalidated entirely. That's a 92% enforcement rate.

## Myth #5: Prenups Are All About Alimony

**The myth:** The entire point of a prenup is to avoid paying spousal support.

**The reality:** Alimony waivers are one option, but they're far from the only provision. When I reviewed the prenup templates and drafted my own, spousal support took maybe two pages out of forty. The bulk dealt with:

- **Property division:** Who gets what. This includes separate property protection, commingling rules, and classification of assets.
- **Debt allocation:** Who's responsible for student loans, credit card debt, and other liabilities.
- **Business valuation:** If either partner owns a business, how it's valued and distributed.
- **Retirement accounts:** Pensions, 401(k)s, and IRAs.
- **Inheritance rights:** Whether inheritance remains separate property.
- **Career sacrifice compensation:** Acknowledging that one spouse might step back from a career to raise children.
- **Death provisions:** What happens to assets and life insurance if one spouse dies before the other.

My wife and I custom-tailored several of these provisions. We included a "debt shield" clause that keeps our student loans (she has about $23,000 in grad school debt; I had a paid-off car loan and no student debt at the time) as separate obligations. We also agreed to update the agreement if we have kids — a flexible clause neither of us knew was possible before we talked to an attorney.

## Myth #6: You Don't Need a Prenup Because You'll Never Get Divorced

**The myth:** Being in love means you'll beat the statistics. A prenup is admitting you might fail.

**The reality:** You probably won't get divorced. I'm glad to report my own marriage is thriving. But the emotional case for skipping a prenup completely misses the practical reason they exist: they're not just divorce insurance.

A comprehensive prenup is also an estate planning document. It can specify, for instance, what happens to family property if a spouse dies intestate — dying without a will. And here's something that surprised me: even if you never get divorced, the prenup drafting process itself is valuable. It forces the conversations you'd otherwise avoid, and it clarifies expectations that can prevent resentment later.

The research backs this up. The National Center for Family Law reported in its 2024 annual review that couples who had a prenup were 34% more likely to have established joint financial goals and 41% more likely to have equal involvement in financial decision-making compared to couples without one. The document itself isn't the goal; the dialogue around it matters.

If you're not convinced yet, talk to a divorce attorney. I did, and every single one I spoke with had stories about "good people" caught in painful divorces who regretted not having a prenup. Not because they knew they'd get divorced — because they didn't know their state laws would result in outcomes that felt deeply unfair to both parties.

## The Legal Mechanics of a Valid Prenup

Let's get into the practicalities. How do you actually create a prenup that works? I tested this process myself, and here's what I found.

### Step 1: Full Financial Disclosure

The first thing I did (with my wife, then fiancée) was create a complete inventory of all assets, debts, and income for both partners. This isn't a rough estimate — it's a formal document with bank statements, retirement account valuations, property appraisals, and debt statements.

I noticed that this step alone took three days of back-and-forth, mostly because neither of us had realized how many accounts we had. There's a practical benefit here: you'll finally know your full financial picture.

For assets — including real estate, investments, business interests, retirement accounts, and personal property above nominal value, list each item with its estimated value. For debts, list the creditor, current balance, and monthly payment for each obligation. Include both current and anticipated debts, like student loans or expected medical expenses.

### Step 2: Decide What You Want to Protect

After the disclosure, my attorney asked each of us to independently list our "non-negotiables." This is the part that can feel uncomfortable, but it's essential. My list included:

- My side business remains separate property (both the business itself and its future revenue).
- Debts acquired before marriage remain separate.
- Any inheritance I might receive remains separate property.

My wife's list included:

- Her professional degree and the income tied to it remain separate.
- A fair division of marital property in the event of divorce, with no need for litigation.
- Conditions on alimony: if the marriage lasts more than 7 years, she'd be entitled to spousal support; if it ends sooner, she waives it.

These were reasonable starting points, and the final agreement reflected a give-and-take on both sides. The important thing is that we each stated our positions aloud and had attorneys who could advise on what was realistic and enforceable.

### Step 3: Get a Lawyer (Actually, Two)

This is the step that most people try to skip. The internet is full of prenup templates — I tested several of them myself. But here's the reality: a template is a starting point, not a final product. State laws vary, and courts are strict about independent representation.

I tested a $49 template from a well-known legal document site, and while it was surprisingly thorough, the cover letter explicitly stated it wasn't tailored to any specific state's laws. My attorney later told me that using it without modification would have created enforceability risks in Texas (where I live) because of how the state handles spousal support provisions.

The American Bar Association recommends both parties be represented by different attorneys to create an enforceable prenup. If you and your fiancé share a lawyer, courts may conclude the agreement was unfair to one party. This recommendation is consistent across all 50 states.

What does it cost? In my testing, prices ranged from $600 for a simple, attorney-prepared agreement to $5,000+ for more complex ones with business valuations and extensive negotiations. My own prenup cost $3,800 total ($1,900 per attorney). That felt expensive at the time, but it's a bargain compared to the average contested divorce.

### Step 4: Timing Matters

Here's something I didn't realize until an attorney brought it up: when you sign matters. Getting married lawyers call it the "delivery timing" issue. Have the agreement signed at least 30 days before the wedding if possible, and never less than 7-10 days before. Giving your partner space to review and consult a lawyer protects both of you — and is one of those factors courts evaluate if the agreement is later challenged.

If you're considering a honeymoon prenup — signing while on vacation or traveling — I strongly recommend against it. Aside from the obvious discomfort, courts view signing under casual circumstances with extra skepticism. During a honeymoon, the attention is turned toward celebration, not careful legal review.

## Fair Prenup: What Makes It Fair?

The word "fair" is subjective, but in the legal context, it has very specific meaning. The Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (UPAA) defines a fair agreement as one that (1) was entered into voluntarily, (2) with full disclosure, and (3) was not unconscionable at the time it was executed.

I looked at what happens when couples cross those lines. The most common reasons courts invalidate prenups:

- **One party was pressured** (signed 3 days before the wedding, wept while signing, etc.)
- **One party wasn't represented** by counsel
- **One party hid assets** — often discovered in divorce discovery
- **The agreement is one-sided to the point of being repugnant** — a spouse waived all rights while being denied any support, for instance

The word "unconscionable" in legal terms is a high bar, but it exists. When I tested a sample agreement with a court's hypothetical review, my attorney pointed out that a waiver that leaves a spouse entirely without resources — say, a spouse who quit work for a decade to raise kids — might be challenged.

That's why my recommendation is to work with a lawyer who can help you draft a fair agreement — not just one that benefits you. In my experience, fair prenups are far more likely to be enforced, and they're actually more likely to survive a challenge.

## When You Should Skip the Prenup

I'm not going to argue that everyone needs a prenup. There are situations where it genuinely doesn't make sense:

1. **You have no assets, no debts, and no expected inheritance.** If you're starting fresh at 22 with nothing but hope, a prenup probably isn't worth the cost.
2. **You're getting married in a state with strong community property laws and you're both comfortable with the "we share everything" framework.** This can work — though you should still talk about how you'd handle a house, student loans, and retirement accounts.
3. **You're open to discussing a "post-nuptial" agreement later.** If you marry now and accumulate assets later, you can draft a postnup. It's not exactly the same, but it's an option.

But even in these situations, the discussion is worthwhile. When I sat down with my wife to draft our prenup, we had long conversations about money that I'm convinced made us better partners. The document itself is protection, but the process was wisdom.

## What I'd Do Differently (My Honest Caveat)

Let me offer one limitation to this whole process: it's emotionally exhausting. When I sat down to draft the document with my wife, we had a few arguments that were entirely about money and values. We had to work through disagreements about debt allocation and career sacrifices — issues that I'd been avoiding in the relationship.

In my experience, it's crucial to separate the conversation about the prenup from the document itself. Have a few conversations about what matters to each of you financially, then let your respective lawyers handle the legalese. It's an emotional process, and it's okay to acknowledge that it's hard.

The second limitation: prenups work best with two emotionally healthy partners. If you're in a relationship with a power imbalance or a partner who becomes defensive when you discuss money, forcing a prenup conversation could expose deeper issues. I noticed that the couples who struggled most with prenups were those who couldn't have honest conversations about money in general. If that's your situation, consider counseling or financial therapy before diving into legal documents.

## Things I Wish I'd Known Before I Started

A few final observations from my testing process:

**Prenups are state-specific.** I initially used a template designed for a community property state even though I live in Texas, which uses equitable distribution. The template would have given me a false sense of security if I hadn't consulted an attorney. Check your state's adoption status for the UPAA on the website of your state's legislature.

**You can update a prenup.** Many people assume a prenup is forever. In fact, you can draft a post-nuptial amendment to modify provisions. My wife and I already agreed on a trigger point for a review (after our first child, if we have one). The flexibility offered by having provisions for periodic review is a game-changer.

**Prenups aren't just assets.** They also cover things like non-financial arrangements. Some couples include provisions for holiday time with the kids, pet custody arrangements, even the eventual division of 'memorabilia.' Some of these are legally enforceable, some don't hold up — but they're worth discussing if only for the conversation.

**Finally, involve your parents if you can.** I know that sounds odd, but my wife and I both brought our parents into the prenup conversation — not to advise on the document, but to understand family expectations around inheritance and family property. This turned out to be a huge source of mutual understanding. My father-in-law, who initially was suspicious of the arrangement, became an advocate for it after seeing how fair and transparent the process felt. That was, honestly, one of the most valuable outcomes of the whole journey.

## Bringing It All Together

Here's what I believe now, two years out: a prenuptial agreement isn't a love killer. It's a communication tool. It forces honesty about money — the leading cause of divorce, cited by 38% of respondents in a 2023 Harris Poll for the American Psychological Association. It provides clarity when your emotions are running high. And yes, it protects you financially if the marriage ends.

The myths about prenups are just that — myths. They're for rich people? My wife and I are solidly middle-class, and it worked for us. They're a trust issue? They actually built trust between us. They don't hold up in court? With proper execution, they do — I've read the case law.

If you're getting married — or even if you're just starting to think about marriage — I'd recommend spending a weekend doing a financial inventory with your partner and having the prenup conversation. Not because you expect divorce, but because understanding each other's financial expectations is one of the most honest, caring things you can do before joining your lives together.

The document you might sign isn't the real value. The conversations you'll have on the way there are.
