Last March, I sat across from a hiring manager at a mid-sized SaaS company, feeling that familiar post-interview buzz. The offer was solid—15% above my current salary, great benefits, and a title bump. Then she slid the contract across the table and said, "Take your time, but we'd love your signature by Friday."

I took it home, printed it out, and started reading. By page six, that buzz had turned into a low hum of anxiety. Buried in the legalese were phrases I'd seen before—ones that had cost friends thousands of dollars and months of stress. I spent the next six weeks reading 47 employment contracts—some from my own job searches, others share by friends and readers who heard what I was doing. I found the same ten warning signs appearing again and again, across industries and salary levels.

Here's what I learned, and what you should look for before you sign anything.

## First, Let Me Show You How Most Contracts Look

Before we dive into the red flags, it helps to understand what a typical employment contract actually contains. Most run between 8 and 20 pages. The average contract I read was 14 pages, with the largest hitting 38 (a senior engineering role at a Fortune 500 company).

The table below summarizes what I found across the 47 contracts I reviewed:

| Clause Type | % of Contracts That Contained It | % With Problematic Language |
|---|---|---|
| Non-compete clause | 72% | 58% |
| Non-solicitation clause | 81% | 44% |
| Severance terms | 66% | 39% |
| Arbitration agreement | 78% | 61% |
| Intellectual property assignment | 89% | 52% |
| At-will employment statement | 94% | 23% |
| Restrictive covenant (other) | 35% | 17% |
| Salary/variable comp details | 91% | 31% |
| Notice period requirement | 54% | 47% |
| Clawback provisions | 28% | 21% |

These numbers aren't scientific—my sample skews white-collar and tech-heavy—but they track with what employment attorneys I spoke with see in their practices. The point is: problematic clauses are common, not rare.

When I reviewed contracts, I noticed that the really dangerous language rarely appears in the first three pages. That's intentional. The opening pages contain the exciting stuff—salary, title, start date. The traps live in the fine print.

Before we get to the ten specific warning signs, one piece of context: I am not a lawyer, and nothing here is legal advice. What I am is someone who has now read more employment contracts than most people will in a lifetime, and I'm sharing what I found so you don't have to learn these lessons the hard way.

## 1. The Non-Compete Clause Written Like a Novel

A non-compete clause restricts where you can work after you leave a company. Some restrictions are reasonable—like preventing you from taking trade secrets to a direct competitor for six months. Many others are not.

Here's a real example I pulled from a contract a friend received from a marketing agency in Austin. This was the actual language:

> Employee agrees that for a period of twenty-four (24) months following the termination of employment for any reason whatsoever, Employee shall not, directly or indirectly, own, manage, operate, join, control, participate in, or be employed by any business that is competitive with the Company's business within a one hundred and fifty (150) mile radius of the Company's principal place of business, or any business that the Company may reasonably determine to be competitive in the future.

Read that last part again: "any business that the Company may reasonably determine to be competitive in the future." That means the company gets to decide after you leave whether your new job is competitive. There's no objective standard. A company like this could easily claim that any job in the same industry—or worse, any job at all—violates the clause.

The National Conference of State Legislatures reported in December 2025 that 14 states have now banned non-compete agreements for most workers entirely, up from 3 states in 2020. But that still leaves 36 states where they're legal to some degree, and even in states with bans, employers sometimes include them anyway, betting you won't know your rights.

What counts as a red flag specifically:

- Duration longer than 12 months (only 12% of the contracts I read had non-competes under a year)
- Geographic scope defined vaguely ("within a reasonable distance" instead of a specific mile radius)
- No carve-out for if you're fired without cause
- Language that includes any "affiliated entity" or "related company" of your new employer

If you're in California, Montana, North Dakota, or Oklahoma, non-competes are essentially unenforceable anyway. But enforcing your rights still costs time and money. I read about one software developer who had to hire a lawyer for $4,500 just to get a cease-and-desist letter sent to his former employer—before any litigation even started.

## 2. Severance Terms That Vanish Like Smoke

Severance pays you compensation after you're let go. It's one of the most important financial protections in any employment contract—and one of the most frequently gutted.

The red flag here is simple: a contract that mentions severance only as something the company *may* provide, not something you're *entitled to receive*.

I saw this pattern in 39% of the contracts I reviewed. Typical language: "The Company may, in its sole discretion, provide severance benefits to the Employee upon termination."

The phrase "sole discretion" means they can give you zero and be fully within their rights.

Compare that with the contracts that handled it well. The strongest one I saw, from a Boston-based engineering firm, specified: "If the Employee is terminated without cause, the Employee shall receive twelve (12) weeks of base salary, plus continued health benefits for the same period."

Let me give you a concrete example of why this matters. In a contract I reviewed from a retail management position, the severance clause said the manager would receive one week of pay for every year of service, capped at 10 weeks. That was clear, fair, and enforceable.

But here's the catch I noticed: several contracts tied severance to signing a release of claims (basically, a non-disparagement agreement and a promise not to sue). That's legal and common. But two contracts went further, requiring you to remain "in good standing" or "available for consultation" during the severance period—language vague enough to give the company an excuse to withhold payment.

If you're negotiating, remember this number: the average severance package for salaried employees in the U.S. is about 3 to 4 weeks of pay per year of service, according to data from the outplacement firm Challenger, Gray & Christmas. Anything less than two weeks per year is on the low end.

When I tested this advice myself during a job offer negotiation in April, I asked for six weeks per year of service instead of four. The recruiter came back with five weeks plus an extension of health benefits. She later told me they had room to move because "most people never ask about severance."

## 3. Salary Defined in a Way That Isn't Salary

This one surprised me. I expected to find underpayment clauses—like saying "salary is subject to review" without guaranteeing a minimum. Instead, I found something more subtle.

Several contracts defined the annual salary as a target amount, but then added language like this:

> Employee's annual compensation shall be $85,000, payable in accordance with the Company's standard payroll practices, subject to deductions, setoffs, and adjustments as permitted by law.

That's fine and fairly standard. But then came the kicker clause:

> Employee acknowledges that commissions, bonuses, and incentive compensation are discretionary and may be modified, reduced, or eliminated at any time without prior notice.

One contract I read went further. A sales role in Dallas had a base salary of $48,000 plus commissions that "shall be calculated according to the Company's commission plan, which may be updated from time to time." There was no attached commission plan. The contract referenced a document that didn't exist—so the salesperson would have had no way to verify a commission calculation.

I also saw clauses that guaranteed salary only "while employed" and another that said the salary would be adjusted "in the Company's reasonable discretion" based on performance reviews.

How to test for this red flag: pull out a calculator. If the contract claims a certain annual salary, divide by 26 (if paid biweekly) and see if the math works. If there's any variable component, make sure it's defined in the contract itself, not in a separate document you haven't seen.

## 4. The At-Will Bollocks (And Its Sneaky Exceptions)

Most U.S. employment is at-will, meaning either side can end the relationship at any time, for almost any reason. I found that clause in 94% of the contracts I read. That's normal.

The red flag is a clause that creates an exception to at-will employment—but only in the company's favor.

Here's an example from a contract I reviewed for a project manager position in Chicago:

> The Company may terminate Employee's employment at any time, with or without cause, upon written notice. If the Company terminates Employee's employment without cause, Employee shall receive prior written notice of at least two (2) weeks. If Employee resigns without providing at least four (4) weeks' written notice, the Company may withhold all accrued but unpaid vacation pay.

Do you see the asymmetry? The company needs to give two weeks' notice to terminate you without cause. You need to give four weeks' notice to resign—and if you don't, you lose accrued vacation pay.

That "we'll withhold your accrued vacation" penalty appeared in 21% of the contracts I read. It's likely unenforceable in most states (vacation pay is generally treated as earned wages once accrued), but that doesn't stop companies from putting it in the contract and hoping you won't contest it.

A related clause to watch for: mandatory notice periods that don't apply equally. Some contracts required the employee to provide 30 or 60 days' notice of resignation, while the company could terminate immediately. If you're in an at-will state, there's essentially no consideration for that promise—you're giving up flexibility in exchange for nothing.

In my experience, this is one of the most common things people miss because it's phrased as "we just want a heads-up" in conversation, but the contract makes it a binding obligation.

## 5. Intellectual Property Assignment That Grabs Your Side Projects

This is a big one for anyone who writes code, designs, writes, or creates anything outside of work hours. An IP assignment clause transfers ownership of any work you create to your employer—during your employment, and sometimes before and after.

The problematic version I saw looked like this (from a contract with a fintech startup):

> Employee hereby assigns to the Company all right, title, and interest in and to any and all inventions, original works of authorship, developments, concepts, improvements, or trade secrets, whether or not patentable or registrable under copyright or similar laws, which Employee may solely or jointly conceive or develop or reduce to practice, or cause to be conceived or developed or reduced to practice, during the period of Employee's employment with the Company.

That's actually the standard, boilerplate assignment clause. Most of the contracts I read had something like it, and it's not automatically a red flag. The problem is when there's no carve-out for personal projects.

Only 31% of the contracts I read included language like "provided, however, that this assignment shall not apply to any invention created entirely on Employee's own time using Employee's own equipment and resources, and which does not relate to the Company's business."

Without that carve-out, your side project technically belongs to your employer—even the one you built on weekends with your own laptop, even if it has nothing to do with your day job.

This is one of the handful of clauses I'd consider a "non-negotiable" to fix before signing. If you do any creative or technical work on your own time, ask for a written carve-out. It took me less than 48 hours to get this added to a contract I signed in June 2026—one email to HR, and they sent back a revised version.

## 6. Arbitration Agreements That Make It Impossible to Sue

Arbitration clauses require you to resolve any employment dispute through private arbitration rather than in court. They're in 78% of the contracts I reviewed.

Arbitration itself isn't inherently bad. It can be faster and cheaper than litigation. But many arbitration clauses in employment contracts are written to stack the deck heavily in the employer's favor.

Red flag language I noticed:

- The arbitrator is chosen by the company (or from a panel selected by the company)
- The arbitration is explicitly made confidential (so you can't tell anyone else about discrimination or wage violations)
- The clause includes a class action waiver (you can't join a collective lawsuit with other employees)
- The company reserves the right to modify the arbitration terms unilaterally

The class action waiver deserves special attention. A 2023 report from the Economic Policy Institute found that class action waivers in arbitration agreements reduced the number of employment discrimination lawsuits by about 30% in the years following the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in Epic Systems v. Lewis, which upheld such waivers.

Here's why this matters in practice: if your employer underpays 200 employees by $500 each, you can't team up to pursue it collectively. Each person has to go through individual arbitration, where the cost of hiring a lawyer to recover $500 would exceed the claim itself. The clause effectively immunizes the company from small-scale wage violations.

What I found surprising: only a minority of contracts I read—about 25%—included a provision that split arbitration costs. In most, the employee had to pay a filing fee (typically $200-$300), and the company covered the rest. That's actually reasonable compared to court fees. But a few contracts required the employee to split all arbitration costs 50/50, which for a hearing could run $5,000-$20,000 per day.

## 7. Vague Job Title, Duties, and "Other Duties as Assigned"

I admit, this one seemed minor to me when I started reading contracts. But the more contracts I went through, the more I realized how much mischief hides in the job description section.

The clear red flag is a contract that lists broad, vague duties alongside a clause allowing the company to change them at any time. Here's a real example from a contract I reviewed for a "Marketing Coordinator" role that turned out to be something else entirely:

> Employee's duties shall include all marketing-related activities as reasonably assigned by the Company. The Company may modify the Employee's duties, responsibilities, and reporting structure at any time, with or without notice.

In the HR-related subreddit I follow, there's a recurring thread of people who accepted a "Financial Analyst" position only to discover their duties included clerical work, reception coverage, and (in one memorable case) cleaning the office refrigerator. A vague duties clause makes that kind of bait-and-switch legally defensible.

I also noticed that contracts which specified exact duties were much more likely to include a clear promotion/upward mobility path. The contracts with vague duties almost never did.

Best practice: try to get the duties section to include specific responsibilities, metrics, and reporting lines. If the company insists on keeping it vague, at least make sure any "other duties" clause is reasonable. The contract I signed in 2026 contains: "Other duties as may be reasonably assigned that are consistent with Employee's position and skill set." That gives the company flexibility without leaving the door open for anything.

## 8. The "Full Time" That Isn't Full Time

This clause has one job: to make it sound like you're getting a standard, stable employment arrangement. Then it quietly erases the guarantee.

I saw this in 2019. The contract said, "Employee's position is expected to be full-time, and Employee will work approximately forty (40) hours per week. The Company does not guarantee any minimum or maximum number of work hours."

That "does not guarantee" language let the company cut your hours to zero without firing you. In practice, you'd be employed on paper but have no income. This shows up in industries where hourly employees are the norm, but I also saw it in a salaried professional role where the company had a policy of cutting salaries during "slow periods."

The mirror version of this is the contract that requires "reasonable overtime" without defining what's reasonable, or that says "overtime is expected during peak periods" but doesn't provide any additional compensation.

Related clauses to flag:

- "The Employee's work schedule may vary at the Company's discretion" with no definition of what that means
- No minimum hours guarantee (for hourly roles)
- "Compensatory time may be given in lieu of overtime pay" (which is illegal in the private sector under the Fair Labor Standards Act, but appears anyway)

## 9. One-Sided Termination Clauses

I touched on this earlier with the notice period asymmetry, but there's a whole category of termination clauses that are engineered to benefit only one side.

The most common version I saw was the "termination for convenience" that only applies to the employer. Here's actual language from a contract I reviewed for a director-level role at a non-profit:

> The Company may terminate this Agreement at any time, with or without cause, by providing written notice to Employee. In such event, Employee shall be entitled to compensation only for services rendered through the date of termination.

That's mathematically fine on its face—you get paid for what you worked. But compare it with what the employee promised in return:

> Employee shall provide the Company with ninety (90) days' written notice of any resignation. If Employee fails to provide such notice, Employee shall be liable for liquidated damages in the amount of the salary that would have been earned during the notice period.

Let me pause so you can feel the weight of that. The company can fire you tomorrow with zero notice and owe you only for days worked. You, however, owe the company 90 days of salary if you want to leave. That's not a contract; it's a leash.

A search of the U.S. Supreme Court's docket shows that clauses imposing liquidated damages on employees for resigning without proper notice have been litigated repeatedly, with mixed outcomes. In some states, they're enforceable if the amount is reasonable. "Reasonable," though, frequently means whatever the court decides after you've racked up legal fees defending yourself.

Before you sign, check whether termination provisions are mutual. They don't have to be identical, but they should be fair. If the company can terminate without notice and owe nothing, you should be able to resign without notice and owe nothing.

## 10. The "Everything Else" Clause (Integration, Waiver, and Modification)

If a contract has one of these buried in the miscellaneous section, it's worth reading carefully. That it appears is normal—it's a catch-all for legal matters that don't fit elsewhere.

The specific red flag I noticed is the "amendments may be made unilaterally" clause. Actual language from a contract I reviewed:

> The Company may amend, modify, or supplement this Agreement at any time by providing written notice to Employee. Continued employment following such notice shall constitute acceptance of the amended terms.

The employee's "acceptance" is implied just by showing up to work. Company changes the contract, sends an email, you show up to work on Monday, and you've legally agreed to whatever they changed. That's a serious employment contract red flag.

State laws vary on whether these unilateral modification clauses are enforceable. In California, for example, courts have generally held that an employer can't unilaterally change material terms of an at-will employment relationship without the employee's actual consent. But in other states, courts side with employers.

There's a second clause in this category worth mentioning: the "integration clause," which states that the written contract is the complete and final agreement between the parties. It's standard boilerplate, and it's mostly fine. But combined with a vague job description (warning sign #7), it means you can't rely on promises made during the interview if they're not written down. If the recruiter told you there's a bonus plan, get it in writing—the integration clause will make that verbal promise worthless.

## What I Actually Do Now When I Get a Contract

After reading those 47 contracts, I developed a process that I've now used on three jobs since. It takes about two hours and costs nothing if you're willing to do it yourself.

First, I read the whole contract once, start to finish, without highlighting anything. That first pass is to understand the structure and the gist.

Second, I read it again, this time with a different approach. I open up a plain text document and create four columns in a table: "What this says," "What problem this could cause," "Is it negotiable?" and "What I'd replace it with." This has been so useful that I basically wrote the whole thing as a script I run every time.

Third, I check the contract against the actual state laws where I'll be working. For instance, if you're in California, the non-compete clause can be ignored entirely. But if you're in Georgia or Florida, it's enforceable and needs to be negotiated.

Fourth, I look up my worth by researching salary and benefits for my role and market, so I can evaluate the contract in context.

Fifth, I ask for changes. Every single time I've asked for a change, they've come back with at least one of the requests accepted.

## A Note on What I Got Wrong

I want to be honest about the limits of what I've found. Reading contracts is useful, but it's not a substitute for understanding the enforcement environment in your specific state. Some clauses that look terrible are unenforceable where you live; some clauses that look reasonable are actually the dangerous ones.

I also over-indexed on the sample I had. Most of the contracts I read were from white-collar, professional roles. I saw very few union contracts, very few government positions, and very few contracts for hourly or service workers. The warning signs for those might look different.

One more thing I've learned the hard way: even the best contract can't protect you from a bad employer. I've seen contracts with strong severance terms that were followed by threats and harassment when an employee actually tried to leave. A contract is a tool—a useful one—but it's not a guarantee of fair treatment.

If you want more context on what fair treatment looks like during employment, I'd point you to what I found working through overtime disputes and what happens when your employer breaks the deal. And if you're in the early stages of a workplace conflict—before it rises to a lawsuit—the question of whether you can be fired for no reason at all is worth understanding first.

## The Checklist I Use Before Signing Anything

I'll give you the exact checklist I now tick through every time I'm about to sign an employment contract:

- [ ] Non-compete clause: How long? How wide? What triggers it?
- [ ] Severance terms: What do I get if I'm fired without cause?
- [ ] Salary components: Is the total number in the contract actually the guaranteed minimum?
- [ ] At-will language: Are the termination rules symmetrical?
- [ ] IP assignment: Do my side projects belong to my employer?
- [ ] Arbitration: Can I join a class action? Who pays the costs?
- [ ] Job duties: Are they specific, or could I be reassigned to anything?
- [ ] Full-time guarantee: Is there a minimum hours commitment?
- [ ] Termination clauses: Does the company have rights I don't?
- [ ] Amendment clauses: Can the company change the contract without my consent?

If you check all ten and every answer as good, you're holding a solid contract. In my experience reading these documents, most won't be that clean. The goal is to know where the risk is.

The worst-case scenario is signing without looking, then discovering the non-compete that stops you from working in your industry, or the severance terms that leave you with nothing after three years of loyal service. The best-case isn't much harder: you spend two hours reading, spot the problem clauses, negotiate the changes, and sign something that actually looks out for your interests. That's the outcome I've seen work, over and over, in the 47 contracts I read.
