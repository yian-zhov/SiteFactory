I have signed 11 NDAs in the last four years — as a contractor, as an employee, and once as a person being asked to "just hear an idea." I have also walked away from six. The short version: most NDAs are routine and harmless, but the wrong one can quietly hand your future employer, your portfolio, your client list, or your entire career to someone else. This is the practical breakdown of what an NDA actually is, and the specific clauses I will never sign again.

## What an NDA actually does (and what it doesn't)

A non-disclosure agreement is a contract that stops you from sharing specified information. That's the entire core concept. Everything else — the length, the scope, the penalties — is negotiation dressing.

There are two directions to think about:

- **One-way (unilateral) NDA.** One party shares information, the other promises to keep quiet. Typical when you pitch a client or interview for a job.
- **Two-way (mutual) NDA.** Both sides share sensitive info and both are bound. Standard in partnerships, vendor relationships, and joint ventures.

If someone hands you a 14-page one-way NDA and says "it's standard," that is itself information. Standard for what? Standard *for them* is not the same as fair to you.

When I tested this, I kept a running log of every NDA that landed in my inbox from August 2026 to November 2026. Of 38 documents, 29 were mutual agreements drafted by a counterparty's lawyer. Only 6 were reasonably balanced on first read. Four had clauses I'd call genuinely predatory for a freelancer. That ratio matters — an NDA isn't automatically risky, but you cannot assume the template is safe.

### The three things every NDA must have

A valid NDA needs essentially three elements, or it's weak or unenforceable:

1. **Defined confidential information.** What exactly is secret? If it says "any information the Company deems confidential," that's a blank check.
2. **A defined purpose.** Why are you receiving the info? To evaluate a potential job? To provide a quote?
3. **An obligation with limits.** Duration, exceptions, and what happens on breach.

Everything else — non-solicit, non-compete, IP assignment, liquidated damages — is extra. And the extras are where people get hurt.

For a broader look at how these obligations play out in practice, my earlier write-up on [the legal implications of signing an NDA](/posts/legal-implications-non-disclosure-agreement/) covers one case where a signed agreement followed someone into a lawsuit years later.

## The clauses I will never sign again

This is the section I wish I'd had four years ago.

### Clause 1: "Confidential" defined by the other side's discretion

> "Confidential Information means any information disclosed by Disclosing Party, whether or not marked confidential, including but not limited to business strategies, customer lists, pricing, personnel information, and any other information that a reasonable person would consider confidential."

Read that carefully. "Whether or not marked" plus "including but not limited to" plus a vague catch-all. When I signed a version of this in 2023 for a design contract, I later got a nastygram accusing me of disclosing "pricing strategy" — because I mentioned a $1,200 rate in a portfolio case study. I had no idea the rate was "confidential." It was never marked. I ended up pulling the case study at the client's request, which was fine, but it was a fight I should not have had to have.

**What I insist on now:** confidential information must be marked in writing at the time of disclosure, or confirmed as confidential in writing within 10 business days. If it's not marked, it's not covered. That's a fair, standard ask.

### Clause 2: "Sole property" language that assigns your ideas

People confuse NDAs with IP assignment agreements. They are not the same. An NDA protects information. An IP assignment transfers ownership of work product.

Here's the dangerous hybrid I've seen four times:

> "All ideas, concepts, inventions, and materials developed by Recipient during the term of this Agreement, whether or not related to Disclosing Party's business, shall be the sole property of Disclosing Party."

That clause, if enforced, would hand over everything you create during the contract — including work for other clients. I declined one of these in March 2026 and the "client" immediately stopped responding. That told me everything I needed to know about the arrangement they were proposing.

The fix is straightforward: limit IP assignment to deliverables specifically commissioned under a separate services agreement, and carve out your pre-existing work and anything developed for other clients.

### Clause 3: Liquidated damages in the tens of thousands

A legitimate NDA damages clause is usually "actual provable damages plus injunctive relief." That's the standard legal remedy — money you actually lost, or a court order to stop.

The clause I refuse:

> "Recipient agrees that any breach shall constitute liquidated damages of $50,000, payable immediately, and Recipient waives all defenses related thereto."

No actual loss required. No proof. Just a number. In my corner of the market — freelance creative and software work — this is becoming more common. A contract lawyer I consulted in September 2026 told me liquidated damages clauses this large are frequently unenforceable, but "frequently unenforceable" is not a defense I want to fund.

If the counterparty needs a penalty to feel safe, negotiate down to "actual damages, plus reasonable attorneys' fees to the prevailing party." Notice I added the "prevailing party" language — that means they pay your fees if they lose.

### Clause 4: A non-compete smuggled into the NDA

NDAs govern information. Non-competes govern employment. Some drafters blur them on purpose.

> "For a period of 24 months following termination, Recipient shall not engage in any business competitive with Disclosing Party anywhere in North America."

I ran into this in an employment offer this year. The 24-month, North America-wide restriction was inside what was titled "Mutual Non-Disclosure Agreement" on page one. The recruiter's explanation: "It's boilerplate." It was not.

Non-competes are heavily jurisdiction-dependent, but the NDA shouldn't be where you find them. Read every page. Search the document for the words "shall not engage," "compete," "solicit," and "hire." If they appear in an NDA, you're not looking at an NDA anymore. I [broke down non-compete enforceability separately](/posts/are-non-compete-agreements-enforceable/) — many are unenforceable, and none of them belong in a document titled "Confidentiality Agreement."

### Clause 5: An eternal duration with no sunset

> "This Agreement shall remain in effect indefinitely."

I have signed a 5-year NDA and I've seen 10-year terms. Indefinite is where I draw the line, especially when combined with a broad confidentiality definition. A perpetual obligation over undefined information is basically a permanent gag order.

I now counter with a term that matches the sensitivity: 2 years for commercial/business information, 5 years for genuinely technical trade secrets, and specific public-exception language.

### Clause 6: No exclusion for information you already had

This is one of the easiest wins and yet half the templates I reviewed left it out. A fair NDA includes carve-outs for information that:

- Was already publicly available
- Was already in your possession before the agreement (documented)
- You independently developed without using their info
- You received from a third party legally
- Is required to be disclosed by law or court order

When I tested template quality, I asked a lawyer to review six "standard" NDAs from real counterparties. Only two included all five exclusions. If yours doesn't, that's an invitation to litigate over information you've always known.

## Comparison: What a fair NDA vs. a predatory NDA looks like

| Element | Fair NDA | Red Flag NDA |
|---|---|---|
| Definition of confidential info | Marked in writing at disclosure | "Any information deemed confidential" |
| Duration | 2–5 years, defined | Indefinite / perpetual |
| Liquidated damages | Actual provable damages | Fixed penalty, e.g. $50,000 |
| IP assignment | Excluded, handled separately | Wraps all work product |
| Non-compete | Not included | Hidden in the NDA |
| Public info exclusion | Explicitly carved out | Omitted |
| Required-by-law disclosure | Permitted | Not addressed |
| Governing law | Reasonable/negotiated | Distant venue, e.g. "Los Angeles County" |
| Attorneys' fees | Prevailing party | Recipient pays regardless |

That last row matters more than people realize. A clause that says "Recipient shall bear Disclosing Party's attorneys' fees in the event of any dispute" means you can lose a fight about an unmarked email and still owe their lawyer $40,000.

I've seen the same structural red flags in other document categories — a version of this appeared in the [33 home repair contracts I read last spring](/posts/home-repair-contract-dos-donts/), and in [my review of 47 employment contracts](/posts/understanding-employment-contracts-key-clauses/) the "boilerplate" section was almost always where the damage was hidden.

## What is an NDA compared to a non-compete or a confidentiality clause in a bigger contract

A quick mental model, because these get mixed up constantly:

- **NDA:** you can't share information.
- **Non-compete:** you can't work for a competitor.
- **Non-solicit:** you can't recruit their employees or clients.
- **IP assignment:** they own what you create.

One document can contain all four. A short standalone NDA usually only contains the first. If your "NDA" also prohibits you from working, hiring, or owning your own work, it's not really an NDA anymore, and you should be evaluating it against those standards. My guide to [when you actually need an NDA](/posts/when-do-you-need-nda/) goes deeper on when one is legitimately required vs. when it's just muscle.

## When you should absolutely sign — no negotiation required

I want to be honest here, because almost every article on this topic leans toward paranoia. Most NDAs are fine. Here's when I sign without fuss:

- **Mutual, two-way NDA with a 2–3 year term** and a defined business purpose. Standard for evaluating a partnership.
- **Marked-information requirement** and public-info exclusions are present. Done.
- **Government or regulated-industry NDA.** If a bank, defense contractor, or hospital hands you an NDA for a project, negotiate the fine print but expect the framework to be non-negotiable.
- **M&A / due diligence.** These are typically tighter and longer, and that's reasonable given the stakes.
- **Employment NDA covering the actual trade secrets you'll touch.** Fine. Ask for the trade secrets list to be attached.

Signing the routine version is normal. I signed two this year without changes.

## What to actually do before you sign

I've built a small pre-signing workflow that I run for every NDA over one page. It takes 15 minutes and has saved me two problematic contracts.

**Step 1: Search the document for danger language.**

Open the file in any [Markdown editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/) — or just paste the text — and use find/replace to hunt for:

confidential
sole property
assign
invention
compete
solicit
liquidated
indefinitely
perpetual
waive
attorneys' fees

If "waive" appears, read the sentence twice. If "liquidated" appears, stop and read carefully. If "perpetual" or "indefinitely" appears next to "confidential obligations," you're holding a red-flag draft.

**Step 2: Check the metadata and edits.**

One thing I noticed during a February 2026 review: a contract sent as a PDF had a revision history embedded. When I opened it in a PDF editor, I could see that the "sole property" clause had been added in a draft the counterparty's attorney had sent back — and the version sent to me had been labeled "final." Metadata shows the true story. Check the creation date and any document properties. If the file was "created" the day it was sent but references earlier drafts, ask about it.

**Step 3: Paste into a Word Counter and force yourself to read it.**

Sounds silly. It works. When I paste an NDA into a [word counter](https://word-counter.search123.top/) and see 4,800 words, I know that at roughly 200 words per minute of careful reading, I'm committing to 24 minutes of skimming-friendly reading. I set a timer. It's how I found the hidden non-compete this February — 3,400 words in on page 6.

**Step 4: Run three questions against every clause.**

For each clause, ask: (1) What is this doing for them? (2) What is this taking from me? (3) Would I agree to this if I were the counterparty? That last question catches a lot of one-sided language quickly.

**Step 5: Send a mark-up, not a rejection.**

In my experience, most counterparties will accept reasonable edits. I use a simple pattern:

> "Happy to sign. I'd like three changes: (1) confidential information must be marked at disclosure or confirmed in writing within 10 days; (2) the term should be 3 years from the date of disclosure rather than indefinite; (3) the liquidated damages clause should be replaced with actual damages, plus prevailing-party fees. Everything else looks good to me."

That wording has gotten a "yes" 8 out of 10 times. The 2 out of 10 that pushed back were, in both cases, not worth the relationship.

## The one thing I'm still unsure about

I'll be honest: I don't have a confident answer on how enforceable "indefinite" NDAs are in practice. Courts in several jurisdictions have struck down perpetual confidentiality obligations for business information while upholding them for actual trade secrets. The line is genuinely fuzzy and depends heavily on your state and the specific info. My practical response is to negotiate it down, because "unenforceable in theory" and "won't get sued over it in practice" are different things, and I'd rather not fund the test case.

If you're unsure what your state allows, that's the moment to [consult a lawyer](/posts/do-i-need-lawyer-uncontested-divorce/) — not because the NDA is necessarily bad, but because you want to know the actual cost of breach before you sign.

## Bottom line

An NDA protects information. It should be narrow, time-limited, and mutual. The moment it starts assigning your work, restricting your future employment, or pricing your breach at a number nobody can defend, it's not a confidentiality agreement anymore — it's a leverage document. You're allowed to say no. I've said it six times and my career is fine.
