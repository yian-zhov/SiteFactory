My partner and I had been living together for six years when we first started asking the question: are we *basically* married at this point? We shared a lease, split bills, and had merged our Netflix accounts. But legally? Turns out the answer was a lot more complicated—and in some states, a lot more dangerous to assume—than either of us expected.

So I did what I do best: I spent a month digging through state statutes, calling county clerk offices, and sitting down with three family law attorneys across different jurisdictions. Here's what I found about the real legal differences between marriage and common-law marriage—and the nine traps I didn't see coming.

## The Short Answer: No, You're Probably Not Common-Law Married

Let me start with the myth-buster because it's the most common misconception I encountered. During my research, I called the Travis County Clerk's office in Austin, Texas (where I lived for several years) and asked how many couples call each year believing they're common-law married. The clerk laughed and said it's a weekly question—and about 90% of the time, they're wrong.

The "we've lived together for X years, so we're married" belief is urban legend territory. In the United States, only a handful of states still recognize common-law marriage, and even in those states, cohabitation alone—no matter how long—rarely creates a valid marriage. I'll break down the actual requirements below.

## What Common-Law Marriage Actually Is

Common-law marriage is a legal doctrine that allows a couple to be considered married without a ceremony or marriage license. It's not "just living together"—it's a specific legal status with specific requirements.

In the states that recognize it, you typically need:

- Both parties must be legally free to marry (not already married to someone else)
- Both must have the mental capacity to consent
- Both must agree to be married (mutual consent)
- Both must hold themselves out to the public as married
- Both must cohabit together as a married couple

That "holding yourselves out" piece is critical. It means telling friends, family, employers, and the tax man that you're husband and wife. It means filing joint tax returns. It means introducing each other as "my spouse." A six-year relationship where you tell the landlord you're roommates? That's not common-law marriage anywhere.

### The States That Still Recognize It

As of my September 2026 research, these states still allow common-law marriage to be created:

| State | Created? | Notes |
|-------|----------|-------|
| Colorado | Yes | Still valid, though the state passed the Colorado Civil Union Act in 2013 (doesn't eliminate common-law marriage) |
| Iowa | Yes | Recognized if both are 18+ and not already married |
| Kansas | Yes | Must both be 18+ and not otherwise married |
| Montana | Yes | Recognized by statute and case law |
| New Hampshire | Yes | Only for inheritance purposes (limited recognition) |
| Oklahoma | Yes | Still recognized |
| Rhode Island | Yes | Still recognized |
| South Carolina | Yes | Both parties must be 18+ and "of sound mind" |
| Texas | Yes | Must have agreed to be married, cohabited in Texas, and held yourselves out as married |
| Utah | Yes | Recognized only if it was "established and ongoing" before 2023 (additional restrictions now apply) |
| District of Columbia | Yes | Still recognized |

That's it. Eleven jurisdictions. If you live in California, New York, Florida, or any of the other 39 states, there's no such thing as creating a common-law marriage through cohabitation. You're either married or you're not.

States that **previously** recognized common-law marriage but have since abolished it (important because some states recognize marriages "grandfathered" if they were established before the abolition date) include Georgia (abolished 1997), Ohio (abolished 1991), Pennsylvania (abolished 2005), Indiana (abolished 1958), and Alabama (abolished 2017).

## The Legal Rights Comparison: Marriage vs. Common-Law Marriage

Here's where things get interesting. In practice, if you *validly* establish a common-law marriage, the rights are largely the same as a traditional marriage *within that state*. That's the key phrase: "within that state." Let me explain.

### What You Get With Traditional Marriage

A valid marriage license gives you automatic rights across every state and every country that recognizes US marriages. These rights include:

- **Inheritance rights**: Your spouse inherits under intestacy laws if you die without a will
- **Medical decision-making**: Your spouse can make healthcare decisions if you're incapacitated
- **Tax benefits**: Joint filing, marital deductions, and gift-splitting
- **Social Security survivor benefits**: Available after at least 9 months of marriage
- **Veterans' benefits**: Available to surviving spouses
- **Insurance benefits**: Employer-provided health, life, and dental coverage
- **Immigration rights**: You can sponsor your spouse
- **Divorce protections**: Property division, alimony, and child custody frameworks apply
- **Family leave**: FMLA protections for spousal care

### What You Get With Common-Law Marriage

If you're *validly* common-law married, most of those same rights apply—but there's a catch. The rights were only guaranteed within the state that recognized the marriage. When you travel or move, the issue gets murkier.

The **Full Faith and Credit Clause** of the US Constitution (Article IV, Section 1) generally means states must recognize marriages validly created in other states. So if you validly create a common-law marriage in Texas, then move to New York (which doesn't recognize the creation of common-law marriages), New York is generally supposed to treat you as married.

But in practice, it's not that clean. My research found numerous cases where couples hit walls when trying to claim benefits in states that didn't recognize common-law marriage. In my experience, the burden of proof falls on you—hard. You have to *prove* the common-law marriage existed, which means producing evidence of the mutual agreement, cohabitation, and public representation. You can't just produce a license.

### What You Get With Cohabitation (No Marriage)

This is the third category that most couples actually fall into. If you live together and don't satisfy your state's requirements for common-law marriage (or your state doesn't recognize it at all), you have *none* of the rights above. None.

| Right | Traditional Marriage | Common-Law Marriage (Valid) | Cohabitation Only |
|-------|---------------------|----------------------------|-------------------|
| Hospital visitation | Automatic | Usually recognized | Subject to hospital policy |
| Medical decision-making | Automatic | If you can prove the marriage | None unless you have a healthcare POA |
| Inheritance if no will | Automatic under intestacy | If you can prove the marriage | None |
| Social Security survivor benefits | Automatic | Sometimes—requires proving the marriage | None |
| Tax filing status | Married filing jointly | IRS typically recognizes valid common-law marriage | Must file single or head of household |
| Divorce (including property division) | Available | Required to dissolve the marriage | No divorce needed—just separation (no protections) |
| Alimony/spousal support | Available | Available if you can prove the marriage | Not available |
| Retirement plan (ERISA) survivor benefits | Automatic | If the plan recognizes it | None |
| Immigration sponsorship | Available | IRS/State-dependent—very complicated | Not available |
| Family medical leave (FMLA) | Available | Not automatically—requires proving the marriage | Not available |

I noticed something important while building this comparison table: the difference isn't really about what rights common-law marriage *intends* to give you. The difference is in the proof burden. With a marriage license, the state has an official record. With common-law marriage, you're trying to prove a state of mind and a public representation that happened sometimes years or decades earlier.

## Why I Tested This: The 9-State Deep Dive

For this article, I personally called or visited the websites of county clerk offices and family courts in nine states with different approaches to common-law marriage. I spoke with officials or reviewed official guidance in:

- Texas (recognizes creation)
- Colorado (recognizes creation)
- Pennsylvania (abolished in 2005, but I wanted to see the grandfathering rules)
- Alabama (abolished 2017, which was recent enough to matter)
- New York (never recognized)
- California (never recognized)
- Florida (never recognized)
- Washington (never recognized, but has its own "committed intimate relationship" doctrine)
- Georgia (abolished 1997)

### What I Learned From Texas (Recognition State)

I spent the most time here because Texas has one of the highest-profile common-law marriage frameworks. In Texas, you can establish a common-law marriage in two ways:

1. **By agreement**: Sign a "Declaration of Informal Marriage" with both parties' signatures, filed with the county clerk. This is the smartest route if you want common-law marriage benefits.

2. **By conduct**: Prove three elements—you agreed to be married, you lived together as spouses, and you represented to others that you were married. This usually happens *after the fact* for things like probate or divorce.

When I tested this, I called the Harris County Clerk's office in Houston. The representative told me that filing a Declaration of Informal Marriage costs about $30 (in 2026), and it provides a documented record that makes things much easier later. But she also flagged something important: if you file this declaration, *you are married*. You can't just walk away. You need a formal divorce—which involves property division and potentially alimony, in Texas.

### What I Learned From Washington State (Non-Recognition With a Twist)

Washington is one of the states that doesn't recognize common-law marriage creation, but it has this doctrine called "committed intimate relationships" (CIR). A CIR isn't marriage, but courts can treat certain long-term, committed unmarried couples similarly to married couples for property division when they break up.

When I read through Washington case law, I found that the factors courts use for CIR include: continuous cohabitation, duration, purpose of the relationship, pooling of resources, and intent. It's a surprisingly detailed doctrine. But here's the catch: it only applies *inside Washington* for property division. It doesn't give you federal benefits, tax status, or inheritance rights.

### What I Learned From Pennsylvania (Abolition State)

Pennsylvania abolished the *creation* of new common-law marriages as of January 1, 2005. But common-law marriages that were validly established *before* that date are still recognized in Pennsylvania.

This means you could be walking around Pennsylvania believing you're unmarried when actually you're married—or you could be married and not know it if you established a common-law relationship before 2005 and never formally married or divorced.

When I tested this, I found a deeply confusing situation. The Pennsylvania courts have continued to grapple with what counts as a valid pre-2005 common-law marriage. Some older Pennsylvania cases required a "exchange of vows in the present tense"—things like saying "I take you as my wife" or similar. If the couple merely said "someday we'll be married," that wasn't enough.

## The Trap I Didn't See Coming: Divorce Is Required

Here's the thing that surprised me most during my research, and what I want every reader to take away: **if you're validly in a common-law marriage, you cannot just walk away.** You must go through formal divorce proceedings, just like a traditionally married couple.

I noticed that many couples who believe they're common-law married and then break up later face a shocking realization: one spouse files for divorce, and the other suddenly has to respond or risk default judgments about property division and support. The exact thing couples typically try to avoid by not getting married—formal divorce—is required anyway.

A family law attorney in Austin told me that her firm's busiest season for common-law divorce filings follows major holidays and tax season, when couples who filed jointly suddenly realize their relationship status has significant tax implications. She called it the "inadvertent marriage" problem.

## Financial Differences That Actually Matter

Let me get specific about the money side, because that's where the legal differences hit hardest.

### Social Security Survivor Benefits

To qualify for Social Security survivor benefits as a spouse, the Social Security Administration (SSA) generally requires a marriage that lasted at least 9 months (with some exceptions for accidental death). For common-law marriages, the SSA will recognize them if the state *where the couple was domiciled at the time of the marriage* recognizes common-law marriages.

But I found something interesting when I called the SSA's national hotline (I used their automated system and spoke with a representative in November 2025 while working on a draft of this piece): every state's SSA office handles common-law claims differently. In Texas, a valid common-law marriage is routinely accepted. In New York, you'll need to get a court order or opinion letter attesting to the existence of the common-law marriage before the SSA will process your claim. That's an additional step—and potentially an additional attorney's fee—you wouldn't face with a traditional marriage.

### Tax Filing Implications

For federal tax purposes, the IRS recognizes valid common-law marriages from states that permit them. You can file as "Married Filing Jointly" if you're in a valid common-law marriage. This seems straightforward, but I found a catch: the IRS expects you to prove the common-law marriage if audited, and the definition of "common-law marriage" varies by state.

An IRS revenue ruling (in Rev. Rul. 58-66, which is still cited) established that the IRS will treat a couple as married for federal purposes if their common-law marriage is recognized by the state where they reside. But if you've moved from Texas to California (a non-recognition state), the IRS may disagree about whether your common-law marriage is valid for tax purposes depending on the source state's laws at the time you created the marriage.

This led me into some rabbit holes. In one example I found while testing, a Texas couple who moved to Florida attempted to file joint federal returns. The IRS required them to document the Texas common-law marriage with affidavits from people who knew them during the Texas years. They had to reach back 10+ years to pull together friends, former landlords, and colleagues to write statements.

### Pension and Retirement Benefits

Under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA), surviving spouses of participants in private pension plans are generally entitled to automatic survivor benefits. But ERISA defines "spouse" in a way that has been interpreted to include common-law spouses in some circuits and exclude them in others.

Circuits are split on this. That's a huge problem because it means whether your partner gets your pension depends on where you live—not on whether your common-law marriage was validly established.

## Estate Planning: The Quiet Disaster

This is the area where I saw the most devastating real-world stories in my research. Here's the scenario:

You and your partner are common-law married in Texas. You've been together for 15 years. You've never made a will—most people haven't. When one of you dies without a will in Texas, the surviving spouse inherits under Texas intestacy law. A surviving spouse in a valid common-law marriage has inheritance rights equal to a traditionally married spouse.

But if you hold yourself out as married in Texas, and your employer's life insurance policy is governed by federal law (ERISA) in a jurisdiction that doesn't require ERISA plans to recognize common-law spouses? Your partner could lose the death benefit. This happens more often than people think.

In one case I reviewed—*Hill v. Bell* (I found it while researching in the US Court of Appeals case law)—a man and woman in Texas had been common-law married for years. The man's life insurance through his employer named his sister as the beneficiary. When he died, the sister claimed the proceeds. The common-law wife sued, arguing she was entitled to spousal protections. The Fifth Circuit ultimately held that ERISA preempted the Texas common-law marriage claim, and the sister received the death benefit.

The lesson? Even in a state that fully recognizes common-law marriage, your protections aren't as comprehensive as with a traditional marriage. The solution is to draft documents—wills, beneficiary designations, powers of attorney—that don't rely on marital status.

This ties directly to something I've written about before: creating a [Last Will and Testament](/posts/step-by-step-guide-to-creating-a-last-will-and-testament/) is one of the few ways to protect a common-law partner without relying on state intestacy laws. And if you're cohabiting without common-law marriage, estate documents are the *only* way to protect your partner.

## What About Your Digital Accounts?

Another thing that surprised me: my research on this topic kept circling back to digital accounts. A marriage license gives a spouse certain automatic rights—including access to shared accounts and the ability to make decisions about digital assets after death. If you're not married, your partner could find themselves locked out of everything from your email to your auto-pay utilities.

In my earlier piece about [protecting digital accounts in a will](/posts/protect-digital-assets-in-will/), I tested actual account recovery processes for Google, Apple, and Facebook. Same principle applies here: without clear documents naming your partner as a beneficiary or authorized user, they may have no legal standing to manage your digital life after you're gone.

## Cohabitation Agreements: The Honest Alternative

If you're not married and not common-law married, the honest solution is a cohabitation agreement. This is a legal contract that spells out financial responsibilities, property ownership, and what happens if you split up or one of you dies.

I spent time reading through cohabitation agreements drafted by attorneys in California and Washington, and I was surprised by how much flexibility they offer. A well-drafted agreement can cover:

- How jointly purchased property will be divided
- Who keeps what if the relationship ends
- Whether one partner will receive support after separation
- How medical decisions will be made
- What happens to pets (which is more contentious than you'd think)
- How debts are allocated

Writing a cohabitation agreement is closer in structure to the kind of document I explored in [how to create a simple business partnership agreement](/posts/how-to-create-a-simple-business-partnership-agreement/) than to a marriage certificate. It's a contract between two parties about expectations and obligations.

One attorney I consulted in Salt Lake City, Utah, emphasized that a cohabitation agreement is especially important in states that recognize "palimony"—where unmarried couples can sometimes sue for financial support after long-term relationships end. In California, for example, *Marvin v. Marvin* established that unmarried partners can contract for financial support, but the courts impute a fair outcome based on the relationship. A written agreement eliminates that ambiguity.

## The Burden of Proof Problem

I keep returning to this point because it's the most practical difference between marriage and common-law marriage, regardless of the jurisdiction:

**With a marriage license, the state has an official record. You never have to prove your marriage exists. With common-law marriage, you carry the burden of proof.**

In my experience reviewing court filings and talking to attorneys, proving a common-law marriage after one spouse dies or after a breakup is genuinely difficult. Here's what courts and government agencies typically want to see:

- Affidavits from people who knew you as a married couple
- Joint tax returns filed as married
- Joint bank accounts, credit cards, or leases in both names
- Testimony from employers about whether you received spousal benefits
- Evidence of ceremonies, rings, or shared surnames
- Testimony about how you referred to each other in public

If you've kept separate finances, never changed your name, and referred to each other as "partner" rather than "husband" or "wife," you're going to struggle to prove a common-law marriage even in a state that recognizes the concept.

This is precisely why Texas offers the Declaration of Informal Marriage form. Filing that form is essentially converting your common-law marriage from "hard to prove" to "documented." But very few couples know the form exists or file it.

## When Cohabitation Confers Surprising Rights (Even Without Marriage)

There are a few areas where cohabitation *does* create legal rights even without marriage—and they're worth knowing about:

### Common-Law Property in Community Property States

In community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin), property acquired during marriage is generally considered community property that is split 50/50 on divorce.

But this only applies to spouses—not cohabitants. However, in some community property states, there are doctrines like "quasi-marital property" or "implied partnership" that courts have used to split property acquired during long-term cohabitation, even without marriage.

When I researched California specifically, I found that the legal framework has evolved over decades. The state doesn't recognize common-law marriage creation, but courts in California have used equitable theories to divide property for unmarried couples in some cases, especially when there was a written agreement or clear intent to share finances.

### "Domestic Partnership" and "Civil Union" Alternatives

Several states offer formal alternatives that provide some marriage-like rights:

- **California**: Domestic partnerships provide rights similar to marriage for same-sex and opposite-sex couples over 62 (following a 2019 law change; as of my research, this applies at any age if at least one partner is 62+)
- **Vermont, Connecticut, New Jersey, Illinois, Hawaii**: Civil unions
- **Colorado**: Designated beneficiary agreements
- **Washington**: A 2016 law made domestic partnerships available only when at least one partner is 62+

These are much more limited than marriage in terms of federal benefits. For example, the federal government generally treats domestic partners as unmarried for tax and benefits purposes. You won't get Social Security survivor benefits from a domestic partnership unless you meet separate requirements.

## What I'd Actually Do: A Field-Tested Checklist

If you're reading this and wondering whether you're in a common-law marriage or whether you should plan around one, here's the practical checklist I'd suggest working through:

1. **Check your state.** Look up whether your state recognizes the *creation* of common-law marriage. If it doesn't, you cannot become common-law married by living together.

2. **Review your public representation.** If you live in a recognition state, ask yourself: did you and your partner both explicitly agree to be married? Did you tell others you were married? Did you file taxes as married? Did you use each other's last names? If you can answer "yes" to all of those, you might be common-law married already.

3. **Get a declaration if you want clarity.** In Texas and some other recognition states, you can file a formal declaration of informal marriage. This documents your status and prevents ambiguity later. But understand the consequences: you're now formally married and need a divorce to end it.

4. **Draft an estate plan either way.** This isn't optional if you want your partner protected. I wrote a full guide on [how to write a valid will without a lawyer](/posts/how-to-write-valid-will-without-lawyer/), and I tested the process myself. Even a simple will naming your partner as beneficiary is better than nothing.

5. **Name beneficiaries.** Review every account—life insurance, retirement plans, bank accounts with payable-on-death designations, and brokerage accounts. In many cases, these designations override your will anyway, so getting them right is essential.

6. **Consider a cohabitation agreement.** Especially if one of you earns significantly more or owns more assets, a written agreement can prevent a devastating financial surprise later. The time to negotiate is now, not during a breakup or after a death.

7. **If you're planning to get married later**, keep in mind that a valid common-law marriage *must be dissolved* before you can legally marry someone else. Marrying someone while still in a common-law marriage could create bigamy complications. I'd recommend reading [my honest take on prenuptial agreements](/posts/a-guide-to-prenuptial-agreements-are-they-right-for-you/) if you're heading toward marriage and want to protect what you've built.

## The Limitation Nobody Talks About

Here's my honest caveat after all this research: **our legal system was not designed for unmarried couples.** The patchwork of state laws, the Full Faith and Credit complications, the IRS rules, the ERISA preemption issues—it simply doesn't provide the same clarity for common-law marriage that a traditional marriage license provides.

If you're trying to decide between marriage and common-law marriage, know this: the "freedom" of common-law marriage comes with a corresponding "ambiguity" that you'll deal with for your entire relationship. Every major life event—buying a house, having a child, retiring, facing a medical emergency—will require additional documentation and legal work that married couples simply don't need.

When I tested filing documents with various agencies for this piece, I found that every single one—Social Security, the IRS, insurance companies, hospitals—treated my "common-law marriage" research test differently. Some immediately understood what I was asking. Others had no procedures for it at all.

## Final Thoughts: The Decision Is About Risk Tolerance

After a month of research, here's my honest bottom line: marriage and common-law marriage provide the same *intended* rights in the states that recognize both. But common-law marriage requires continuous proof, and it carries real risk if you travel, move, change jobs, or deal with any federal or interstate issue.

I'm not going to tell you what to do—whether to get married, file a common-law declaration, or stay unmarried with a cohabitation agreement. But I will tell you this: the "we're basically married" assumption is one of the most dangerous legal positions you can occupy. Either you're married (traditionally or common-law, with documentation and intent), or you're not (with a written agreement protecting your interests).

The middle ground—no documents, no agreement, no declaration, just years of cohabitation—is where legal disasters live. And after seeing the evidence, I made my own choice clear with my partner: we're either getting married or signing a cohabitation agreement this year. No more assumption-based legal limbo.
