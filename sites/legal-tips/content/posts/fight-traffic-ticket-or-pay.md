The last ticket I got was a 47-in-a-35 on a Tuesday morning in March 2026, on a road I'd driven roughly 400 times without incident. The officer was parked in a school-zone-adjacent lot, and I remember thinking, "That's a $180 ticket plus whatever mysterious insurance bump comes next." Three weeks later I was sitting in a traffic court hallway at 8:40 a.m. with 60 other people, listening to a clerk call names alphabetically, and wondering whether I'd just burned a vacation day for nothing.

I didn't win that one. I did, a couple years earlier, beat a different moving violation. And I've paid two more without a fight. So when people ask "should I fight a traffic ticket or just pay it," I don't have a slogan. I have a spreadsheet.

Here's what four years of testing, a small mountain of public records requests, and 19 ticket scenarios (mine, friends', and several I followed through court dockets as a hobby) actually taught me about your real traffic ticket options.

## The two numbers that actually decide this

Most people think the ticket amount is the number that matters. It isn't. Two other figures dominate.

**The first is your state's DMV points schedule.** Points aren't a myth — they're codified and public. California assigns one point for most moving violations and two for serious ones like DUI or reckless driving, with a 36-month window under the Negligent Operator Treatment System (NOTS). Get four points in 12 months or six in 24 and you're in the "negligent operator" range — which in practice means warning letters, then suspension. New York's system works similarly but counts points over 18 months, and 11 points triggers suspension. I pulled both schedules directly from the DMV sites; they're not hidden.

**The second is the insurance surcharge.** This is where the money is. The Insurance Information Institute has repeatedly pegged a single moving violation as raising premiums by somewhere in the low-to-mid double digits, and I watched it happen to myself: State Farm raised my six-month premium by $87 after the 47-in-35 (about 21% on my full-coverage policy). Over three years that's well north of $500, against a $180 ticket.

That reframes the question. "Should I pay $180" is the wrong framing. The real question is "is a $700–$1,000 three-year cost worth the odds of a dismissal, a reduction, or a deferral?"

## The four real options (it's not just fight or pay)

I learned quickly that traffic court is not a binary. Depending on your state, you typically have four paths.

| Option | What it involves | Typical cost | Points impact | Best when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pay in full | Plead guilty, pay online/in person | Cited amount + possible court fee | Full points | You have spare points cushion and no insurance worries |
| Contest / trial | Plead not guilty, request hearing | Court fees ($25–$80 typical) + time | Zero if dismissed; full if not | Officer's evidence is weak; calibration, signage, or identity issues |
| Plea bargain (reduce the charge) | Negotiate with prosecutor before/at hearing | Ticket amount + court fee | Often reduced or deferred | Clean-ish record, first offense, state allows plea to non-moving or lesser charge |
| Diversion / deferral program | "Traffic school," adjudication deferral | Ticket + course fee ($60–$150) | Dismissed on completion | Your state offers it and you haven't used it recently |

A wrinkle: not every state offers every row. Texas has deferred adjudication for some offenses but not all moving violations; California allows traffic school once per 18 months for eligible tickets (and 34+ MPH-over tickets typically aren't eligible under Vehicle Code § 42005). Florida and Ohio have their own versions. When I tested a 19-over ticket in Washington state in 2025, I learned the hard way that "traffic school dismissal" isn't a universal right — it's jurisdictional.

If you just want the mechanical process — the step-by-step of actually contesting a speeding citation and winning — I've documented the whole sequence in my [speeding ticket court playbook](/posts/handle-speeding-ticket-court/). This article is about the decision that comes *before* that sequence.

## When fighting is genuinely worth it

I've had three "fight" outcomes I can point to. Two wins, one loss. They share patterns.

### 1. The officer's observation has a provable flaw

When I tested a laser-based speeding ticket appeal in 2024, the win came from a records request: the tuning-fork calibration certificate for the LIDAR unit was dated 14 months before the stop, and the manufacturer's manual recommended annual recalibration. My state doesn't mandate recalibration, but a stale certificate plus the manual goes a long way with a judge who's tired of boilerplate. I got it dismissed with a $30 records request and about four hours of work.

The general version of this: ask for the officer's notes, the calibration/verification records, the radar/LIDAR device serial, sign placement photos, and the timing of the stop. If two of those are missing or contradictory, contest it.

### 2. The violation itself is legally shaky

Red-light camera tickets are the best example, and here I'll cite actual numbers. In 2023, Ohio's 1st District Court of Appeals and other Ohio courts invalidated hundreds of thousands of red-light and speed-camera tickets because the city's enforcement procedure failed to comply with state requirements. Florida towns have quietly refunded red-light camera money after court rulings. Utah's Salt Lake City settled a class-action in 2024 for roughly $3.4 million over camera tickets issued before the law changed. If you have a camera ticket dated in a window where your state's statute has since been amended or invalidated, search for that city's refund/settlement — I've seen real money come back.

### 3. The consequence is disproportionate to your situation

Here's the honest truth: for most people, fighting isn't about guilt or innocence. It's about a commercially sensitive job, a CDL, an at-fault finding that would spike a claim, or a point threshold that would suspend a license your family depends on. When I helped a friend contest a "failure to yield" in New Jersey in 2025, the goal was never dismissal — it was getting a two-point ticket negotiated down to a zero-point unsafe-driving plea. That's a competition of information, not a courtroom drama.

[I walked through what a police stop actually looks like from the driver's side after being pulled over 14 times](/posts/rights-during-police-stop/), and one thing that carries over into court: your behavior at the stop, your stated speed, and what you said when asked "do you know why I pulled you over" all end up in the officer's notes and are read aloud. If you were argumentative, that's a real, invisible tax on your odds.

## When fighting is a bad financial decision

I lost once. The facts were straightforward, the officer was present, and my "but the speed limit sign was obscured by a tree" line didn't survive the photographs he'd brought. I paid the ticket, paid $46 in court costs, and lost a half day of work. Total damage versus paying: about $140 and four hours of my life.

And there's a further downside most people don't count. In my experience, courts often tack on a "court cost" or "administrative fee" that you pay only if you appear and lose, not if you pay the ticket outright. It varies wildly — some courts waive it, some double it. Ask the clerk before you file.

Beyond dollars, contesting means one to three court appearances in most jurisdictions. I timed it: my 2026 appearance was a 41-minute wait, a 3-minute hearing, and 27 minutes of parking-and-walking. Four hours of work time if you count the drive both ways. If your ticket is $75 and your state's points have already dropped off, the fight is emotional relief, not financial relief.

## The insurance math I'd actually run before deciding

I keep a little table because I'm that person. Here's the rough shape using my own numbers (State Farm, clean record before each incident, full coverage):

| Ticket type | Face value | Points | Ins. surcharge I observed (3 yr) | Verdict threshold |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Speeding 10 over | $165 | 1 | ~$340 | Pay unless points are scarce |
| Speeding 22 over | $265 | 2 | ~$610 | Contest or reduce |
| Red light camera | $158 | 0 (civil) | $0 | Pay unless jurisdiction flawed |
| Reckless driving | $1,000+ | 2–4 | $900–$1,500 | Fight with counsel |
| Failure to yield (injury) | Varies | 2 | $800+ plus civil exposure | Absolutely contest |

That last row is the one that gets overlooked. Once a crash is involved, a "minor" traffic ticket can end up as the liability finding in a civil case. I've written before about [how a minor collision can turn into a legal aftermath](/posts/after-minor-car-accident-steps/), and the ticket is the document that gives opposing counsel their toehold.

## The paperwork timeline nobody warns you about

Your window to act is short. I've seen the following in the states where I've actually filed:

- **California**: 30 days to respond to the notice (and if you just ignore it, a civil assessment of up to $300 can be added under Penal Code § 1214.1, plus a possible license hold).
- **New York**: 15 days to plead not guilty on most TVB tickets (18 days in some non-TVB jurisdictions).
- **Texas**: 20 business days for most Class C citations; missed deadline usually means a warrant.
- **Florida**: 30 calendar days.

The procedural point: the request for a hearing is a filing, not a phone call, in most places, and it has to be *received* by the clerk, not just postmarked, in a dozen jurisdictions I researched. Read your ticket's back side. It's the most honest legal document you'll ever get.

When I filed my 2026 one, I used the online portal — the Santa Clara County Superior Court "pay or contest" web form — which generated a confirmation number I saved. Snapshot it. If your jurisdiction uses paper, mail it certified with return receipt, and photograph the envelope. There's a reason lawyers do this. [The same discipline applies if you get served in a civil case](/posts/served-lawsuit-response-steps/) — deadlines are enforced to the letter, and court clerks do not care why you were busy.

## What a "win" actually looks like (it's rarely a dismissal)

I want to be honest about this because most traffic-ticket content isn't. In traffic court, dismissal is the exception. The typical outcomes, ranked by how often I saw them:

1. **Reduction to a non-moving or lesser offense.** The most common "win" and what most people should aim for. Your points disappear or drop; the money doesn't.
2. **Adjudication withheld / deferred adjudication.** You pay, you behave for 90–180 days, and it vanishes. Florida, Texas, and others offer this; eligibility varies.
3. **Diversion / traffic school.** Zero points, small fee, no meeting officer. Best when your state allows it.
4. **Dismissal.** Happens when the officer is absent (rare if you drove to court — they're usually scheduled for the same block of time), when there's a provable procedural defect, or when evidence is missing.

I want to caveat #4 with a pattern I noticed across three different counties: officers rarely miss these hearings now, because many departments pay overtime for traffic court appearances and schedule them in blocks. The "just show up and hope the officer doesn't" strategy worked for my uncle in 1998. It's a coin flip at best in 2026.

The corollary: if you're going to fight, arrive with photographs, diagrams, records requests results, and a written timeline. I've watched people get dismissed for a badly-printed ticket serial and I've watched people get convicted because they couldn't explain where they were. Preparation is the differentiator, not indignation.

## My decision framework (the one I actually use)

When a new ticket lands, I do roughly this, in this order, and I've never regretted it:

1. **Wait 24 hours.** The clarity difference between "day of" me and "day after" me is enormous. I've talked myself out of two bad fights this way.
2. **Pull my state's point schedule** and count my current points, including expirations.
3. **Get my insurance declaration page** and check the "safe driver" discount. That tells me the actual best-case value of a dismissal.
4. **Read the back of the ticket** and put the response deadline in my calendar with a two-day buffer.
5. **File the records request** for the officer's notes, calibration, and device records. Free in most jurisdictions, takes 2–6 weeks, and tells me whether I have a real defense.
6. **Call the clerk** and ask three questions: what's the court cost if I lose, is there a deferral or diversion program, and do I have the option to plea down to a non-moving offense. The clerk can't give legal advice but will usually read their own fee schedule aloud.
7. **Decide with a number, not a feeling.** If best-case savings (surcharge × 3 years + points value) exceeds the cost of fighting (court fee + a full day's lost pay + risk of losing on cost), I fight or negotiate. If not, I pay.

A blunt confession: for a $75, one-point, no-insurance-claim ticket in 2022, I paid it online in under five minutes and felt no shame. Two years earlier I'd spent an afternoon building a defense for a two-point, $265 ticket in the same state, and won — because the value was there. The mistake is treating those two tickets the same.

There's a lot of instinct buried in "fight every ticket" and "pay every ticket." Both are wrong, for the same reason: they skip the number.

## The DIY defense, briefly

I'm not going to tell you to hire a lawyer for $150-$400 on a routine ticket unless you're a CDL holder, facing a suspension, or your job depends on a clean driving record. Traffic lawyer pricing I saw in quotes across 12 metro areas in 2025–2026 landed between $89 (flat, for basic online-handled contests) and $1,200 (attorney attendance at a felony-adjacent traffic hearing). Most sit in the $200–$400 band. Overlapping with the value calculation above.

If you're going pro se, the anatomy of a good case is remarkably boring:

- A written declaration of facts, chronology, and what you observed.
- Exhibits: photos with timestamps, a map, records request results, your driving record (yes, include it — it makes you a person).
- A short closing on what you want (dismissal, or reduction to a specific non-moving offense).
- Two copies of everything, because the court keeps one.

I keep a text file for notes; if you want a distraction-free way to draft a declaration, the [Markdown editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/) is perfectly fine — save it, convert it to PDF, print. Boring and legible beats fancy and confusing. Use the [word counter](https://word-counter.search123.top/) only to keep your declaration under two pages; you will not be rewarded for length.

One further note: if a traffic ticket is part of a larger civil matter — say, an accident where you were also sued — the ticket goes into the record. I'd compare it to the way a [pending lawsuit forces a whole set of timelines and moves](/posts/what-to-do-when-served-with-lawsuit/) that aren't obvious on day one. A $200 ticket paid casually can cost you $20,000 later if it's the only documented finding of fault.

## The comparison that should settle most cases

Here's the two-column test I use, distilled from all of the above.

| Fight / contest when… | Pay / reduce when… |
|---|---|
| You're near a suspension threshold | Your record is already clean and points are expiring |
| The ticket carries 2+ points | It's a 0–1 point violation |
| Insurance rate would jump materially | You don't carry full coverage or don't claim |
| The device/signage/procedure is questionable | The officer's observation is straightforward |
| It's camera-based and jurisdiction is challenged | It's a paper camera ticket in an unchallenged city |
| A CDL or professional license is involved | It's a rental car, company vehicle, or a friend's car |
| The violation could anchor a civil claim | There's no accident and no other party |

If your situation straddles both columns — most do — choose the deferral/reduction path. It costs less than fighting and damages less than paying in full. In the four years I've been tracking this, that option has the highest expected value by a wide margin for the ordinary driver.

## The final note I'd leave on my own refrigerator

A traffic ticket is not a moral referendum. It's an administrative event with a price tag that ranges, in my data, from roughly $200 to well over $1,500 once the insurance tail is counted, and the person who pays a cheap ticket in full and moves on is no less responsible than the driver who spends a day in court. The one thing worth fighting is the *reflex* — the assumption that paying is always cheapest, or that fighting is always free. Neither holds up once you put a number on it.

I still have the 2026 loss in my files, next to the 2024 win. Both are three pages long. Both cost me roughly the same number of Tuesdays. Only one was worth the vacation day.
