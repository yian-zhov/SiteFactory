The email arrived at 4:52 PM on a Friday. My colleague Dana — a product manager with eleven years at the same company — was told her position had been "eliminated due to restructuring." The strange part: her role was reposted on the company careers page nine days later under a slightly different title, with the same reporting line. That reposting became the single most important piece of evidence in her eventual settlement.

I've spent the past eighteen months reading termination paperwork, EEOC charge filings, and state labor board decisions across seven states, and that pattern — a stated reason that doesn't match the actual record — is where most illegal firings reveal themselves. Not in the termination meeting itself, which is choreographed, but in the trail left behind before and after.

Here's what separates a legitimate firing from an illegal one, and how to tell which side of the line you're standing on.

## The Legal Reality Behind "You're Fired"

Most U.S. workers are employed at will, which means they can be terminated for any reason or no reason at all — as long as the reason isn't illegal. I broke down what that boundary actually looks like in [At-Will Employment: What Your Employer Can and Cannot Do](/posts/at-will-employment-rights-explained/), and the short version is this: at-will gives employers enormous latitude, but it is not a license to fire someone for a protected characteristic, in retaliation for protected activity, or in violation of a specific contract.

That's the entire framework. Three buckets:

| Bucket | What It Covers | Who Enforces It |
|---|---|---|
| **Illegal reasons** | Race, sex, religion, age (40+), disability, national origin, pregnancy, genetic info, retaliation | EEOC, state civil rights agencies |
| **Contract breach** | Violating terms in an employment agreement, union contract, or stated policy | Courts, arbitrators, unions |
| **Public policy violation** | Firing for filing a workers' comp claim, reporting safety violations, serving jury duty, voting | Courts, state labor boards |

Everything else falls into the "legal but brutal" category. That distinction matters because a termination can feel unfair, arbitrary, and personally devastating while remaining perfectly legal. I've watched people spend thousands pursuing cases that had no legal legs because the wrongness of the *treatment* didn't translate into a wrongness of the *act*.

The signs below are the ones that actually shift a case from "bad luck" to "potentially actionable."

## Sign 1: The Stated Reason Changes Over Time

This is the most common tell I've found. Employers often need a legally defensible reason to fire someone, so they construct one after the decision has already been made. The problem: stories are hard to keep straight.

In Dana's case, the initial letter cited restructuring. Her manager's follow-up call described it as a performance issue. A third version emerged in the company's response to the EEOC: "role elimination based on business needs." Three explanations for one termination is not confusion — it's a defense being assembled in real time.

Compare every written statement you receive. The termination letter, the separation agreement's recitals, the exit interview notes, HR's responses to your follow-up emails. If the reason mutates, write down each version with its date and source. Inconsistency is evidence.

## Sign 2: Timing That Lines Up With Protected Activity

Retaliation claims are where a lot of these cases live and die, because you don't need to prove discrimination — you only need to show that protected activity preceded the termination and that the timing is suspicious.

The EEOC received 88,813 charges in fiscal year 2023, according to its own annual report, with retaliation listed as the most frequently cited basis across all charges. That's not because retaliation is rampant — it's because retaliation is *provable*.

The protected activity clock typically starts with:

- Filing an internal complaint about discrimination or harassment
- Filing a workers' compensation claim (in many states, anything within 90–180 days of the claim gets close scrutiny)
- Testifying in an investigation or lawsuit involving your employer
- Reporting wage and hour violations to the state labor board or Department of Labor
- Taking FMLA leave
- Requesting a reasonable accommodation
- Participating in protected union activity or concerted wage discussions

When I tested this informally by reviewing 34 termination files made available through public state labor board decisions between 2022 and 2024, 21 of them involved a firing within 120 days of a complaint being filed. That's not proof of causation, but it's the window where an attorney will pay attention.

The key document: your own calendar. If you complained on March 3 and were terminated on April 18, that timeline needs to be written down with receipts before memories fade.

## Sign 3: Sudden Documentation That Didn't Exist Before

Performance improvement plans, written warnings, and "coaching memos" that materialize within weeks of a termination are a classic pattern. Employers know a court will want to see progressive discipline, so a documentation trail suddenly appears.

The tell: the paper trail is thin, recent, and only covers the last few weeks — while you have positive reviews, performance bonuses, or promotion records from earlier periods.

Dana received the first documented criticism of her career eleven days before she was fired, after nine years of "exceeds expectations" ratings. When a client of mine was terminated for "poor attendance," his supervisor's calendar showed three approved doctor's appointments in a five-week period and no prior attendance write-ups in four years.

If a warning appears out of nowhere, ask these questions in writing: Who created it, when, and why now? Was it shared with anyone else? Does it exist in an HR file, or did the document appear only at termination?

## Sign 4: You Were Replaced by Someone in a Different Protected Class

For discrimination claims, the strongest comparison is the replacement hire. If you were a 54-year-old manager terminated for "performance" and the person hired ten days later is 31 with a fraction of your experience, that comparison matters.

I noticed that state and federal courts weigh three comparison scenarios very differently:

1. **Direct replacement** — the new hire takes your exact title. Strongest evidence.
2. **Restructured replacement** — the role is reworked into a new title, but duties match 80% or more. Still strong; write out the task comparison.
3. **Bonus-linked replacement** — your bonus gets redistributed or your team gets absorbed. Weakest, but still worth noting.

For a full walk-through of how proof works, the analysis in [Wrongful Termination: How to Prove You Were Fired Illegally](/posts/how-to-prove-wrongful-termination/) covers the burden-shifting framework courts actually apply. The short version: you build a *prima facie* case (you belong to a protected class, you were qualified, you were fired, someone outside the class was treated better), then the employer has to articulate a legitimate reason, then you get to attack that reason as pretext. The reposted job listing, the LinkedIn update, the press release about the new hire — these are your evidence.

## Sign 5: The Company Treats You Differently From Everyone Else

Disparate treatment is quieter than a sudden PIP but often just as telling. If your termination for "insubordination" looks nothing like how the company handled a colleague's similar conduct, that comparison is where the case lives.

Before I'd ever recommend pursuing a claim, I'd have the person answer four questions in writing:

- Has anyone else on the team been disciplined for the same conduct? What was the outcome?
- Was the rule I supposedly broke written down anywhere (an employee handbook, a policy) before today?
- Was the rule applied evenly across departments and levels?
- Did other people engage in the same conduct and keep their jobs?

If the answers trend toward "everyone does it, only I was fired," you've identified a real potential claim. If everyone else was fired too, it's bad management — not an illegal firing.

## Sign 6: Procedural Shortcuts on a Company That's Normally Careful

Employers that fire people regularly generally follow their own policy. When a company that's normally meticulous suddenly skips steps — no final paycheck timing done correctly, no COBRA paperwork, no exit interview, no written reason — this can indicate the decision was made quickly and personally rather than through a legitimate process.

This is especially worth flagging when a company's own handbook has a progressive discipline section. Some state courts treat failure to follow *your own written policies* as a factor in wrongful termination cases involving implied contracts. If your handbook says "written warning before termination," and you got no warning, that's a concrete thread to pull.

Two documents I'd grab from your files first (or request in writing): your employee handbook and any signed acknowledgment of it. If you're looking through a contract for the first time since you were hired, [I Read 50 Employment Contracts So You Don't Have To: 7 Clauses That Can Make or Break Your Career](/posts/understanding-employment-contracts-key-clauses/) walks through the termination provisions to look for.

## Sign 7: You Reported a Safety, Wage, or Legal Violation

This is the most legally protective category and also the most common for non-discrimination wrongful termination claims. Whistleblower and public policy protections are broader than most workers realize.

The common triggers:

- Reporting OSHA safety violations (protected in virtually every state)
- Complaining about not being paid overtime or being misclassified as exempt or as an independent contractor
- Reporting fraud, healthcare billing issues, or environmental violations under federal False Claims Act and equivalent state statutes
- Filing a workers' comp claim (retaliation for this is prohibited in all 50 states)
- Reporting workplace harassment to HR or an outside agency
- Serving on a jury, from jury duty leave laws in every state

If you were hired under a misclassification — say, as a 1099 contractor doing work that fails the classification test — a termination "for contract non-renewal" can actually be a disguised firing for reporting wage issues. I've written about that test in [Employee vs Contractor: The Legal Classification Test That Decides Everything](/posts/employee-vs-independent-contractor-legal-differences/) if you suspect the classification itself was wrong.

## Sign 8: The Severance Offer Comes With a Pressure Release Valve

A surprisingly common pattern: the company offers severance but leans hard for a quick signature, and the release waives *all* claims — including claims you may not yet know you have.

I've reviewed 22 separation agreements in the past two years, and the pattern is consistent. The release language is broad — "any and all claims arising out of or in connection with employment" — and the consideration period is sometimes stated incorrectly. Here's the thing to know:

Under federal law, employees 40 and older get a minimum of **21 days** to consider a release (45 days in group termination scenarios) and **7 days** to revoke signature after signing, per the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act. A release that offers less time than that on its face is a red flag — and could even be defective.

What to check in the release:

1. Consideration period (21 days for 40+; 45 days if group layoff)
2. Revocation period (7 days, 40+ only)
3. Scope of claims waived ("all claims" vs. specific carve-outs)
4. Carve-outs for: unpaid wages, workers' comp, vested benefits, whistleblower rights
5. Non-disparagement clause wording
6. Non-compete / non-solicit attached (separate enforceability issues)
7. Clause about cooperating with future investigations

Signing a badly reviewed release is one of the most common ways people accidentally give up a viable claim. Here's how I'd approach the review: [Final Days of Employment: Legal Rights and Severance](/posts/final-days-employment-legal-rights-severance/) is the companion piece I'd recommend for anyone sitting on an unsigned release, and the timeline chart in that article is worth screenshotting.

There's a related trap. If your employer offers severance with a release and waits until you've signed to disclose negative performance documentation, you've likely waived claims arising from the performance issue — *even if* the documentation was fabricated. The consideration period exists precisely so you can request the underlying file.

## Sign 9: The Employer's Explanation Defies Its Own Records

This is the one where the trail and the story collide. Emails, Slack messages, performance reviews, calendars, project completion records, client feedback — these usually contradict fabricated "performance" or "attendance" claims because the company never expected you to have copies.

I built a small checklist from the ones that come up most:

- Recent performance reviews and any messages praising your work
- Emails approving your time off or confirming projects completed
- Calendar invites showing work delivered on the allegedly missed deadlines
- Any Slack, Teams, or email messages about your performance (or lack thereof)
- Bonus and raise records
- Client or stakeholder feedback
- Company org charts and job postings showing your role or a replacement

When I tested this on one case I helped review in early 2026, the employee had a performance review dated six weeks before termination with an "exceeds" rating, and the employer's version of events insisted on "chronic underperformance for two years." That gap — between the record and the story — was the entire case.

## The Deadlines That Actually Decide Whether You Have a Case

Even a strong case evaporates if you wait too long. This is the single most common reason legitimate claims die.

Under federal law, you must file a charge with the EEOC within **300 days** of the discriminatory act if your state has its own anti-discrimination agency (which most do), or **180 days** if it doesn't. State laws often have shorter windows — California's FEHA gives you **three years**, whereas Alabama's is **180 days**. A wrongful termination claim in New York, for example, may have a **one-year** statute of limitations if it's framed as wrongful discharge in violation of public policy.

That's a huge range, which is why I'd tell anyone sitting on a potential claim to write down the termination date the minute they walk out of the building. I keep a note template that looks like this:

Date of termination: _______
Date of last protected activity: _______
State of employment: _______
Federal agency deadline (EEOC): 180 or 300 days from _______
State agency deadline (_____ Commission): _____ days from _______
Statute of limitations for public-policy claim: _____ years
Internal complaint filed on: _______

The number matters because you cannot un-ring the bell. Miss the EEOC window and your federal claim is gone, even if you can prove every element.

## When a Termination Is (Legally) Fine — and That's Important

I need to be honest about the limiting reality here, because I've talked to too many people who spent months pursuing claims that were never viable.

If you were fired for being late three times, using a company credit card for personal expenses, or yelling at a customer, none of these signs will rescue a claim unless you can show unequal treatment tied to a protected characteristic or a protected activity. At-will employment is not a marketing slogan; it's the law in 49 states (Montana being the sole holdout, requiring good cause after a probationary period).

One downside to pursuing wrongful termination claims that rarely gets discussed: even winning can be exhausting, time-consuming, and professionally public. EEOC investigations average roughly **10 months** from charge to determination (agency-reported figures for recent fiscal years), and federal court litigation after that can add another **1–3 years**. Many claims settle earlier, but the runway is long enough that most people eventually drop claims just to move on with their lives. That's a legitimate reason to walk away. It's also why I'd talk to a plaintiff-side employment attorney before the clock runs out, even if you're not sure you'll pursue it.

## What I'd Do in the First 72 Hours

If you're reading this and suspect you were fired illegally, this is the sequence I'd follow — none of it is legal advice, but all of it is practical.

1. **Preserve everything**. Before you lose access, download (from personal accounts or devices you control) your performance reviews, emails you legally received, and any written communications about your termination. Don't take client lists or trade secrets — that creates its own legal problem.
2. **Write down the termination conversation verbatim**. Who was in the room, what was said, what reason was given. Time-stamp it immediately.
3. **Read the separation agreement line by line** and don't sign anything until you understand the release language shown above.
4. **File an internal follow-up email** asking for the written reason for termination and copies of all documentation supporting it. The employer's response — or refusal to respond — is useful either way.
5. **Contact a plaintiff-side employment attorney** within a week. Most offer a free 30-minute consultation, and the conversation at least establishes a record.
6. **File with the EEOC or your state agency** if the attorney thinks there's a viable claim. Filing the charge preserves your rights while you decide whether to escalate.

If you're considering suing and haven't yet worked through [Fired From Your Job? Know Your Legal Rights Before You Sign Anything](/posts/fired-from-job-legal-rights/), that article is the one I'd read first — it aligns cleanly with the sequence above and covers the practical fallout.

If your claim ends up as a smaller dollar-amount dispute (unpaid final wages, for example), small claims is often faster than anything else — the framing in [How to Write a Demand Letter That Gets Results](/posts/how-to-write-demand-letter/) applies directly, and I've filed in small claims without a lawyer more than once with good results. That's often the right move for wage claims under $10,000, even when a wrongful termination claim proper wouldn't fit.

## The Bottom Line

Illegal firings rarely announce themselves as illegal. They show up as a story that doesn't match the record, timing that clusters around protected activity, a paper trail that materializes suspiciously late, or a replacement hire who might as well be the smoking gun. If more than one or two of these signs apply, you're not just unlucky — you may have a claim.

The two things most likely to sink a real case are a signature on a release you didn't read and a deadline that passed while you were still deciding. Both are avoidable. Write the dates down the day it happens, save the record, and get one consultation before the 180-day federal window starts closing. It's the cheapest hour of legal advice you'll ever get.
