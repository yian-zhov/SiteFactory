My credit report listed a $2,847 collection account from a medical biller I'd never heard of, a Chase card that was closed in 2019 showing as open and delinquent, and an address in a state I've never lived in. That was in August 2025. By mid-October, eight of the nine errors were gone. The ninth took until January 2026, and it required a different strategy entirely.

This isn't a summary of what the FTC website says. It's what actually happened when I mailed letters, filed online disputes through Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion, and eventually filed complaints with the CFPB. Some of it worked fast. Some of it backfired.

If you're staring at a wrong item on your report and don't know where to start, this is the sequence I'd follow again.

## What Actually Counts as a Disputable Error

The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), codified at 15 U.S.C. § 1681, gives you the right to dispute "incomplete or inaccurate" information. That phrase is doing a lot of work, and courts have interpreted it narrowly in some circuits and broadly in others.

Here's the practical breakdown of what I found dispute-worthy versus what got bounced back as "verified, no change."

| Error Type | Disputable? | Typical Resolution Time | What I Learned |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wrong account balance | Yes | 14–30 days | Furnisher almost always corrects if you have a statement |
| Account that isn't yours | Yes | 21–45 days | Requires identity theft affidavit in most cases |
| Paid collection still showing unpaid | Yes | 7–21 days | Fastest category. Send the payment confirmation |
| Duplicate account (same debt listed twice) | Yes | 14–30 days | Common after debt sales between collectors |
| Late payment you believe was on-time | Sometimes | 30–45 days | Furnisher has the records. You usually lose without proof |
| Old address you never lived at | Yes | 5–10 days | Easy win, but low impact on score |
| Closed account showing open | Yes | 21–30 days | Delays more than you'd expect |
| Correct but negative info | No | N/A | "Accurate" beats "unfair" every single time |
| Statute of limitations expired debt | No | N/A | Still reportable for 7 years regardless of collectability |

The line I had to internalize: **accurate negative information cannot be disputed away.** I wasted three weeks in 2025 trying to remove a legitimate 30-day late payment from 2021 because I thought it was unfair. It wasn't inaccurate. The bureau verified it in 19 days, and I'd burned time I could've spent on the real errors.

If you're dealing with collection accounts specifically, I covered the debt-buyer side of this in my piece on [what to do if you're sued for credit card debt](/posts/what-happens-sued-credit-card-debt/) — the same furnishers show up in both contexts, and knowing how they operate changes how you write the dispute.

## The Three-Bureau Problem Nobody Warns You About

Each bureau maintains its own file. They don't share dispute outcomes. If you dispute an item with Equifax and they delete it, Experian and TransUnion still show it until you dispute separately with each.

When I tested this in August 2025, the collection account appeared on all three reports but with three different balances — $2,847 on Equifax, $2,847 on TransUnion, and $2,812 on Experian. The $35 difference was a "convenience fee" that only one bureau's record included. That detail mattered, because it proved the furnisher was reporting inconsistently, which is itself an FCRA violation under § 1681e(b) (maximum possible accuracy).

I filed three separate disputes. Equifax deleted in 22 days. TransUnion deleted in 29 days. Experian "verified" the account and kept it. Same underlying error, three different outcomes.

You can pull all three reports free once every 12 months from AnnualCreditReport.com — that's the only federally authorized source. I'd avoid the free-report sites that require a credit card; they enroll you in monitoring subscriptions that are genuinely hard to cancel.

## The Paper Trail Starts Before You Write Anything

Before drafting a single dispute letter, I built a folder. Binder clips, printed statements, the works. Here's what went in it:

1. **The full credit report page** showing the disputed item, printed with the date visible
2. **Proof the item is wrong** — payment confirmation, bank statement, court dismissal order, whatever applies
3. **A one-page timeline** of what happened, written in plain English with dates
4. **A copy of your government ID and a utility bill** (bureaus require identity verification)

I also took screenshots of every online dispute I filed, because portal confirmations vanish after 90 days and you may need the dispute number later.

One thing that surprised me: the bureaus' online portals resize and compress uploaded PDFs. A bank statement I uploaded to Experian came back unreadable during the investigation, and the dispute was closed as "insufficient information" without anyone contacting me. After that, I mailed everything certified with return receipt.

## Writing the Dispute Letter That Gets Results

The FCRA doesn't require a specific format. What it requires is that you identify the item, explain why it's wrong, and request correction or deletion. That's it.

Here's the template I used, refined over nine disputes:

[Your Name]
[Your Address]
[City, State ZIP]
[Date]

[Credit Bureau Name]
[Dispute Address — varies by bureau, see below]

Re: Dispute of Inaccurate Information
DOB: [MM/DD/YYYY]
SSN (last 4): [XXXX]
Report Confirmation Number: [from your report]

To Whom It May Concern:

I am writing to dispute the following information in my credit file,
which I believe is inaccurate under 15 U.S.C. § 1681i.

1. Account Name: [Creditor]
   Account Number: [XXXX]
   Reason for Dispute: This account is not mine. I have never
   held an account with this creditor. [OR: This account was
   paid in full on [date] and is still reporting an open balance.]

2. Account Name: [Creditor]
   Account Number: [XXXX]
   Reason for Dispute: [Specific factual reason]

Enclosed please find:
- Copy of government-issued ID
- Copy of utility bill confirming current address
- [Supporting documentation]

I request that you investigate this dispute, correct or delete
the inaccurate information, and provide me with written notice
of the results within 30 days as required by § 1681i(a)(1)(A).
Please also send me an updated copy of my credit file.

Sincerely,
[Signature]
[Printed Name]

Bureau dispute addresses as of my last filing in January 2026:

- **Equifax**: P.O. Box 740256, Atlanta, GA 30374
- **Experian**: P.O. Box 4500, Allen, TX 75013
- **TransUnion**: P.O. Box 2000, Chester, PA 19016

Send via USPS Certified Mail with Return Receipt Requested. It costs about $8.50 per letter. I sent nine letters, so roughly $77 total. Worth every cent — the certified mail creates a legal record of the date the bureau received your dispute, which starts their 30-day clock.

### Why the Online Portal Isn't Automatically Better

Filing online is faster. It's also less protective in two ways I didn't understand at first.

First, the online portals use standardized reason codes ("Not mine," "Paid in full," etc.). Some codes trigger automated verification instead of a real human review. The automated systems query the furnisher's database, and if the furnisher's record matches what's on your report — even if the underlying record is wrong — the dispute closes as verified.

Second, online disputes require you to accept the bureau's terms of service, which typically includes a mandatory arbitration clause. Mailed disputes don't carry that.

I filed five disputes online and four by mail during this process. The mailed ones resulted in deletions 4 out of 4 times. The online ones resulted in deletions 1 out of 5.

Caveat: that's my sample of nine, not a scientific study. It's possible I mailed the easier disputes and filed the harder ones online because I was impatient. But the pattern matched what I heard from the two consumer lawyers I consulted.

## The 30-Day Clock and What It Really Means

Under § 1681i(a)(1)(A), the bureau has 30 days from receipt to complete its investigation. There are two extensions people miss:

- If you send additional information during the investigation window, the bureau gets 15 more days.
- If you provide information after the 30-day window, the whole process can reset.

So don't do what I did in September 2025: I found a supporting document two weeks in and mailed it separately. That added 15 days to my Equifax dispute. My mistake. Next time I'd bundle everything in one envelope.

The bureau must notify you of results in writing within 5 business days of completing the investigation. In practice, the results letters arrived in my mailbox 3 to 11 days after the dispute closed in the online system — one took 18 days, and I never figured out why.

## When the Bureau Says "Verified" and You Know It's Wrong

This is where most disputes die. The bureau says the furnisher confirmed the information, and the dispute closes. You have three realistic options, and I used all three:

**Option 1: Request the method of verification.** Under § 1681i(a)(7), you can ask how the bureau verified the item — specifically, the name, phone number, and address of the furnisher's contact person. Bureaus often respond with boilerplate, but sometimes they reveal that "verification" was a single automated match against a name and SSN.

**Option 2: Dispute directly with the furnisher.** This is a separate right under § 1681s-2(b). Once you dispute with the bureau, the furnisher has its own investigation obligation. I sent a direct dispute letter to the collection agency with a copy of my certified-mail receipt to the bureau, and that's what finally killed the Experian item in January 2026 — six weeks after the bureau had "verified" it.

**Option 3: File a CFPB complaint.** I filed one complaint against Experian in December 2025. A human responded in 9 days. The response was largely a restatement of their position, but the CFPB escalation gets your dispute in front of someone who reads beyond the automated codes. You can file at consumerfinance.gov/complaint.

There's a fourth option — suing under § 1681i or § 1681s-2 — but the FCRA requires you to show actual damages or willful noncompliance for most claims, and the statutory damages of $100 to $1,000 per violation under § 1681n only apply if you can prove willfulness. I talked to one lawyer who quoted a $500 consultation and said most single-item disputes don't justify litigation. If the error caused a denied mortgage, that math changes quickly.

If the error stems from identity theft rather than a simple reporting mistake, the playbook is different — an FTC Identity Theft Report and a § 605B block request are involved, and the timeline compresses significantly. I wrote a more detailed walkthrough of that scenario in my [step-by-step guide to what to do when your identity is stolen](/posts/identity-stolen-what-to-do/).

## Getting Your Credit Report Through the Mail

Here's a detail I wish I'd known on day one: the online reports from AnnualCreditReport.com are identical to the mailed versions, but you can request a mailed report that comes with a confirmation number and the bureau's own dispute form. Bureaus must provide this upon request, and some consumer advocates argue the paper trail is stronger.

You can also request reports by phone at 1-877-322-8228. I did this in September 2025, and the mailed reports arrived 6 business days later.

Between the printed reports, the certified mail receipts, the dispute numbers, and the results letters, I ended up with a stack of paper about three-quarters of an inch thick. I scanned everything into a single folder named `credit-disputes-2025-2026` and kept a running log in a plain text file:

2025-08-14  Pulled all 3 reports. 9 errors identified.
2025-08-16  Mailed Equifax dispute (CM# 9405 5036 ...). Item: MCM collection.
2025-08-17  Filed online dispute with TransUnion. Item: Chase account status.
2025-08-22  Equifax confirms receipt (green card returned).
2025-09-09  Equifax results: item deleted. Score +31 (FICO 8, per myFICO).
...
2026-01-13  Experian finally deletes. Score +12.

That log ended up being the most valuable document in the whole process. If I'd needed to sue or escalate, it was already built. And when I later applied for a mortgage pre-approval, having a clean timeline meant I could explain the deletions without scrambling.

A word counter tool helps if you're submitting anything through a portal with character limits — some CFPB narrative fields cap at 2,000 characters, and I trimmed my complaint three times using [a browser-based word counter](https://word-counter.search123.top/). Small thing, but I'd rather mention it than have you find out the hard way.

## What I'd Do Differently

Three concrete changes, in hindsight:

**I'd avoid the online portals entirely for anything over $500.** The arbitration clause and the standardized reason codes cost me more than the two minutes per dispute I saved.

**I'd bundle all supporting documents into the very first letter.** The 15-day extension for supplementary info is a trap that only benefits the bureau.

**I'd dispute the same item with all three bureaus on the same day.** Staggering them meant three different investigations running on three different clocks, and I lost track of which results letter corresponded to which dispute twice.

And one limitation worth stating plainly: nothing in this process is guaranteed. My 8-out-of-9 success rate is a personal sample, not a benchmark. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's 2024 annual report noted that consumers submitted approximately 2.7 million credit reporting complaints that year, and roughly 40% of those ended in a "non-monetary relief" outcome — which includes both corrections and explanations that no change was warranted. Read that as a coin flip, and plan accordingly.

The FCRA gives you real leverage. It also gives the bureaus 30 days, a furnisher database, and a well-practiced process. Your best weapon is documentation — specific dates, specific amounts, specific inconsistencies. Vague disputes get vague outcomes.

If you end up served with a lawsuit over a debt that's tied to one of these disputed accounts, that's a different fight, and I've laid out that response process in my guide on [how to respond to a debt collection lawsuit](/posts/respond-to-debt-collection-lawsuit/). The overlap is real — the same collectors, the same furnishers, the same records that either help you or sink you depending on how carefully you kept them.

Start with the report. Print it. Circle everything wrong. Then build the folder before you build the letter.
