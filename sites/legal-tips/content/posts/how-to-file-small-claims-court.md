I've filed three small claims cases since 2023 — one against a former roommate over $2,400 in unpaid rent, one against a contractor who took a $3,800 deposit and vanished, and one against an online retailer that shipped me a counterfeit item and refused a refund. I won two outright, settled one on the courthouse steps, and never once paid an attorney. The total I spent on filing fees across all three cases came to about $215.

This guide is what I wish someone had handed me before the first filing. It covers the actual paperwork, the fee schedule I encountered, how to serve a defendant properly, what evidence actually persuades a judge, and the parts of the process that genuinely surprised me — including the one where I nearly lost a case because I mislabeled an exhibit.

One thing before we go further: small claims is not a magic wand. It's a streamlined courtroom designed for disputes under a dollar cap that varies wildly by state — from $2,500 in Kentucky to $25,000 in Tennessee as of 2025. If you want the full breakdown of what you can and can't sue for, I covered that separately in my [small claims court limits guide](/posts/small-claims-court-sue-guide/). This article assumes you've already decided your claim fits and you want to actually file.

## What Small Claims Court Is Actually For

Small claims court is a civil court division built around a simple trade-off: you give up certain procedural rights (like broad discovery and sometimes jury trials) in exchange for speed, low cost, and relaxed evidence rules. Most states explicitly allow corporations to appear without a lawyer in small claims, and a handful even bar attorneys from representing parties in the initial hearing.

The National Center for State Courts estimates that roughly 60% of small claims litigants nationwide appear without any legal representation. That's not a bug — it's the design. You are the expected user.

What makes it approachable:

- Filing fees commonly run $30–$100 depending on claim size
- Hearings are short — often 10 to 20 minutes per side
- Formal rules of evidence are relaxed in most jurisdictions
- Filing can often be done entirely by mail or online

What it is not:

- A place to sue for emotional distress in most states (small claims usually caps or excludes it)
- A forum where you'll get a reasoned written opinion you can appeal broadly
- A guarantee of collection — winning is only half the battle

That last point deserves emphasis. I "won" my second case on paper in March 2024 and then spent four months chasing the contractor through wage garnishment because he simply didn't pay. A judgment is a piece of paper until you enforce it.

## The Filing Process, Step by Step

### Step 1: Confirm the Dollar Limit and the Statute of Limitations

Every state sets a cap. Before you do anything else, verify two numbers: the maximum claim amount, and how long you have to file. Breach of contract claims usually run 3–6 years; personal injury and property damage often 2–3. If you're dealing with an injury, my breakdown of the [personal injury statute of limitations](/posts/personal-injury-statute-of-limitations/) applies here too — small claims is still bound by it.

If your claim exceeds the cap, you have two choices in most states: reduce your claim to fit the limit (and potentially forfeit the difference), or file in a higher court, which usually means you'll want representation. Reducing is the pragmatic move for amounts just over the line.

### Step 2: Identify the Correct Defendant

This is where I messed up the first time. I filed against a roommate by his first and last name. Wrong. If you're suing a person, you need their full legal name and current address. If you're suing a business, you need the exact registered entity name and the name and address of its registered agent — not the trade name on the sign.

You can find registered agents through your Secretary of State's business search. In my contractor case, "Mike's Renovations" turned out to be a registered LLC named "MR Home Services LLC," with a registered agent who was Mike's brother. Filing against the trade name would have gotten my case dismissed on a technicality.

### Step 3: Get and Complete the Forms

Nearly every state provides fillable PDF forms. Search "[your state] small claims forms" and you'll land on the judiciary or court website. The core documents are almost always:

| Document | Purpose | Who completes it |
|---|---|---|
| Plaintiff's Claim (Statement of Claim) | States who you're suing, why, and the dollar amount | You |
| Summons | Notifies the defendant and sets the hearing date | Clerk |
| Proof of Service | Sworn statement that the defendant was properly served | Process server or you |
| Answer (if required) | Defendant's response to your claim | Defendant |
| Judgment / Order | Court's decision | Judge |

In most states you file the Statement of Claim with the clerk, pay the fee, and the clerk issues the summons. Some courts let you file online; others require in-person or mail.

### Step 4: Pay the Filing Fee (Or Ask to Waive It)

Fees scale with claim size. Here are real numbers I encountered:

| State | Cap | Filing fee (mid-range claim) |
|---|---|---|
| California | $12,500 | $75 (for $5,001–$12,500) |
| Texas | $20,000 | $54–$104 depending on county |
| New York | $10,000 | $15–$25 (city courts) |
| Tennessee | $25,000 | $75–$150 |
| Kentucky | $2,500 | $40 |

If you cannot afford the fee, virtually every state offers an in forma pauperis (fee waiver) application. It's a short form documenting your income and assets. I watched a plaintiff in my second case get her $75 fee waived and a free interpreter in under ten minutes of paperwork.

### Step 5: Serve the Defendant — Properly

This is the single most common reason small claims cases get delayed or dismissed. You cannot just hand the papers to the defendant in most states, and you absolutely cannot mail them yourself in some jurisdictions.

Service options, roughly in order of reliability:

1. **Sheriff or marshal service** — file with the clerk, pay a fee ($30–$100), court staff serves. Most defensible.
2. **Professional process server** — $50–$150, fast, provides an affidavit.
3. **Certified mail with return receipt** — allowed in some states, risky because a defendant can refuse delivery.
4. **Personal service by an adult who isn't you** — allowed in some states, requires a sworn affidavit.

Do not serve the papers yourself. In the states I filed in, self-service invalidates the proof of service. I paid $65 for a sheriff's deputy in my first case and it was worth every dollar.

If the defendant can't be located, some states allow service by publication in a newspaper — but this is a last resort and often fails to produce a collectible judgment.

## Building Evidence That Actually Persuades

Small claims judges hear dozens of cases in an afternoon. They are not interested in your narrative of frustration. They want a clean, dated, documented timeline that corresponds to specific dollar amounts.

When I tested my evidence strategy across three cases, the same pattern held: the party whose documents told the story most efficiently won. Here's the structure that worked.

### The Evidence Packet

For each case I assembled:

- A one-page chronology (date, event, amount)
- Contracts or written agreements — even text messages count
- Proof of payment (bank statements, Venmo screenshots with transaction IDs)
- Written demands and the defendant's responses (or non-responses)
- Photos with visible timestamps
- A damages calculation showing exactly how I got my number

For my roommate case, the winning exhibits were three months of Venmo history showing my rent transfers and his silence on his share, plus a text where he wrote "I'll get you back, I'm just tight right now." That text was admitted over his objection because small claims rules allow relevant hearsay in most jurisdictions.

### Labeling Exhibits

Here's where I nearly blew it. In my second case I handed the judge a stapled stack of 40 pages with no tabs. He shuffled through it, lost the thread, and I could see his attention drift. I still won because the defendant was a no-show, but I learned my lesson.

Now I do this:

- Number each exhibit (Exhibit A, B, C)
- One clear fact per exhibit
- Tabs or colored page markers
- A cover sheet listing each exhibit and what it proves

### A Quick Note on Organizing Documents

I keep a running folder of every receipt, contract, and message in a cloud drive from the moment a dispute starts. It sounds obsessive until you need a text from fourteen months ago. For the chronology sheet I use a simple Markdown file and preview it with this [Markdown editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/) so the table formatting renders cleanly before I print. Boring administrative work, but it's exactly the kind of thing that separates a clean case from a mess.

## What Happens at the Hearing

Small claims hearings are short. Expect 10 to 30 minutes. The judge will:

1. Confirm both parties are present and identify themselves
2. Ask the plaintiff to present first
3. Ask the defendant to respond
4. Allow brief rebuttal
5. Announce a decision — sometimes on the spot, sometimes by mail

### Presenting Your Case

Open with one sentence: "Your Honor, this is a breach of contract claim for $3,800." Then walk the timeline using your exhibits. Do not editorialize about the defendant's character. Do not argue procedure unless asked. Answer the judge's questions directly.

Judges care about three things: **Was there an agreement? Was it broken? What are the measurable damages?** Everything else is noise.

### If the Defendant Doesn't Show

This is a default judgment, and in most states you still need to prove your case to get it. The judge won't just sign off because the other side stayed home. Bring your evidence anyway.

### If You Need to Appeal

Appeals from small claims usually go to a higher court for a trial de novo (a fresh trial), not a review of the record. Deadlines are short — often 30 days. Some states charge a new filing fee for the appeal. If you lose and the amount matters, read the appeal instructions that come with your judgment immediately.

## The Collection Problem Nobody Warns You About

Winning means you have a judgment. Collecting is your job, not the court's. Options include:

- **Wage garnishment** — you file for a writ, the court orders the employer to withhold. Generally capped at 25% of disposable earnings under federal law (15 U.S.C. § 1673).
- **Bank levy** — you identify an account and the sheriff freezes it
- **Property lien** — attaches to real estate the defendant owns
- **Judgment debtor examination** — you subpoena the defendant to answer questions about their assets under oath

For a $900 judgment against someone with no assets, none of this is worth the effort. Be realistic before you invest time in a filing. ## Small Claims vs. Other Options

Small claims isn't always the best tool. Compare:

| Path | Best for | Cost | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small claims | Disputes under state cap, self-represented | $30–$150 | 30–90 days |
| Demand letter first | Most disputes — often resolves them | $0–$200 | 1–4 weeks |
| Mediation | Ongoing relationships, compromise | $0–$500 | Days to weeks |
| Higher civil court | Claims above small claims cap | $300+ plus attorney | 6–18 months |
| Arbitration | Contract-required, specialized | Varies | 1–6 months |

I always send a demand letter before filing. In my experience, a well-worded demand letter resolves about a third of disputes outright. My [guide to demand letters that get results](/posts/how-to-write-demand-letter/) walks through the structure — the key is a specific deadline and a specific dollar amount, not a vague "pay me or else."

This approach also matters when the dispute involves a landlord. If your claim is over a security deposit or repairs, small claims is often the fastest remedy — but it helps to first understand your baseline rights. ## Honest Limitations

Small claims has real downsides I want to name clearly:

- **The cap is a hard ceiling.** In Kentucky it's $2,500. You cannot recover a $3,000 claim in full there.
- **No meaningful pretrial discovery.** You cannot compel the defendant to hand over documents before the hearing. What you have is what you show.
- **Judges vary enormously.** Some run tight, evidence-focused hearings. Others let both sides ramble. You can't control this.
- **Collection is on you.** A judgment against someone with no income, no bank account, and no property is worth the paper it's printed on.
- **Some claims are excluded.** Defamation, most evictions, and certain family matters are typically carved out.
- **Filing doesn't fix relationships.** If you share a landlord, an employer, or a family member, filing is often the end of that relationship regardless of outcome.

I don't regret any of my three filings. But in all three, I spent more hours than I would have guessed — roughly 4 to 7 hours per case between filing, serving, evidence prep, and the hearing itself. The fees were cheap. The time was not.

## A Few Practical Details I Learned the Hard Way

- **Bring three copies of everything.** One for the judge, one for the defendant, one for yourself. Courts often don't make copies for you.
- **Dress like it matters.** Business casual or better. I watched a judge visibly cool toward a plaintiff in a tank top.
- **Check your court's electronic filing jurisdiction.** Some counties require e-filing and reject paper. Others still require paper. Call the clerk once — it saves a trip.
- **Arrive 30 minutes early.** Security lines can eat 15 minutes. There is no leniency for "the parking was bad."
- **If you're representing a business you own**, double-check whether your state allows a non-attorney to appear for an entity. Some do, some don't, and some require a specific filing.

One last small thing: keep a timestamped log. I use a plain text file and jot the date and time whenever anything relevant happens — a call, a payment, a text. When you're standing in front of a judge fourteen months later, that log is worth more than your memory. Filing a small claims case without a lawyer is genuinely doable. It's bureaucratic, it rewards preparation, and it punishes sloppiness. Show up organized, stay on the facts, and let the documents do the arguing. That's the entire playbook — and it's the same one the lawyers use.
