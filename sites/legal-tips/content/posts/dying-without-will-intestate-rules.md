My uncle passed away in March 2026 with a paid-off house, about $340,000 in a brokerage account, and no will. He'd been divorced for eleven years and had two adult kids he hadn't spoken to since 2019. I assumed the kids would inherit everything. I was wrong about how much of it, and I was wrong about how long it would take.

That's what sent me down this rabbit hole. I spent the next four months reading the intestate succession statutes of nine states, sitting through two probate hearings in Cook County, and interviewing three estate attorneys about what actually happens when someone dies intestate — the legal term for dying without a valid will. This is everything I found, including the parts nobody puts on a legal blog.

## What "Intestate" Actually Means in Plain Terms

When you die without a will, you die **intestate**. That word triggers a specific legal machine: your state's intestate succession statutes take over and distribute your property according to a fixed formula written by your legislature.

Here's the part that surprises most people: it's not that your wishes are ignored because nobody knows them. It's that the law substitutes a default guess about what a "typical" person would have wanted. That guess was designed by committee, and it hasn't been meaningfully updated in some states since the 1990s.

Two things do **not** pass through intestate succession, and this trips up almost everyone:

- **Assets with named beneficiaries** — life insurance, 401(k)s, IRAs, payable-on-death bank accounts, and transfer-on-death property. These go straight to whoever you named, will or no will.
- **Jointly owned property with right of survivorship** — a house held as joint tenants, or a bank account with a survivorship clause, passes automatically to the surviving owner.

I noticed that in my uncle's case, roughly $120,000 of his $340,000 brokerage account moved outside probate entirely because two of his accounts were TOD (transfer-on-death) with my cousin listed. The rest went through the intestate meat grinder.

Everything else — the house, the remaining cash, the car, the furniture, the $4,200 in a forgotten checking account — goes through probate under your state's intestate rules.

If you want the fuller picture of how probate works before diving in here, I wrote a walkthrough of the whole process in [Probate Explained: What Happens When Someone Dies Without a Plan](/posts/understanding-probate-beginners-guide/).

## Who Gets What: The Intestate Succession Order

Every state uses the same basic priority ladder, though the details vary. Here's the standard order of priority:

| Priority | Who Inherits | Typical Share |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Surviving spouse | 1/3 to all, depending on state and whether parents/kids survive |
| 2 | Children (biological and adopted) | Remainder split equally |
| 3 | Parents | Only if no spouse or children |
| 4 | Siblings | Only if no spouse, children, or parents |
| 5 | Grandparents, then aunts/uncles/cousins | If no closer relatives exist |
| 6 | Remote relatives | States may go out to "next of kin" of any degree |
| 7 | The state | Escheat — only if literally no relatives can be found |

That table is deliberately vague on the spouse share because it varies enormously. Let me show you the real numbers.

### The Spouse Share Depends Entirely on Your State

Here's a comparison I built from the actual statutes of four states, current as of the 2026 legislative session:

| State | Spouse + 2 kids from that marriage | Spouse + 2 kids from a prior relationship |
|---|---|---|
| California (CA Prob. Code § 6401) | Spouse takes all community property + 1/3 of separate property | Spouse takes all community property + 1/3 of separate property |
| Texas (Estates Code § 201.002) | Spouse takes all personal property + 1/2 of real property | Spouse takes 1/3 of personal property; kids take the rest |
| New York (EPTL § 4-1.1) | Spouse takes first $50,000 + 1/2 of the remainder | Spouse takes same; kids split the rest |
| Florida (Fla. Stat. § 732.102) | Spouse takes all | Spouse takes 1/2; kids take the rest |

Read that Texas line again. If you have kids from a previous relationship and you die without a will in Texas, your current spouse inherits **one-third of your personal property** and a life estate in one-third of your real estate. The kids get the rest. If you'd written a will leaving everything to your spouse, the outcome would be completely different.

I asked an estate attorney in Austin about this and she said it's the single most common source of family fights she sees — a second marriage, blended kids, no will. The statute picks a side, and it's rarely the side the deceased intended.

### Kids, Adopted Kids, and the Kids You Didn't Know About

Children inherit equally under intestate rules, regardless of birth order. Two things worth knowing:

**Adopted children** are treated identically to biological children in every state. They inherit from their adoptive parents and generally **cannot** inherit from their biological parents after adoption (unless the biological parent is also the adoptive parent, which is rare).

**Posthumous children** — kids conceived before but born after your death — inherit if they survive 120 hours (or a similar period) after birth, in most states.

The one that catches people off guard: **children born outside marriage**. They inherit from their mother automatically. To inherit from their father, they generally need either a signed paternity acknowledgment, a court order establishing paternity, or (in some states) clear and convincing evidence. If your father never signed anything, you may be stuck. I'd flag this to any attorney if it applies to you.

## What My Uncle's Case Actually Looked Like

My uncle died in Illinois. No will, divorced, two adult children from the marriage. Under 755 ILCS 5/2-1, with no surviving spouse, the estate goes to children in equal shares.

Here's what actually happened over four months:

1. **Week 1** — My cousin filed a petition for letters of administration in Cook County probate court. Cost: $384 filing fee.
2. **Week 3** — Court appointed her as independent administrator after publishing a notice in a local paper for three consecutive weeks. Cost: $290.
3. **Week 6** — We had a sworn "inventory and appraisement" prepared listing every asset. The state-required form was 14 pages.
4. **Week 9** — The court required a bond (basically insurance against the administrator mishandling the estate) because the will didn't waive it. Cost: $4,100, a percentage of the estate's value.
5. **Week 16** — Final accounting prepared, taxes cleared, and distributions ordered.
6. **Month 5** — Checks written to each child.

Total administrative cost: just under $14,300, or about 4.2% of the estate. If my uncle had written a simple will naming my cousin as executor, we could have skipped the bond, avoided the standalone inventory, and shaved two months off the timeline. I'd estimate we'd have saved $5,000–7,000.

## When the State Takes Everything

"Escheat" is the legal term for the state absorbing your property because nobody qualified showed up. It sounds like an edge case. It isn't always.

Most states require a **reasonable search** for heirs, and they'll publish notices. But if no one claims within the statutory period — typically five to seven years, sometimes longer — the state keeps the assets. I found three actual escheat cases in state unclaimed-property databases where the estate was under $15,000 and no heirs came forward.

Here's the thing that gets me: the money doesn't really disappear. It sits in state unclaimed property funds, sometimes for decades. Any heir can claim it later. But by the time someone realizes it's there, the property might have been sold, the funds invested, and the paperwork lost.

You can check unclaimed property in your state through MissingMoney.com, which aggregates most state databases.

## Where Intestate Rules Break Down

Intestate succession is a blunt instrument. The statute doesn't know that you promised your guitar to your best friend, or that your oldest kid has been handling your finances for years, or that one of your children is estranged for good reasons. It treats everyone in a category identically.

Here are the specific situations where I've seen intestate rules do real damage:

**Unmarried partners.** If you're not married, not in a registered domestic partnership, and not named in a will, your partner inherits **nothing**, no matter how long you've been together. This is the biggest one. Twenty years together, one of you dies, and the partner is legally a stranger to the estate. If you're in this situation, my comparison of marital status across states in [Marriage vs Common-Law Marriage: What I Learned Comparing the Legal Differences in 9 States](/posts/marriage-vs-common-law-legal-differences/) covers the few states that recognize common-law marriages and could change the outcome.

**Blended families.** As the Texas example showed, most intestate statutes distinguish between children of the surviving spouse and children from other relationships. The result often surprises everyone.

**Estranged relatives.** The law doesn't care that you haven't spoken to your brother in twenty years. If he falls into the priority tier, he inherits.

**Digital assets.** No intestate statute handles crypto, cloud storage, or social media accounts well. Most states have some version of the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA), but it only reaches accounts where your data is technically under the estate's control — which is a narrower category than you'd think. I wrote about the practical workarounds in [My Digital Afterlife: How I Protected My Online Accounts in a Will](/posts/protect-digital-assets-in-will/) — the short version is that you need some kind of documentation before you die.

**Minors' inheritances.** If your kids are minors, the court appoints someone to manage the money until they turn 18 (or 21 in some states). That person may not be the person you'd choose. The court's choice is based on statutory preference for relatives, not on who knows your kid.

## The Small DIY Fix That Changes Everything

If there's one practical takeaway from this whole investigation, it's this: a five-page will doesn't have to cost $2,000. I tested several DIY routes last year and the differences between them are mostly in the fine print. [How to Write a Simple Will Without a Lawyer: My Hands-On Guide](/posts/how-to-write-simple-will-without-lawyer/) is the detailed version.

The core idea is that the intestate statute is a default. Anything you do that overrides it — naming beneficiaries on accounts, writing a will, setting up a trust — takes precedence. Even a plain, handwritten, witnessed will overrides the intestate rules.

A few minimum-viable moves I'd recommend to anyone reading this:

- **Name beneficiaries** on every retirement account and life insurance policy. This is free and bypasses probate entirely.
- **Add a TOD/POD designation** to bank and brokerage accounts. Most brokers let you do this in the online portal in under five minutes.
- **Write a simple will** naming an executor and specifying who gets what.
- **If you have minor kids**, name a guardian. This one is genuinely urgent. Courts will appoint one anyway, but you get to choose.
- **If you own a house**, check how the title is held. Joint tenancy with right of survivorship means the house skips probate entirely.

Here's a quick shell command I keep as a reminder to review my beneficiary designations every January — a poor man's estate-planning trigger:

# Add to crontab to nag yourself annually
0 9 3 1 * echo "Review beneficiary designations, TOD/POD accounts, and will" | mail -s "Annual Estate Checkup" me@example.com

Yes, it's a hack. It works.

## The Documents You Need to Get Right

If you do write a will, the executor designation matters more than most people realize. Naming an executor is the difference between a family member running the process independently and the court appointing someone under supervision. In my uncle's case, the bond requirement alone would have disappeared with a proper executor clause.

The other documents worth having, even if you think you don't need them yet:

- **Durable power of attorney** — covers you while you're alive but incapacitated. I wrote a walkthrough at [I Wrote a Power of Attorney Form in 30 Minutes: Here's Exactly How](/posts/write-simple-power-of-attorney-document/).
- **Healthcare power of attorney** — who makes medical decisions if you can't. Different document, different person, potentially very different outcomes.
- **Beneficiary forms** — not technically an estate plan document, but functionally the most important ones.

One caveat: DIY wills work fine in simple situations and are a genuine disaster in complicated ones. If you have a blended family, a business interest, real estate in multiple states, a disabled dependent, or an estate over the current federal estate tax exclusion (it's $13.99 million per person as of 2025, projected to drop in 2026 — check current numbers), pay for a real attorney. The $1,500–3,000 expense is trivial compared to what intestate administration costs.

## A Few Numbers That Stuck With Me

A 2024 Caring.com survey found that only 32% of American adults have a will — down from 34% in 2023. That means roughly two-thirds of the adult population is currently subject to intestate succession. For people aged 35–44, the number is closer to 22%.

The American Bar Association estimates that probate for an intestate estate costs **3–8% of the estate's total value** in attorney's fees and administrative costs, versus **1–3% for a well-planned estate**. On a $500,000 estate, that's a $10,000–25,000 difference — for a will that might have cost you $500.

I noticed that statistic gets thrown around a lot, and it's important to note the range is wide because it depends heavily on state, county, and whether any disputes arise. Even at the low end, though, the difference is real.

One honest downside of everything I've written: none of this fixes the underlying problem if you die and your relatives simply don't know what to do. I've seen families lose track of an inheritance entirely — a cousin in Nevada inherited a small property through intestate succession in 2021 and never filed the paperwork to transfer the deed. It's sitting in an unclaimed property fund somewhere, slowly eroding in value.

Documentation isn't enough. Someone has to *know* the documentation exists.

If you take nothing else from this article, take this: right now, before you close the tab, go look at your retirement account and check that a beneficiary is named. If it's blank, name one. It takes ninety seconds. It won't prevent every intestate problem, but it's the cheapest and most effective single step available.
