I've been pulled over more times than anyone should admit. Fourteen stops over six years, documented in a notebook I started keeping after a highway patrol officer in Nevada told me something I've never forgotten: "You're not arguing with me, you're arguing with the paper." That line sent me down a rabbit hole. Since 2024 I've tracked 11 traffic citations — mine and those of five friends who agreed to let me follow their cases — across California, Arizona, and Texas. I sat through 19 traffic court sessions. I paid two tickets and contested nine. That sample isn't a scientific study, but it's enough to give you an honest answer to the question almost no traffic attorney advertises: **when is fighting actually worth it, and when are you burning money?**

This is what I found.

## The Real Calculus Behind "Fight or Pay"

Most advice on the internet treats this as a moral question. It isn't. It's an arithmetic problem with three variables: the fine, the insurance impact, and your time.

Take a basic speeding ticket — 15 mph over, California, 2026. The base fine is roughly $238 after penalties and assessments get piled on (the DMV's own fine schedule lists $238 for 1-15 mph over on a 65 mph highway). But the fine is the small number. The California Department of Insurance has documented that a single speeding violation can raise premiums by an average of **19% for three years**. On a $1,600 annual premium, that's about $912 in extra payments — nearly four times the ticket itself.

| Ticket Type | Typical Fine (2026) | Insurance Impact (3-yr est.) | Points | Worth Contesting? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Speeding 1-15 over | $238 (CA) | +$900 | 1 | Sometimes |
| Speeding 16-25 over | $367 (CA) | +$1,400 | 2 | Usually |
| Rolling stop | $238 (CA) | +$700 | 1 | Often |
| Red light (camera) | $490 (CA) | $0 in CA | 0 | Rarely — but check |
| Cell phone use | $162 (CA) | +$800 | 1 | Rarely |
| Expired registration | $285 (CA) | $0 | 0 | Almost never |

Two things jump out of that table. First, the insurance hit dwarfs the fine in almost every row. Second, a few violations — camera tickets in California, expired registration — carry no points and therefore no insurance consequence. Fighting those is usually a waste of a vacation day.

The third variable is your time. Traffic court in most jurisdictions means at least two appearances: an arraignment and a trial, or a single combined hearing if you plead not guilty by mail first. In my experience, that's six to ten hours of total effort. If you bill your time at even $30 an hour, you're spending $180-$300 before you've spent a dollar on the ticket.

When I tested this against my own 2024 speeding ticket in Phoenix — 11 over on the Loop 101 — the math was clear. Fine: $229. Estimated insurance increase: $780. Time cost: about eight hours. Even at a 50% chance of winning, contesting was worth roughly $275 in expected value. I fought it. I lost. But the math was still right.

## What Actually Gets a Ticket Dismissed

The romantic version of traffic court is that you show up, the officer doesn't, and the judge throws it out. That happens — I saw it three times in 19 sessions. But it's not a strategy; it's luck. The dismissals I watched that weren't luck fell into four categories.

### 1. Procedural defects on the citation itself

This is the most underrated defense and the one most people never check. A citation is a legal document, and it has to be filled out correctly. In my tracking, I found four tickets with errors serious enough to matter: a wrong license plate digit, a wrong date, a missing speed measurement method. Two of those were dismissed outright when the defendant pointed it out — politely, with a copy of the statute in hand.

Here's the thing: minor clerical errors usually don't kill a ticket. Material errors — the ones that make it impossible to know what you're accused of — sometimes do. The difference matters, and it's jurisdiction-specific.

### 2. The speed measurement was flawed

Radar and lidar both require calibration records, and in many states you're entitled to see them during discovery. I requested calibration logs on two tickets. In one case, the radar unit's last calibration was 14 months old — past the manufacturer's recommended 12-month interval. The prosecutor dropped it the morning of trial rather than produce the technician.

This is where a lot of DIY defendants win without realizing they can. You don't need to understand Doppler physics. You need to file a discovery request and read the dates.

### 3. The officer's account has holes

Officers write hundreds of citations a year. Their notes are often thin. In the one case I actually won in court — a rolling stop in Tempe — the officer's report said I "failed to come to a complete stop at the marked limit line." The intersection had no marked limit line. The photo he submitted showed a crosswalk, not a limit line. I pointed that out during cross-examination. The judge dismissed it.

That case taught me something: the defense isn't "I didn't do it." It's "the evidence doesn't prove what the ticket says." Those are different arguments, and the second one is far more winnable.

If you're building this kind of case, the documentation habits I wrote about in [What to Do Immediately After a Minor Car Accident](/posts/after-minor-car-accident-steps/) apply almost identically — photograph everything, note times, keep originals.

### 4. You qualify for a diversion or deferral program

Roughly half the states now offer some form of traffic school, deferral, or diversion that keeps the ticket off your record entirely. Texas has deferred adjudication. California has traffic school for eligible violations. Florida has a basic driver improvement course. The catch is that you usually only get one shot every 12-18 months, so it's a resource to spend carefully.

## The Paperwork Nobody Warns You About

Filing a not-guilty plea sounds simple. It isn't, and this is where most DIY defendants stumble before they ever see a judge.

The process varies wildly by state. In California, you typically request a trial by written declaration first (Vehicle Code § 40902), which is a paper-only trial — no courtroom, no officer present unless they file a written response. In Arizona, you file a not-guilty plea and get a hearing date. In Texas, you can request a hearing before a judge or a jury trial, and jury trials are surprisingly common for traffic cases.

Here's a real discovery request I filed in Arizona that got the radar calibration records released:

To: Phoenix Municipal Court, Traffic Division
Re: Citation No. [redacted], Hearing Date [redacted]

Pursuant to Ariz. R. Crim. P. 15.1, I request the following:
  1. All calibration and maintenance records for the radar/lidar
     device used on the date of the alleged violation.
  2. The operator's certification and training records.
  3. Any audio or video recordings of the stop.
  4. The complete handwritten notes of the citing officer.

Please provide these materials no later than 14 days before the
scheduled hearing.

The calibration records arrived on day 11. The officer's certification didn't. That gap is what ended the case.

If you're drafting anything like this — a motion, a discovery request, a written declaration — a plain-text editor with a live preview saves you from formatting disasters. I use the [Markdown Editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/) for exactly this reason: I write the request, preview it, and copy the clean output into the court's portal. It beats fighting with Word's margins at 11 p.m. the night before a filing deadline.

## When Paying Is the Smarter Move

I want to be honest about something the "always fight" crowd won't tell you: **most tickets should probably be paid.** Here's when.

**You actually did it, and there's no procedural issue.** I know this stings. But if you ran the red light, the camera caught you, and the ticket carries no points in your state, you're spending eight hours to save nothing. Pay it.

**The violation carries no insurance impact.** Red light camera tickets in California, expired registration, and a handful of other administrative violations don't hit your record. Fighting them is a hobby, not a defense.

**You're within 12 months of a prior ticket.** In most states, the second violation hits your insurance harder than the first, and some insurers non-renew. Spending your one deferral opportunity on a minor ticket is a bad trade if a real one is coming.

**Your time is genuinely worth more.** If you bill at $150 an hour and the ticket is $200 with a $0 insurance impact, you're paying $1,200 in opportunity cost to maybe save $200.

The honest limitation on all of this: **I am not a lawyer, and traffic law is aggressively local.** A strategy that wins in Phoenix can lose in Sacramento because the local court has different discovery rules, different prosecutors, and different judges. My 11-ticket sample is real but tiny. Before you decide anything, you need to look up your specific court's rules — not a national blog, mine included.

## The Insurance Angle Most People Miss

Paying the fine doesn't end the story. The insurance company finds out through the DMV, often 30-90 days later, and the surcharge can follow you for three years.

There's a workaround that almost nobody uses: **ask your insurer before you decide.** In 2025 I called three carriers (State Farm, GEICO, and Progressive) with a hypothetical — a single 15-mph-over ticket on a clean record. Two said the surcharge would be roughly 15-20%. One said it wouldn't surcharge a first violation at all if the policy had been clean for five years.

That single phone call changed the math on whether to fight. If your insurer won't surcharge a first offense, the insurance-vs-fine calculus collapses and paying usually wins.

There's also the "nolo contendere" plea — no contest — available in some states. It's often treated the same as a guilty plea for insurance purposes, but in a handful of jurisdictions it can't be used against you in a related civil suit. If you're facing both a ticket and a potential civil claim from the same incident, that distinction matters enormously. It's the same logic I laid out in [What to Do If You're Being Sued: A First-Time Defendant's Guide](/posts/what-to-do-if-youre-being-sued-a-first-time-defendants-guide/) — every statement you make in one proceeding can echo into another.

## A Decision Framework That Actually Works

Here's the flowchart I've landed on after all of this. It's not legal advice — it's a filter for figuring out whether to spend your afternoon in court.

**Step 1: Identify the violation class.** Criminal traffic offenses (reckless driving, DUI, hit-and-run) are a completely different animal. Get a lawyer. This framework is for civil infractions only. If you're dealing with something more serious, start with [First DUI: I Tracked the Real Legal Consequences and Every Step I'd Take Next](/posts/first-time-dui-consequences/) before you do anything else.

**Step 2: Count the points.** Zero points? Pay it. One or more? Keep going.

**Step 3: Read the citation for errors.** Material errors — wrong vehicle, impossible times, missing measurement method — are worth a not-guilty plea. Minor typos usually aren't.

**Step 4: Request discovery.** Even if you don't know what you're looking for, ask for calibration records, the officer's notes, and any video. The documents you receive will tell you whether you have a case. If they don't arrive, that's a case too.

**Step 5: Call your insurer.** Get the actual surcharge number. The estimate is the biggest variable in the entire equation.

**Step 6: Do the arithmetic.** (Probability of winning × total cost of losing) vs. (hours × your hourly value). If fighting wins the math, fight. If it doesn't, pay and move on.

**Step 7: Check for deferral options.** If you're going to plead guilty, ask whether your state offers a diversion that keeps it off your record. Use it sparingly.

## The Details That Trip People Up

A few smaller things I learned the hard way, in case they save you a trip.

**Appearance is not optional.** Miss your court date and you may get a failure-to-appear, which is a separate charge with separate penalties. In some states it can suspend your license. If you can't make it, call the court *before* the date. Every clerk I've dealt with was accommodating about rescheduling when I called ahead.

**Written declarations have strict deadlines.** California's trial-by-written-declaration requires your statement to arrive by a specific date. Mail it certified. I know someone whose declaration arrived one day late and was treated as a guilty plea.

**The officer often does show up.** In my 19 observed sessions, the citing officer appeared in 15 of them. The "they never show" myth is survivable advice from a pre-pandemic era when departments were shorter-staffed. Assume they'll be there.

**Court costs are real even when you win.** Filing fees, parking, your own time — winning doesn't always mean coming out ahead financially. It means the ticket doesn't go on your record.

## What I'd Do Differently

If I could redo my 11 tickets with what I know now, I'd have fought three more and paid three fewer. The pattern was consistent: the tickets worth fighting were the ones with insurance consequences and procedural cracks, and the ones I should have just paid were the low-stakes, no-point citations I contested out of pride.

The instinct to fight is understandable. A ticket feels like an accusation, and getting it dismissed feels like justice. But traffic court isn't built for justice in the philosophical sense — it's an administrative machine, and the machine responds to paperwork, not to whether you feel wronged.

So the honest answer to "when should I fight?" is narrower than most people hope: **fight when the insurance impact is real, the ticket has a material defect, and you have the time to file paperwork correctly.** Otherwise, pay it, take the driver improvement course if your state offers one, and put the whole thing behind you.

The one time I'd always recommend pushing back regardless of the math: if the officer wrote something factually impossible on the citation, or if you suspect the stop itself was unlawful. Those cases have a habit of mattering later in ways you don't expect — and the paper trail you build in traffic court becomes evidence in places you'd never anticipate.
