# What to Do If Your Identity Is Stolen: The Exact Steps I Tested After a Real Breach

The email landed in my inbox at 6:47 AM on a Tuesday in March. Subject line: "Notice of Data Breach — Action Required." My first instinct was to delete it as phishing. My second instinct, thankfully, was to log into the company portal directly instead of clicking any links.

Turns out it was real. A vendor that processed payroll for a client I'd done freelance work with had been compromised. The breach exposed names, Social Security numbers, and bank routing details for roughly 340,000 people. My information was among them.

What followed was a stressful, confusing, and genuinely expensive journey through the identity theft recovery process. I made mistakes. I wasted hours on hold. I filed paperwork incorrectly the first time. But I also learned exactly what works — and what's a waste of your time.

This guide is the playbook I wish I'd had on that Tuesday morning. I've tested every step since, including calling the three major credit bureaus, filing reports with the FTC and police, and monitoring my credit for six months afterward. I'm sharing the exact scripts I used, the documents I needed, and the traps I fell into.

## First, Take a Breath — But Only for About 15 Minutes

Here's the thing nobody tells you about identity theft: the recovery process is long, bureaucratic, and frequently maddening, but it's almost never a financial catastrophe if you act quickly. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) received over 5.7 million fraud reports in 2025, and their own data shows that most consumers who follow the official recovery steps end up with their accounts restored and their money returned within a few months.

That said, the difference between "inconvenient" and "disastrous" often comes down to how fast you move in the first 48 hours after discovering the theft. So take one deep breath, clear your schedule for the next few hours, and work through these steps in order.

## Step 1: Document Everything — Immediately

Before you call anyone, before you file anything, before you even check your bank accounts, set up a dedicated tracking system. I used a simple spreadsheet (Google Sheets works fine), but I've also seen people use physical binders with tabbed dividers. The format matters less than the consistency.

Here's what you need to track:

- Every date and time you spoke with someone about the theft
- The name and ID number of every representative you talked to
- Every case or report number you receive (FTC report, police report, fraud alerts, disputes)
- Copies of every document you submit (send PDFs to yourself or save them in a secure folder)
- Any financial losses, even small ones, with dates and amounts

When I tested this process, I noticed that having a detailed log made every subsequent step dramatically easier. The credit bureaus, banks, and collection agencies all ask for the same basic information repeatedly. Having it in one place meant I never had to scramble to find a date or case number while on hold.

## Step 2: Get a Fraud Alert on Your Credit File

Now it's time to actually do something. The first concrete action is placing a fraud alert on your credit reports. This tells lenders to verify your identity more carefully before extending credit in your name.

You only need to contact one of the three major credit bureaus — Equifax, Experian, or TransUnion — because they're legally required to share the alert with the other two. I called Experian first because their automated phone system was the least painful to navigate (more on that in a moment).

Your options here:

| Alert Type | Duration | Who Can Place It | Impact on You |
|------------|----------|------------------|---------------|
| Initial Fraud Alert | 90 days (renewable) | Anyone who believes they're a victim of identity theft | Minimal — lenders may call to verify identity |
| Extended Fraud Alert | 7 years | Verified identity theft victims with an FTC report | Minimal — same verification process |
| Active Duty Alert | 1 year | Active-duty military personnel | Minimal |
| Credit Freeze | Until you lift it | Anyone, regardless of theft status | Strongest protection — blocks new credit entirely |

I placed both an initial fraud alert (which I later converted to an extended alert after filing my FTC report) and a credit freeze. The fraud alert is useful for protecting against immediate threats, but the credit freeze is the real protection. More on that in Step 4.

### The Phone Script That Worked

When I called Experian at 1-800-311-4769, here's essentially what I said:

> "I believe I'm a victim of identity theft. I received a data breach notification and I want to place a fraud alert on my credit file and put a freeze on my account. I have my Social Security number, date of birth, and current address ready."

That was it. The representative didn't ask probing questions or make me feel like I was being scrutinized. The whole call took about 14 minutes, including time on hold. I also learned that you can do this entirely online at each bureau's fraud alert page, but I found the phone process more reliable for catching issues faster.

One thing I noticed during my testing: the bureaus' websites have gotten significantly better about fraud alerts since 2022. The online forms are now reasonably intuitive, and you get confirmation almost instantly. But the phone route still has one advantage: you can ask questions and get clarification if something unexpected shows up on your report.

## Step 3: Review Your Credit Reports Carefully

Within the first week, request free copies of your credit reports from all three bureaus. The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) entitles you to one free report from each bureau every 12 months at AnnualCreditReport.com — that's the only legitimately free source, by the way.

When you have a fraud alert in place, you're also eligible for additional free reports. I requested all three and spent an evening going through each one line by line.

Here's what I was looking for:

- Accounts I didn't recognize (especially new ones opened in recent weeks)
- Credit inquiries from companies I'd never contacted
- Address changes I didn't make (identity thieves often change your address first)
- Small, unfamiliar charges on existing accounts

I found no signs of new accounts during that first review — the breach had happened only days earlier, and apparently no one had used my information yet. But reviewing the reports was still valuable because it established a baseline I could compare against in subsequent months.

## Step 4: Place a Credit Freeze (Yes, Even if You Have a Fraud Alert)

This is the step I initially got wrong. I assumed the fraud alert was sufficient protection. It's not.

A fraud alert is like putting a sign on your door asking delivery drivers to knock twice before leaving packages. A credit freeze is like installing a deadbolt and taking the key with you.

A credit freeze blocks almost all access to your credit file. Lenders can't pull your report, which means they can't approve credit applications in your name — even legitimate ones. That's exactly what you want when your Social Security number has been compromised.

You have to freeze your credit at each of the three bureaus separately. There's no central freeze system, which is annoying but manageable.

The three freezes took me about 35 minutes total to complete online:

- Equifax: Create an account, navigate to the freeze section, verify identity, confirm. Took about 12 minutes.
- Experian: Similar process, slightly more annoying identity verification questions (they asked about old addresses and a loan I'd co-signed years ago). Took about 15 minutes.
- TransUnion: Simplest of the three, honestly. Took about 8 minutes.

Each bureau gives you a PIN or password you'll need to lift the freeze later. Save these somewhere secure — I used a password manager, but a physical safe or encrypted file works too. Losing your PIN means going through a lengthy identity verification process to lift the freeze, which I've heard can take days.

### The Cost Question

Credit freezes are free under federal law. The Fair Credit Reporting Act was amended by the Economic Growth, Regulatory Relief, and Consumer Protection Act in 2018, which eliminated all freeze fees. Anyone telling you otherwise is trying to sell you something.

### When Should You Consider Lifting the Freeze?

If you're planning to apply for new credit — a mortgage, car loan, or even a new credit card — you'll need to lift the freeze temporarily, either for a specific lender or for a specific time window. I tested this process when I refinanced my car loan about four months after the breach. The transient lift (where you allow access for a specific lender for a specific number of days) worked flawlessly. Experian processed the temporary lift instantly, and the refinancing went through without a hitch.

## Step 5: File a Report with the FTC

The Federal Trade Commission's IdentityTheft.gov is the official government portal for identity theft reports. Filing a report there gives you an Identity Theft Report, which is a powerful legal document. It entitles you to:

- Extended fraud alerts (7 years instead of 90 days)
- Blocked information from appearing on your credit report
- Extended dispute periods with creditors who reported fraudulent charges

The online form took me about 20 minutes to complete. It asks for details about the theft, what information was exposed, and what you've done so far. The site generates a personalized recovery plan based on your answers, which is genuinely useful.

Here's the URL structure you'll want to remember:

https://www.identitytheft.gov/Report

After filing, you'll get a case number. You'll also be able to access your FTC Identity Theft Report as a PDF. Download it immediately — you'll need it for several of the steps that follow.

In my experience, the FTC report was consistently the most respected document in the identity theft recovery process. Banks, credit bureaus, and collection agencies all accepted it without question when I provided it as evidence of the theft.

## Step 6: File a Police Report

This step is controversial because its value varies wildly depending on where you live. In some jurisdictions, police departments are helpful and will take detailed reports. In others, the officer on duty will look at you like you're wasting their time.

Here's the honest truth I discovered through testing: you may never need a police report. The FTC report alone is sufficient for most purposes. But there are situations where a police report helps:

- A creditor or collection agency refuses to remove fraudulent accounts
- You need to dispute charges with your bank and they want a police case number
- The thief used your information for criminal activity (not just financial fraud)
- You plan to pursue legal action against the perpetrator

I called my local police department's non-emergency line to ask whether I should file a report. The officer I spoke with said, and I quote, "If no money was actually taken, most of what we do is take your statement and file it. It doesn't go anywhere." That matched what I'd read from consumer advocates.

If you do file, bring your FTC report, any documentation of the breach, and a list of affected accounts. You'll likely get a copy of the report for your records. Ask for it, because some institutions will specifically request a police report case number.

## Step 7: Contact Your Financial Institutions

By this point, you've done the big-picture protection. Now it's time to lock down your actual accounts.

### Banks and Credit Unions

Call every bank and credit union where you have accounts. Explain that your identity may have been compromised and ask what they recommend. Most institutions will:

- Put alerts on your accounts
- Issue new debit cards with new numbers
- Offer to set up additional verification steps for phone or online access
- Send you a secure message confirming the actions taken

I called my primary bank (a national chain) and was impressed with how well they handled it. They flagged my accounts within minutes, issued new cards (free of charge, arriving in 3 business days), and even set me up with a verbal password I could use when calling them. The total time was 27 minutes from dialing to hanging up.

My credit union was faster — they'd already seen the breach notification because I wasn't the only member affected. They'd proactively placed security freezes on all members' accounts.

### Credit Cards

Even if you haven't seen fraudulent charges, request new credit card numbers. The breach had exposed bank account details but not credit card numbers in my case, yet I still asked for new cards as a precaution.

Here's the thing I almost got wrong: I initially thought I needed to cancel my credit cards entirely. That's not true, and it's not a good idea. Canceling a credit card can lower your credit score by shortening your credit history and increasing your credit utilization ratio. Getting a new card number on the same account achieves the security benefit without the score damage.

### Investment Accounts, Retirement, etc.

If your financial footprint extends beyond basic banking, contact those institutions too. Brokerage accounts, retirement accounts, cryptocurrency exchanges, and even PayPal or Venmo have fraud departments that can add extra security layers to your accounts.

## Step 8: Change Your Passwords — Correctly

Changing your passwords sounds obvious, but there's a right way and a wrong way to do it.

The wrong way is to log into each account and update your password one by one. That's what I initially did — and I missed accounts I'd forgotten about entirely.

The right way:

1. Start with your email accounts first (they're the keys to everything else — password resets for all your other accounts flow through email)
2. Change your banking and financial accounts next
3. Then move to everything else: social media, shopping, utilities, streaming services, work accounts
4. Use a password manager if you don't already — I tested Bitwarden (free tier) and Dashlane during my recovery, and both made the process infinitely more manageable

Here's a command-line approach that's particularly useful if you have any technical comfort:

# Generate strong random passwords for new accounts
openssl rand -base64 24

# Or for more human-friendly options:
openssl rand -hex 16

For every account where you enable two-factor authentication (2FA), do it. The 15 seconds it takes to type a code from your phone is worth the peace of mind.

One more thing: during my recovery, I noticed that the password reset process itself created small windows of vulnerability. If an identity thief already has access to your email, they can intercept reset links. That's why email is the first thing you secure, and why you should consider setting up 2FA on your email account before anything else.

## Step 9: Watch for Account Takeover Attempts

Identity theft doesn't always mean new accounts in your name. Sometimes it means someone taking over accounts you already have. This is called account takeover (ATO) fraud, and it's been growing rapidly. A 2025 report from Javelin Strategy & Research found that ATO fraud losses reached $17.1 billion in 2024, up 38% from the previous year.

Warning signs to watch for:

- Login notifications from unfamiliar devices or locations
- Password reset emails you didn't request
- Unexpected text messages with verification codes you didn't initiate
- Emails confirming changes to your contact information
- Sudden changes in your credit score

If you see any of these, contact the affected institution immediately and try to regain control of your account.

## Step 10: Check Your Credit Reports Again (and Again)

The credit freeze is your first line of defense, but it's not perfect. Here's the honest limitation: a freeze prevents new credit from being opened in your name, but it doesn't prevent someone from using your existing accounts or committing other types of fraud.

During my six months of monitoring post-breach, I checked my credit reports (the free AnnualCreditReport.com copies) every 90 days using the extended alert benefit. Each time, I compared against my baseline document from Step 3. I found no new fraudulent activity, but I did spot a small error on my Experian report — a late payment on an old auto loan that I'd actually paid on time. That error, while not related to the identity theft, was worth disputing.

If you do find fraudulent accounts, the dispute process requires you to contact each credit bureau with copies of your FTC report and any documentation. That's where the file you created in Step 1 becomes absolutely critical.

One tool I found genuinely useful during this monitoring phase: credit monitoring services. I tested multiple free options — Credit Karma, Experian's free tier, and a few others. They're useful for detecting changes to your credit score, but they're not a substitute for actually reviewing your full reports. The free services typically only show you your TransUnion and Equifax VantageScores, not your actual FICO scores or full report details.

## Potential Complications (and How to Handle Them)

### Fraudulent Tax Filings

Identity thieves sometimes file tax returns in their victims' names to claim refunds. If you try to file your taxes and discover someone else has already filed in your name, you'll need to file Form 14039, the Identity Theft Affidavit, with the IRS. This is a separate process from the FTC report and takes additional time to resolve — I've read accounts of it taking six months or longer.

### Collection Agencies for Debts You Never Incurred

If a collection agency contacts you about a debt that isn't yours, respond in writing within 30 days of their first contact. Send them a copy of your FTC Identity Theft Report and a written statement (via certified mail) explaining that the debt is fraudulent. Under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, they must stop collection efforts while this is being investigated.

This is a situation where having a lawyer can genuinely help, particularly if the debt was purchased by a particularly aggressive collection agency. If you need to understand the broader landscape of what collectors can and cannot do, I'd recommend reading my colleague's piece on [rights dealing with debt collectors](/posts/rights-dealing-with-debt-collectors/) — she tested her rights against three agencies and documented exactly what the FDCPA does and doesn't protect.

### Government Benefits Fraud

If someone uses your information to claim unemployment benefits, disability, or other government programs, you'll need to contact the specific agency involved. The FTC report helps here too, but each agency has its own fraud resolution process. The Department of Justice has a list of resources at justice.gov/criminal-fraud, though the actual resolution can take months.

## When to Get a Lawyer Involved

Most identity theft cases don't require legal representation. The administrative processes — credit freezes, fraud alerts, disputing charges, filing reports — are all designed to be self-service. But there are situations where a lawyer is worth the money:

1. **You're being sued over a debt you didn't incur.** If a collector has filed a lawsuit against you for fraudulent charges, you need an attorney immediately. The same 12-step response plan I documented in my [served a lawsuit response guide](/posts/served-lawsuit-response-steps/) applies, and a lawyer experience with the FDCPA and FCRA can often get the case dismissed with prejudice.

2. **Your case is unusually complex.** Criminal hijacking of your identity, government benefits fraud, or a thief who's been operating for months before detection can all require legal intervention.

3. **A creditor won't cooperate.** If a creditor or collection agency refuses to remove fraudulent information from your credit report despite your FTC Identity Theft Report, a lawyer can send a demand letter or file a lawsuit under the FCRA.

The cost can be substantial — most consumer attorneys work on an hourly basis, typically $150-$400 per hour — but if your financial livelihood is on the line, it's justified.

## What I Wish Someone Had Told Me Before I Started

Looking back, there are a few things I wish I'd known on that Tuesday morning:

**The process is long but not scary.** The identity theft recovery process is more tedious than terrifying. I spent maybe 10 hours total on administrative tasks over the first month. That's less than I spend on bureaucratic paperwork for my freelance business in a normal quarter.

**Most people never have funds stolen.** According to the FTC's 2025 Consumer Sentinel Network Data Book, 62% of identity theft reports involve misuse of an existing account (like credit cards) rather than opening new accounts. And in most cases, victims are not held liable for fraudulent charges. If you report unauthorized transactions promptly to your bank or credit card issuer within 60 days of your statement (the legal window under the Fair Credit Billing Act), you're generally protected.

**The credit bureaus are less scary than they used to be.** When I first started researching identity theft recovery, I expected the bureaus to be unhelpful with labyrinthine phone trees. My 2026 experience was different. Experian's automated system correctly routed my call, and the representative was knowledgeable. This seems to be a genuine improvement — perhaps driven by the scrutiny the bureaus have faced since the 2017 Equifax breach.

**Your credit score will likely drop, and then recover.** Placing a credit freeze doesn't affect your credit score. But if any accounts were fraudulently opened under your name, they'll likely appear on your report when they default, which can cause temporary damage. I'm not going to sugarcoat this — it's one of the more frustrating parts of the process, because it feels like being punished for a crime someone else committed. But the recovery process exists exactly to address it: fraudulent accounts get deleted, not just updated, thanks to the extended dispute rights that come with your FTC Identity Theft Report.

**You're not alone in dealing with this.** The FTC has excellent educational resources, and consumer advocacy organizations like the Identity Theft Resource Center offer free assistance. I never needed to call their hotline (888-400-5530), but knowing it existed was reassuring.

## The Step-by-Step Summary (Your Cheat Sheet)

If you're in the middle of a panic right now, here's the short version:

1. Document everything in one place
2. Place a fraud alert (call any bureau)
3. Review your credit reports from all three bureaus
4. Freeze your credit at all three bureaus
5. File an FTC Identity Theft Report (IdentityTheft.gov)
6. File a police report (optional but helpful)
7. Call your banks and credit cards — get new numbers
8. Change passwords (email first!) and enable 2FA
9. Monitor your credit reports every 90 days
10. Keep your documents organized until everything's resolved

## Final Thoughts

Identity theft feels violating in a way that's hard to describe until it happens to you. It's not just the financial risk — it's the uncomfortable sense that a stranger has some piece of you and might do something with it at any moment.

What I've learned over the past several months is that the system, while bureaucratic and sometimes frustrating, does work. The credit freeze mechanism is genuinely effective at stopping new account fraud. The FTC Identity Theft Report is a powerful legal tool that unlocks real protections. And the banks and credit card companies' legal obligations to their customers mean you're rarely on the hook for unauthorized charges.

There's no way to completely eliminate the risk of identity theft — especially when companies holding your data keep getting breached. But knowing what to do when it happens turns a potentially catastrophic event into an inconvenience you can manage.

If you're looking for more protection strategies, I'd point you to my colleague's guide on the [legal steps after a data breach](/posts/legal-steps-after-data-breach/), which covers what to do starting from the moment you receive that breach notification. And if you suspect your information was used fraudulently on a loan or other credit product, check out [what happens when you're sued for a credit card debt](/posts/what-happens-sued-credit-card-debt/) — it's got a section on fighting fraudulent debts that's directly relevant to victims of identity theft.

Finally, once you've locked down your credit, take a moment to assess your broader legal documentation. Coming through this process made me realize how vulnerable my estate planning documents were — I had a will, but I'd never actually reviewed what would happen to my digital assets. My colleague's piece on [protecting digital assets in a will](/posts/protect-digital-assets-in-will/) convinced me to update my documents to include specific provisions for my online accounts.

The identity theft recovery process isn't fun, but it is doable. Take it step by step, document everything, and lean on the resources that exist specifically for people in your situation. I promise you'll get through it.
