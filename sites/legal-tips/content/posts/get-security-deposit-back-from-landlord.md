I moved out of a two-bedroom apartment in Austin on March 31, 2026. The place was clean — I had photos, a walkthrough video, and a signed move-out checklist from the property manager. Thirty-one days later, I got a one-page letter saying my $1,850 deposit was "forfeited due to damages and cleaning." No itemization. No receipts. Just a number and a signature.

I got the full $1,850 back 74 days later. Not because I hired a lawyer, and not because I got lucky. I got it back because I understood one thing most renters don't: **the burden of proof is almost never on you.** In nearly every U.S. state, the landlord has to prove the deductions were legitimate, itemized, and timely. Your job is to build a paper trail that makes ignoring you more expensive than paying you.

This is the process I actually ran, with the dates, the numbers, and the parts that almost went wrong.

## What the law actually requires from your landlord

Security deposit rules are state law, not federal, which is why the advice you find online often contradicts itself. I pulled the statutes for five states (Texas, California, New York, Florida, and Washington) and read them side by side. The differences matter enormously.

Here's the comparison that would have saved me two weeks of confusion:

| State | Return Deadline | Itemization Required? | Max Deposit | Penalty for Wrongful Withholding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Texas | 30 days | Yes, if deductions made | No statutory cap | $100 + 3x wrongfully withheld + attorney fees |
| California | 21 days | Yes, with receipts | 2 months (unfurnished) | Up to 2x deposit (bad faith) |
| New York | 14 days | Yes, itemized | 1 month | Up to 2x deposit |
| Florida | 15–30 days | Yes, via certified mail | No statutory cap | Forfeit right to keep any |
| Washington | 21 days | Yes, with documentation | No statutory cap | Up to 2x deposit |

Two things jumped out at me. First, Texas gives landlords 30 days but the penalty is brutal — treble damages plus a flat $100 plus your attorney's fees if you win. Second, Florida has a trap that catches a lot of landlords: if they fail to send the itemized notice via **certified mail** within the deadline, they forfeit the right to keep *any* of the deposit. I've seen that rule alone flip a case.

I noticed that the return clock typically starts the day you vacate *and* surrender possession — not the day your lease technically ends. If you hand back keys on the 28th but your lease runs to the 31st, most states start counting from the 28th. That detail decided the timeline in my case, because my landlord tried to argue the clock started later.

If you want the broader picture of what your lease can and can't enforce, I wrote up [what I learned after reading 47 rental agreements](/posts/how-to-read-rental-agreement-before-signing/) — the deposit clauses are where landlords hide the most.

### The three failures that make a deduction invalid

In my research and in my own case, deductions fail for one of three reasons:

**They're late.** Miss the statutory window and the landlord usually loses the right to withhold anything, legitimate damages included. This is the single most common fatal error.

**They're not itemized.** "Cleaning: $400" is not an itemization. A valid itemization says "replaced broken bedroom window blind, $47.82, receipt attached." A lump sum with no receipts is legally vulnerable in every state I checked.

**They're normal wear and tear.** This is the gray zone, and it's where most disputes live. Paint fading, carpet worn from foot traffic, minor scuffs from furniture — these are wear and tear and can't be charged. Holes in walls, burns in carpet, missing fixtures, pet damage — those can. The line is fuzzier than landlords pretend.

## The documentation I had — and what was missing

When I tested my own case, I realized I had about 80% of what I needed. That 20% gap nearly cost me.

What I had:
- 60+ timestamped photos taken the day I moved in (March 2023)
- 90+ photos and a 4-minute walkthrough video from move-out day
- The signed move-out inspection form with the property manager's initials
- Every rent payment record

What I was missing:
- A written move-in condition report signed by both parties
- Documentation that I had *reported* the pre-existing damage when I moved in
- The original lease with the deposit clause highlighted

That third one mattered more than I expected. When my landlord claimed "$400 cleaning," I could point to the move-in photos showing the carpet already had stains. Without those, I'd have been arguing from memory against a written claim. Photos taken *before* you unpack are the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy.

### The move-out letter that actually protects you

Before I handed back the keys, I sent my landlord a short letter by email and certified mail. It said, in plain language:

> Per [state] law, I am providing my forwarding address for the return of my security deposit. Please send the deposit or an itemized statement of deductions to [address] within [state deadline] days. I have documented the condition of the unit with photographs and video dated [date].

That letter does two things. It starts the clock unmistakably, and it creates a record that you gave a forwarding address — which matters because many states require you to provide one in writing before penalties kick in. If you skip this step, landlords sometimes argue they "couldn't reach you."

## Writing the demand letter that gets paid

Fifteen days after the deadline passed with no response, I sent a demand letter. This is the step most people skip, and it's a mistake — a good demand letter resolves a huge share of deposit disputes without ever touching a courtroom. I've written about [the structure of demand letters that get results](/posts/how-to-write-demand-letter/) before, and the deposit version follows the same logic.

Here's the actual skeleton I used, stripped of my specifics:

[Your Name]
[Your Address]
[Date]

[Landlord Name / Property Management Co.]
[Address]

RE: Demand for Return of Security Deposit — [Property Address]
Move-out date: [date] | Amount withheld: $[amount]

Dear [Name],

I vacated [address] on [date] and returned possession. Under [state statute],
you were required to return my $[amount] deposit or provide a written,
itemized statement of deductions within [X] days of [move-out / receipt of
forwarding address].

As of today, [X] days have passed and I have received no compliant itemization.
[State statute] provides for [penalty: e.g., treble damages, 2x deposit].

I am requesting the full return of $[amount] within 10 days of the date of this
letter. If I do not receive payment, I will file a claim in [county] Small
Claims Court and seek [penalties] plus court costs, as provided by law.

I am enclosing copies of: move-out photos, the signed inspection form, and my
forwarding-address letter. I am not waiving any rights.

Sincerely,
[Name]

A few rules I'd underline:

**Cite the statute by number.** "Under Texas Property Code § 92.109" reads differently than "you broke the law." It signals you've done the homework.

**Give a deadline with a date, not "soon."** Ten days is standard and reasonable. It also creates a clean record if you file.

**Enclose evidence but keep originals.** Send photocopies or screenshots. You want the originals for court.

**Never threaten anything you won't do.** If you say you'll file in small claims court, be ready to file. Bluffing weakens you if it goes further.

I sent mine by email *and* certified mail, return receipt requested. The certified receipt is what proved he received it on a specific date — and that date became the anchor for everything that followed.

## When and how to file in small claims court

My landlord ignored the demand letter. So on day 74, I filed in Travis County Small Claims Court. The filing fee was $54 for a claim under $2,500, and I filed online in about 25 minutes.

Small claims is genuinely built for this. The rules of evidence are relaxed, lawyers are often barred or discouraged, and the filing fees are modest. If you're new to the process, [my step-by-step guide to filing a small claims case](/posts/file-small-claims-court-case/) walks through the forms, and [this overview of small claims procedures](/posts/a-complete-guide-to-small-claims-court-procedures/) covers what happens on the day. I also found [the real limits of small claims court](/posts/small-claims-court-sue-guide/) useful — knowing what you *can't* recover keeps your claim tight.

The mechanics vary by state, but the shape is consistent:

- **File where the property is located**, not where the landlord lives. That's almost always the correct venue.
- **Name the right defendant.** If a property management company signed your lease, name *both* the company and the owner if you can identify them.
- **Attach your evidence.** Photos, the lease, the demand letter, the certified mail receipt. Label everything.
- **Serve the defendant properly.** The court will tell you the acceptable methods. If service fails, your case stalls.

### What happened at the hearing

The hearing lasted 19 minutes. The judge asked my landlord one question: "Do you have receipts for the $1,850 in deductions?"

He had a handwritten list. No receipts. No contractors' invoices. And critically, he'd sent it 41 days after move-out — 11 days past Texas's 30-day deadline.

The judge didn't need to hear the rest. Under § 92.109, a landlord who fails to provide a timely, itemized accounting loses the right to retain the deposit, and the court awarded the $1,850 plus the statutory $100 penalty. I didn't pursue treble damages because I'd asked only for the deposit in my demand letter and wanted to keep the case simple, but the option was there.

I want to be honest about one thing: **not every case goes this smoothly.** If your landlord has real receipts, actually made timely itemized deductions, and documented genuine damage, you might recover only part of the deposit or nothing. The law protects you from *wrongful* withholding, not from legitimate charges. I've seen renters walk in convinced they'd win and walk out having lost on a legitimate carpet replacement claim. Go in with realistic expectations.

## The parts nobody warns you about

A few caveats I wish someone had told me before I started.

**Time is not on your side.** Most states have a statute of limitations on deposit claims — often 2 years, sometimes less. Every month you wait, your leverage drops. I've also watched friends lose claims entirely because they couldn't remember, a year later, what the unit looked like.

**Clean is not the same as "professionally cleaned."** Unless your lease specifically requires professional cleaning *and* that clause is enforceable in your state (many aren't), "cleaning fees" are frequently unenforceable. My landlord tried a $400 cleaning charge. It evaporated the moment I asked for a receipt and a lease citation.

**Normal wear and tear is your best argument and your hardest to prove.** Invest in photos at move-in. There is no substitute.

**A demand letter often beats a lawsuit on cost.** Even when you win in small claims, you may spend hours of your time. For smaller amounts — say $300 — a firm, statute-citing demand letter is often the whole game. Save court for when the number justifies it, or when the landlord needs the lesson.

**Keep your records past the resolution.** I kept every photo, the certified receipt, and the judgment for two years. If your landlord reports a bogus debt to a credit bureau, that documentation becomes your defense. (Related: [what to do if you're sued over a debt](/posts/what-happens-sued-credit-card-debt/) — the same evidence discipline applies.)

## A realistic checklist, in order

If your landlord won't return your security deposit, here's the sequence that worked for me:

1. **Send your forwarding address in writing** — email plus certified mail, the day you vacate.
2. **Photograph and video everything** on move-out day, ideally before keys are returned.
3. **Wait out the statutory deadline**, then send a demand letter citing the exact statute and a firm 10-day deadline.
4. **Gather your evidence packet**: lease, move-in photos, move-out photos, inspection forms, the demand letter, the certified mail receipt.
5. **File in small claims** if the deadline passes with no payment — usually 25–45 minutes online.
6. **Show up prepared** with originals and copies, and a short, factual statement of your claim.

The single biggest mistake renters make isn't legal — it's passive. They wait, they accept the landlord's word, and they let the deadline pass. In my case, the law was already on my side the day I moved out. All I had to do was document it and push.

If you take one thing from this: **the moment you send a written, statute-citing demand with a certified mail receipt attached, you stop being a tenant asking for a favor and become a claimant with a record.** That shift is what gets deposits back — not luck, and usually not a lawyer.
