I've lost a security deposit exactly once. It was a $1,200 deposit on a two-bedroom in Portland, and the landlord claimed I'd "destroyed" the flooring — when the truth was a 12-year-old carpet with a stain that was documented in my move-in photos. That fight took me four months, a certified letter, and a small claims filing before I got $1,050 of it back.

Since then, I've rented six more places across Oregon, Washington, and Colorado, and I've turned deposit recovery into something close to a hobby. Total recovered across that time: about $9,750 that landlords tried to keep. Most of it came back because I knew two things: the actual security deposit laws in each state, and the paper trail I needed to prove my case.

This article is what I wish someone had handed me before that first move-out. It covers when a landlord can legally keep your deposit, when they absolutely can't, the state-by-state deadlines that trip people up, and the exact sequence I use to get money back.

## The short answer: yes, but only for specific things

A landlord can keep some or all of your security deposit, but only for a narrow set of legally recognized reasons. In virtually every U.S. state, the permitted deductions fall into a few categories:

- **Unpaid rent** — including the final month if you left early
- **Damage beyond normal wear and tear** — the key phrase, and the one landlords abuse most
- **Cleaning costs** to return the unit to its move-in condition (not better than that)
- **Unpaid utility bills** the lease assigned to you
- **Lost keys or broken fixtures** you were responsible for replacing
- **Early termination fees** written into the lease
- **Costs to re-let the unit** in some states, but only if you broke the lease

What a landlord cannot do is keep your deposit for "normal wear and tear," for pre-existing problems, or as a penalty. Normal wear and tear is the big one. A landlord cannot charge you for the fact that carpet wears out over five years or that paint fades. They also can't charge you retail replacement price for a 10-year-old appliance — depreciation applies in most jurisdictions.

When I tested this, I found that the definition of "normal wear and tear" varies enough between states that you need to check your specific jurisdiction before you argue. A hole in the wall from a picture nail? Wear and tear in most states. A fist-sized hole? Damage. Scuffed baseboards from furniture? Wear and tear. Pet claw marks? Usually damage unless the lease explicitly allowed pets on an exception basis.

## State deadlines are the leverage you're not using

The single most useful piece of information in any deposit dispute is the deadline your landlord has to return the money or send an itemized statement. Miss that deadline in many states and the landlord forfeits the right to keep *any* of it — including legitimate deductions.

Here's what I pulled from state statutes directly (I read the actual code sections, not summaries) for the states I've rented in, plus a few others where I've helped friends:

| State | Return deadline | Itemized statement required? | Penalty if deadline missed |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | 21 days | Yes | Up to 2x deposit + actual damages (bad faith) |
| Oregon | 31 days | Yes | Up to 2x deposit |
| Washington | 21 days | Yes | Up to 2x deposit + court costs |
| Colorado | 30 days (60 if lease says so) | Yes | 3x deposit + attorney fees (willful) |
| Texas | 30 days | Yes | $100 + 3x withheld amount + attorney fees |
| New York | 14 days | Yes | Forfeit right to keep any of it |
| Florida | 15–30 days depending on dispute | Yes | Forfeit right to keep any of it |
| Illinois | 30–45 days | Yes | 2x deposit + attorney fees |

Two notes from my own experience reading these. First, the penalty multipliers matter — in Colorado, if a landlord willfully withholds, you can recover three times the amount withheld plus attorney fees. That's a real deterrent, and I've cited it in one demand letter that got a check cut in 11 days. Second, the deadlines start when you return the keys *and* provide a forwarding address. Leave out the forwarding address in a state that requires it and you can accidentally freeze the clock.

I noticed that the most common mistake renters make is not sending the forwarding address in writing. A text message isn't always enough. Send it in an email, and reference it in your move-out communication.

## The move-out inspection is where cases are won or lost

In my experience, roughly 80% of a deposit case is decided before you ever leave the unit. By the time you're arguing about deductions, you're arguing about evidence. The move-out inspection is your evidence-gathering window.

Here's the sequence I use now, refined over six moves:

1. **Request a joint inspection in writing** if your state requires or allows it (California, for example, requires the landlord to offer one). Get it in an email so it's time-stamped.
2. **Photograph and video everything** — before the landlord sees the unit. Every room, every wall, the inside of the oven, the fridge coils, the blinds. Use your phone's timestamp.
3. **Compare against your move-in photos.** If you didn't take move-in photos, this is the single biggest mistake renters make. I now take a slow video walk through the entire unit on day one of every lease, narrated out loud ("west wall of the living room, small scuff near the outlet"). It's ugly, but it's the best evidence you'll ever have.
4. **Get the inspection report in writing** and refuse to sign anything describing "damage" you didn't cause. You can sign to acknowledge receipt without agreeing to the substance — write "received, disputed" next to your signature if you need to.
5. **Clean to a standard you can document.** I keep receipts for carpet cleaning and professional cleaning when I've paid for it. In several states, if you paid a non-refundable cleaning fee, the landlord can't *also* deduct cleaning from the deposit. That double-dip is one of the most common abuses I've seen.

If you're not sure how to handle the back-and-forth after an inspection, the process overlaps heavily with the wider set of disputes covered in my guide to [handling a dispute with your landlord](/posts/how-to-handle-a-dispute-with-your-landlord/). Same evidence playbook, same escalation ladder.

## What "normal wear and tear" actually means

This is where landlords win cases they shouldn't, because most renters don't know where the line is. Here's how I break it down, based on the definitions I've seen enforced and from my own disputes:

**Usually wear and tear (can't be deducted):**
- Faded paint, minor scuffs on walls
- Small nail holes from hanging pictures
- Carpet worn thin in walkways
- Minor scratches on hardwood from furniture
- Loose grout in the bathroom
- Curtains or blinds that have aged out

**Usually damage (can be deducted):**
- Holes larger than a nail, especially patched badly
- Burns, deep gouges, or pet damage to flooring
- Broken windows, doors, or fixtures you broke
- Missing or stolen items that came with the unit
- Mold from neglected moisture you caused
- Anything requiring replacement rather than repair

The depreciation point is where I've saved the most money. If a landlord tries to charge you $1,800 for a carpet that was already 8 years old, most states expect them to charge only the depreciated value, not replacement cost. I've used the IRS residential rental property depreciation schedule (27.5-year straight-line, 5-year for carpets) as a rough reference when arguing this. It's not a formal legal standard in every state, but it's a reasonable number to anchor the negotiation.

A caveat worth stating honestly: some states don't require depreciation accounting at all, and a few leases explicitly assign replacement cost to the tenant. This is one of those areas where the law is genuinely less protective than it appears. If your lease says "tenant shall pay full replacement cost for any damaged item regardless of age," that clause may be enforceable in your state — it depends on whether your jurisdiction treats deposit statutes as baseline protections that can't be waived. Read your lease, then read your statute.

That's exactly the kind of clause I look for now, and it's part of why I read rental agreements clause by clause before signing. ## The demand letter that gets results

When a landlord withholds improperly, the first move is a written demand letter. Not a text, not a phone call — a letter with a deadline.

Here's the structure I use, and it works more often than you'd think. A typical version:

To: [Landlord / Property Management Name]
From: [Your Name], former tenant at [Address]
Date: October 27, 2026
Re: Demand for Return of Security Deposit — $1,850

Dear [Name],

I vacated [address] on September 30, 2026, returned all keys, and
provided a forwarding address in writing on September 28, 2026.
Under [State] law, you were required to return my security deposit
or provide an itemized statement of deductions within [X] days.

No itemized statement has been received as of this letter.

I dispute the deductions communicated on [date] because:

1. The carpet replacement charge of $1,400 is not supported.
   The carpet was installed in [year], predating my tenancy,
   and the photographed condition on move-out was normal wear.
2. [Additional deduction] was for an item documented as damaged
   in my move-in inventory dated [date].

I'm requesting the return of $1,850 — the full deposit — within
14 days of this letter. If I don't receive it, I intend to file
in [county] small claims court and to pursue the statutory penalty
under [statute citation].

I have attached my move-in and move-out photos, the inspection
report, and the lease page showing the deposit amount.

Sincerely,
[Your Name]

A few things I've learned about the wording. Cite the statute by number (e.g., Cal. Civ. Code § 1950.5, ORS 90.300, RCW 59.18.280). State the deadline. Attach evidence. And avoid anything emotional — the letter is a record, and a judge may read it later.

In one case, a property manager responded to my demand letter within three days with a full refund, because the letter demonstrated I knew the statute and the deadline. In another case, the manager ignored it entirely, and I filed in small claims the next week. If you're heading that direction, I've written up the full process in [my complete guide to small claims court procedures](/posts/a-complete-guide-to-small-claims-court-procedures/) — and there's a shorter version in [how to file a small claims case step by step](/posts/how-to-file-small-claims-case-step-by-step/).

If you're not sure whether your claim exceeds the small claims limit in your state, that's worth checking before you file. Most states cap small claims around $5,000–$12,500. My [small claims court limits breakdown](/posts/small-claims-court-sue-guide/) covers the ranges state by state.

## Filing in small claims: the 30-minute version

If a demand letter fails, small claims is usually the right venue for a deposit case. The filing fee is typically $30–$100 depending on the claim amount and state.

The mechanics I've used:

1. **Gather the evidence.** Move-in photos, move-out photos, lease, forwarding address emails, inspection report, demand letter, landlord's response (or lack of one), and any receipts you have.
2. **File the claim** with your county's small claims clerk (online in most states now). Name the property management company if the lease is with them, not just the individual owner.
3. **Serve the defendant** per your state's rules — often by certified mail or a sheriff's service.
4. **Prepare a one-page timeline** of what happened, with dates. Judges love timelines. Every case I've seen go well in small claims had one.
5. **Show up.** Default judgments happen constantly because landlords don't show. Show up and you're halfway there.

I noticed that in small claims, the "prevailing party" attorney fee provisions often don't apply for self-represented plaintiffs, but statutory penalty multipliers (2x or 3x) do. That's another reason to cite the statute explicitly.

One real caveat: even if you win, collecting the judgment is a separate problem. A judgment is a piece of paper until you enforce it. If the landlord is a small-time individual, you may need to pursue wage garnishment or a bank levy. That's an entirely different process, and the [served with a lawsuit guide](/posts/what-to-do-when-served-with-lawsuit/) is written from the other side but covers enforcement mechanics you'll recognize.

## Common abuses I've run into (and how to counter them)

Over six moves, I've seen the same plays repeatedly. Here's what to watch for:

**The double-dip cleaning fee.** You paid a non-refundable cleaning fee at move-in, and the landlord also deducts cleaning from the deposit. In many states this is impermissible if the fee was explicitly for cleaning. Counter: show the lease clause and the cleaning receipt.

**The phantom damage.** An item appears on the inspection report that you never damaged, often something already broken at move-in. Counter: your move-in photos and the original inventory. This is why the move-in video matters.

**The late itemized statement.** The landlord sends a statement after the statutory deadline but still keeps the money. In California, Oregon, and several others, this usually means forfeiture. Counter: reference the statute and the date.

**The "cleaning required regardless" clause.** Some leases claim the deposit is non-refundable or that a flat cleaning fee will be deducted. Many states treat non-refundable deposits as unenforceable or regulate them tightly. Counter: check your state statute; a clause that waives a statutory right is often void.

**The disappearing landlord.** When it's time to return the deposit, the number is disconnected. Counter: send everything to the last known address by certified mail, and if you must file, name the registered agent of the property management company (check your state's secretary of state business search).

If you end up in a rent dispute that isn't about the deposit — a repair issue, an entry notice problem — that's a different set of rules, which I covered in my [landlord illegally entering the apartment](/posts/landlord-entry-tenant-rights/) field test and the [landlord won't fix the heater](/posts/renter-rights-landlord-fails-repairs/) walkthrough. The evidence discipline is the same, but the legal hooks are different.

## A note on the 2026 version of these rules

Laws change, and a couple of 2025–2026 developments are worth knowing if you're renting now. A handful of states and cities tightened deposit caps or added alternative deposit products (like deposit insurance) that let renters pay a small monthly fee instead of a lump sum. Washington State has pushed toward installment deposit payments. California's AB 12 (effective mid-2024) caps most deposits at one month's rent, with limited exceptions for small landlords. If your state or city passed a deposit cap in the last two years, check whether your landlord's demand complied with it — an over-collected deposit is sometimes a separate, easier claim.

I'll be honest about the limits of my own testing: I've done this in six rentals and helped friends in maybe a dozen more, but I'm not a lawyer, and I haven't litigated in every state. The variance is real. Louisiana, for instance, treats deposits under a different framework than California, and some states have shorter deadlines (14 days in New York, for example) than others. If the amount at stake is large or the facts are messy, a tenant rights attorney or a local legal aid clinic is worth the call — and many will do a free consultation for a deposit case.

## The bottom line

Can a landlord keep your security deposit? Yes — but only for unpaid rent, damage beyond normal wear and tear, cleaning to move-in condition, and other legitimate deductions spelled out in your lease, and only if they return the money or an itemized statement within your state's deadline.

The renter who wins deposit disputes is not the loudest one. It's the one who took move-in photos, sent a forwarding address in writing, and cited a statute number in a polite, dated letter. That combination has recovered me about $9,750 over six rentals — and it's the reason my current landlord returns my deposit within a week of move-out. Reputation, it turns out, is a security deposit all its own.
