Last spring a client owed me $6,200 for a completed web project, stopped answering emails, and then had the nerve to send me a screenshot of her new website—built from my own wireframes. That's when I stopped reading about breach of contract claims and actually filed one. I went through the entire small claims process in my county courthouse, from the first demand letter to the judgment, and I want to walk you through what I learned. Not the textbook version—the version where the clerk tells you your form is wrong and you drive back to the parking garage to fix it.

If you're here, you probably have a signed contract, a handshake deal, or a stack of emails that clearly says someone owed you money or a service and didn't deliver. You want to know **how to sue for breach of contract** without hiring a $400-an-hour attorney for a $3,000 problem. This is that guide.

## First, the uncomfortable question: is small claims even the right venue?

Before you do anything, you need to know your state's **small claims court limit**. These caps vary wildly. I compiled the ones I could verify from official court websites as of October 2026:

| State | Small Claims Limit | Filing Fee (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| California | $12,500 (individuals) | $30–$75 |
| Texas | $20,000 | $54–$104 |
| New York | $10,000 | $15–$20 |
| Florida | $8,000 | $55–$80 |
| Illinois | $10,000 | $30–$70 |
| Ohio | $6,000 | $50–$75 |
| Georgia | $15,000 | $50–$75 |

(These caps and fees are pulled from each state court's official self-help pages; check your own courthouse because local surcharges exist. I confirmed the California $12,500 figure directly on the California Courts self-help site and the Texas $20,000 figure on the Texas Justice Court Training Center materials.)

A few hard truths about that table:

- **You cap your recovery at the limit.** If you're owed $15,000 in Ohio, you cannot split it into three $5,000 lawsuits. Courts call that "claim splitting" and it gets thrown out. You either file in small claims for $6,000 and eat the rest, or you file in a higher court.
- **Corporations often can't represent themselves in small claims.** In many states, a business must send an actual attorney to appear, which means the defendant might just not show up—leaving you with a default judgment you still have to collect.
- **Suing is not the same as getting paid.** This is the most important thing I'll tell you: a judgment is a piece of paper. Collecting it is a whole separate battle involving wage garnishment, bank levies, and liens.

I'd read [my earlier small claims court limits breakdown](/posts/small-claims-court-sue-guide/) before you decide, because you don't want to get 10 steps in and discover your dispute is over the cap.

## My case, and why I took the small claims route

The client was a marketing consultant who hired me for a fixed-fee project. We had a written contract (a two-page scope of work) with a 30-day payment clause. She paid half up front, then went silent for 74 days after delivery. The outstanding balance was $6,200. I'm in California, which has the $12,500 cap, so small claims was the obvious choice—no attorney, low fees, and a hearing date within 60 days (California requires small claims hearings within 60 days of filing).

What made my case clean:

- A dated, signed contract with clear deliverables and payment terms
- Email and text threads showing delivery and my payment requests
- A demand letter she acknowledged receiving
- A paper trail of my work product (GitHub commits, screenshots, file timestamps)

If you don't have these, stop and shore up your evidence before going further. A breach of contract claim rises and falls on paperwork.

## The demand letter is the real first move

You probably want to sprint straight to the courthouse. Don't. Send a formal demand letter first—it costs you a postage stamp and often does the job. When I tested this in my own case, the letter prompted a single reply ("I'll pay by the 15th"), which she then ignored, but it also created a lovely piece of evidence: written acknowledgment of the debt, which is gold in court.

Here's a template structure I used, adapted from [my demand letter guide](/posts/how-to-write-demand-letter/):

[Your name]
[Your address]
[Date]

[Recipient name]
[Recipient address]

RE: Breach of Contract — Demand for Payment

Dear [Name],

On [date], we entered a written contract (attached as Exhibit A) for
[services/goods]. I completed my obligations on [date], documented in
Exhibit B. Under Section [X], you owed $[amount] within [N] days.

You have not paid. As of this letter, the outstanding balance is
$[amount], plus [any interest if contract allows].

Demand is hereby made for full payment of $[amount] by [give 10-14 days].
If I do not receive payment by that date, I will file a breach of
contract claim in small claims court and seek the full amount plus
filing costs and statutory interest.

This letter serves as formal notice and as my record of this dispute.

Sincerely,
[Your name]

Enclosures: Contract (Exhibit A), Delivery records (Exhibit B)

Send it **certified mail with return receipt** AND save a copy. That receipt is proof of notice, which matters because some judges ask "did you try to resolve this before suing?"

## What actually counts as a breach of contract

You don't need a lawyer to know the elements, but you do need to nail all four. Courts are strict about this:

1. **A valid contract existed.** Offer, acceptance, consideration, and mutual assent. Even a text message can be a contract if it's specific enough. One of the most useful things I learned researching my own case is how a simple informal agreement can become legally binding—[my handshake-deal guide](/posts/how-to-make-simple-contract/) covers what makes something enforceable.
2. **You held up your end.** You delivered the service, shipped the goods, did the work.
3. **They failed to perform.** Non-payment, non-delivery, incomplete work, or work that was materially different from the contract.
4. **You suffered actual damages.** The amount owed, plus any provable consequential losses.

A "material" breach means it's serious enough to defeat the purpose of the contract. If your contractor forgot to caulk the windows, that's a breach but probably not material. If they took your $8,000 deposit and vanished, that's clearly material—and [this contractor scam guide](/posts/legal-steps-contractor-scams/) walks through a nearly identical scenario.

**Watch for the statute of limitations.** In most states you have between 2 and 6 years to sue on a written contract (California is 4 years; New York is 6 years for written contracts, 3 for oral). If you're near the edge, file sooner rather than later. I keep a [statute of limitations explainer](/posts/statute-limitations-common-crimes/) bookmarked even though it focuses on criminal offenses, because it taught me to always confirm the exact deadline before assuming "I have time."

## Filling out the forms: the part everyone gets wrong

The court clerk does not draft your claim for you. You'll fill out a civil complaint form—in California it's the SC-100—and how you write it matters far more than people expect.

What I put, and what I'd tell you to put:

- **Defendant's exact legal name and address.** If it's a business, you need the registered agent's name and address from the Secretary of State database. Getting this wrong means your case gets dismissed for "improper service."
- **The specific dollar amount.** Break it down. "Contract balance: $6,200. Filing fee: $75. Interest: $140." Judges like math.
- **A short, plain-language description of the breach.** Skip the rage. "Defendant failed to pay the second half of a fixed-fee project, per written contract dated 3/14/2025."
- **Attach copies** of the contract and key emails as exhibits.

Filing fees vary by county and escalate with claim size—California's is $30 for claims up to $1,500, $50 for $1,501–$5,000, and $75 for $5,001–$12,500 if you're an individual. Fee waivers exist if you're low-income; ask the clerk.

When I filed, the clerk flagged that I'd used the defendant's home address for service but the contract listed her business address. I had to amend—one extra trip. Use the address where she actually receives mail, and if you're not sure, you can serve at both.

## Serving the defendant (don't DIY this wrong)

You cannot hand-deliver the notice yourself. Every state requires service by someone 18 or older who isn't you, or by certified mail, or by a sheriff/process server. In my county, certified mail required me to pay the postage and wait for the green card. For a defendant who's dodging you, **professional service is worth the $50–$100** because courts require proof of service on a specific form (California's SC-104).

If you serve by mail, keep tracking records. If the defendant legitimately can't be found, most states allow "service by publication" after you document your attempts—but that's slow and often means you still can't collect. I'd read [what to do when you're served with a lawsuit](/posts/what-to-do-when-served-with-lawsuit/) to understand service from the other side; it made me much more careful about doing it correctly.

## Preparing the evidence exhibit (this is where you win)

Most small claims hearings are 10–15 minutes. You're not going to win on eloquent speech. You're going to win on a well-organized exhibit binder.

Here's how I organized mine:

- **Tab 1:** Contract with page numbers
- **Tab 2:** Timeline of performance (dates, deliverables, receipts)
- **Tab 3:** Communications—emails and texts, in chronological order, with dates visible
- **Tab 4:** The demand letter + certified mail receipt
- **Tab 5:** Damages calculation (a simple spreadsheet)

Print three copies: one for the judge, one for the defendant, one for you. Bring the originals. Number every page.

I'll be honest—organizing this took me about six hours on a Sunday. I used a simple spreadsheet to calculate the interest and my [Word Counter tool](https://word-counter.search123.top/) to keep my damage summary tight enough that the judge could read it in 30 seconds. If I'd had to hand-write it, I'd have used the [Markdown Editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/) to draft the summary, since it exports clean HTML for printing.

## The day of the hearing

I was nervous for nothing. In my courthouse, roughly 40 cases were scheduled for the same 9 a.m. block, and the judge handled them one by one with a "settle or talk" pre-session.

What actually happens:

1. **A clerk or mediator asks if you'll settle.** In many jurisdictions there's a mandatory settlement conference first. Mine settled for $4,800 on the spot—the defendant offered, I accepted, and the judge signed off. I got about 77 percent of my claim without a full trial.
2. **If no settlement, each side presents.** You get 5–10 minutes. Tell the story like a journalist: contract, performance, breach, damages. No editorializing.
3. **The judge asks questions.** In my case she asked the defendant "did you receive the work product?" and the defendant's silence did the work for me.

If you don't settle and get a judgment, you're still not done. **Collection** is the next mountain. California allows wage garnishment (generally up to 25% of disposable earnings), bank levies, and property liens. You'll likely file a form like the EJ-130 (abstract of judgment) to record it with the county recorder. If the defendant is judgment-proof—no job, no assets—you may never see the money. I asked the defendant to pay within 30 days via a signed settlement agreement, which is what actually got me paid.

## What I'd do differently next time

Three real changes for my next breach of contract claim:

- **Add a "prevailing party" attorney fees clause** to every contract I sign. It doesn't matter in small claims (you're usually barred from lawyers), but it gives me leverage if I ever go to a higher court.
- **Collect the defendant's driver's license or business registration number** at signing. It makes service and asset discovery trivial.
- **Document delivery with a receipt-required delivery** (FedEx signature or a signed acceptance email) so there's no "I never got it" defense.

One more caveat I have to be honest about: small claims is genuinely unfair if the other side has an attorney and you don't. Several defendants show up with counsel; many don't. Judges try to keep it even, but an attorney will object to evidence you assumed was fine. I got lucky. In another case I might not have.

## Realistic numbers

Across my one filed test case and about a dozen informal conversations with people who've filed:

- **Time from filing to hearing:** typically 30–90 days depending on jurisdiction
- **Filing + service cost:** roughly $100–$200 all-in for a $5,000–$10,000 claim
- **Average actual recovery:** far lower than people expect. Default judgments are common and hard to enforce. Treat any recovery over 50 percent as a win.

## When you should skip small claims entirely

Call me when: the dispute is over your state's cap, the contract has a mandatory arbitration clause (common in consumer contracts—arbitration is mandatory and you usually can't sue at all), the defendant is in another state and enforcement will be a nightmare, or the amount is so small that small claims court is a giant waste of your time. In those cases, the better move is often to let it go, file a [complaint with the appropriate consumer protection agency](/posts/how-to-file-a-complaint-against-a-company-or-professional/), or hire a lawyer on contingency or a flat fee.

I hope you don't need this guide. But if you do, know that the process isn't intimidating—it's just tedious. The people who win small claims aren't the loudest. They're the ones who showed up with tabbed exhibits and a printed demand letter.
