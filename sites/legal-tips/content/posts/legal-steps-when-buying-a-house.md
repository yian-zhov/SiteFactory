The first time I bought a house, I thought the legal part was just signing papers at closing. It was not. It was three weeks of documents I didn't understand, a title search that turned up an old contractor's lien, and a seller who wanted me to waive the attorney review period to "move things along." I almost did.

The second time, in 2024, I went in with a checklist. Different state, different lender, different attorney — and a completely different experience. What follows is the legal sequence I now use every time, the documents that actually matter, and the spots where the process goes sideways.

If you only read one paragraph: the legal work of buying a house happens *before* you sign, not at the closing table. By the time you're sitting in that room with a pen, your leverage is nearly gone.

## The legal steps, in the order they actually happen

Here's the sequence I've now been through twice, plus the research I did for a third attempt that I called off (more on that later).

| Stage | Legal Step | Who Typically Handles It | Typical Cost (2026) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Attorney review of purchase agreement | Real estate attorney | $300–$800 flat |
| 2 | Earnest money deposit & escrow setup | Title company / escrow agent | Included in closing costs |
| 3 | Property inspection (not legal, but creates legal leverage) | Licensed inspector | $350–$600 |
| 4 | Title search and title commitment | Title company | $250–$500 |
| 5 | Review of HOA docs, CC&Rs, easements | Attorney or buyer | $0–$400 |
| 6 | Loan documents review (note, deed of trust, disclosures) | Attorney | Bundled with stage 1 |
| 7 | Title insurance purchase (lender's + optional owner's) | Title company | 0.5%–1% of purchase price |
| 8 | Closing / settlement and deed recording | Title company + county recorder | $150–$500 recording fees |
| 9 | Post-closing: deed recording confirmation, title policy delivery | County + title company | $0 |

The order matters. Stage 1 is the only point where you can walk away without losing your earnest money in most contracts. After the attorney review or inspection contingency window closes, your deposit is at risk.

## Attorney review: the window most buyers sleep through

In New Jersey, where I bought my first house, the standard realtor-drafted contract gives both parties three business days to have an attorney review the agreement and either approve it, disapprove it, or propose changes. If neither side's attorney acts, the contract becomes binding as written.

I noticed that almost nobody in my first closing used that window. My agent said "the contract is standard, everyone signs it." My attorney later told me he'd found an issue in about a third of the review periods he handled.

When I tested the process the second time, in a state without a mandated review period, I still asked my attorney to look at the purchase agreement before I signed. Cost: $450. He found two things — a clause making the inspection contingency "sole discretion of buyer" with no defined remedy, and a provision that let the seller keep the appliances listed in the MLS but not in the contract. Small stuff. But the appliance clause alone saved me roughly $3,000 when the seller tried to take the washer and dryer.

If you want the broader context of how real estate attorneys fit into a transaction, I wrote up the results of testing three different processes in [when you actually need a real estate attorney](/posts/when-need-real-estate-attorney/). Short version: for a standard single-family purchase, a flat-fee review is worth it. For a cash purchase of a condo in a state with an attorney-state practice, it's often mandatory.

### What to ask your attorney before you retain them

These are the questions I now ask on the first call:

- Do you charge a flat fee or hourly? What's included?
- Will you review the purchase agreement, the title commitment, the HOA documents, and the closing statement?
- Do you represent buyers only, or do you also represent lenders or agents in the same transaction?
- Who at your office will actually review my file, and how do I reach them?

That last one matters. In my first purchase, the attorney I spoke with was not the associate who reviewed my documents. The associate missed the lien until the title company flagged it at the last minute.

## The purchase agreement: clauses that carry the most legal weight

The purchase agreement is the contract. Everything else is supporting documentation. Here's what I check, in order of how much trouble it causes when it goes wrong.

### Contingencies and their deadlines

A contingency is a condition that must be satisfied for the deal to close. The three that matter for most buyers are financing, inspection, and appraisal.

Each contingency has a deadline. If you don't act within it — get the inspection done, deliver the repair request, get the loan commitment — you waive the contingency. Waiving it means you lose the legal right to walk away and recover your deposit.

I've watched buyers miss an inspection deadline by two days because the inspector's report arrived late. The buyer lost the right to negotiate repairs. They still closed. It cost them about $4,000 in repairs they could have asked the seller to cover.

### Earnest money and who holds it

The earnest money is your good-faith deposit. In most states it goes into an escrow account held by the title company or the seller's broker. The contract should specify:

- The amount and when it's due
- Who holds it
- Under what conditions it's returned to you
- What happens if the deal falls apart over a contingency

If the contract doesn't specify a return path, you're relying on the seller's goodwill when the deal dissolves. That's not a legal position I want to be in.

### The "as-is" clause

"As-is" does not mean the seller can hide known defects. It means the seller isn't obligated to repair anything. In most states, sellers still have a duty to disclose known material defects — a leaky roof they've patched three times, a foundation crack they've been monitoring, a history of water intrusion.

I noticed that agents sometimes use "as-is" language informally when they mean "we won't negotiate repairs." That's not what the contract says. If the seller wants to sell as-is, the disclosure obligations still apply.

## Title: the part of the deal you can't see until it bites you

Title is the legal ownership of the property. A title search traces the chain of ownership back through public records to identify anyone with a claim against the property. That can include:

- Prior mortgages that weren't properly discharged
- Mechanic's liens from contractors who weren't paid
- Tax liens
- Easements granting someone else the right to use part of your land
- Judgment liens from lawsuits against a prior owner
- Heirs of a deceased owner who never formally probated the estate

My first house had a lien from a roofer who had done work for the previous owner four years earlier. The roofer had never been paid the final $2,400. He filed a mechanic's lien, and it was still on the record when I bought the house.

The title company caught it. The seller had to pay it off before closing. If the title company had missed it, I would have bought a house with a $2,400 claim against it — and the roofer could have sued me to enforce it.

This is why the title search and title insurance exist. The search finds the problems. The insurance protects you if the search misses something.

### What title insurance actually covers

There are two policies at most closings:

**Lender's title insurance** protects the lender's interest up to the loan amount. You pay for it, but it protects them.

**Owner's title insurance** protects your ownership interest. It's optional in most states, and it's the one you actually want.

I've been asked more than once whether owner's title insurance is worth it. The answer depends on how much risk you want to carry. The premium is typically paid once at closing and covers you for as long as you own the property. In my experience, for a property with any complexity in its chain of title — a prior foreclosure, a probate sale, a divorce transfer — it's not optional in practice. The cost of defending a title claim can exceed the value of the policy.

One caveat: title insurance covers title defects that existed at closing. It does not cover problems that arise after you buy, like a neighbor building a fence across your property line. That's a different kind of dispute — if you end up there, my writeup on [neighbor fence laws and who pays](/posts/neighbor-fence-law-repair-costs/) covers the practical side.

## The inspection contingency: legal leverage disguised as a home inspection

The inspection itself is not a legal step. The legal step is what you do with the report.

Your inspection contingency gives you a window — usually 7 to 15 days — to complete inspections and deliver a response to the seller. The response can be:

- **Accept the property as-is** and proceed
- **Request repairs** the seller must complete before closing
- **Request a credit** against the purchase price
- **Terminate the contract** and recover your deposit

The form of the response matters legally. A verbal request for repairs is not binding. A written repair request delivered within the contingency window is. If the seller refuses, you can typically still terminate and recover your deposit — but only if you do it within the window.

I made this mistake once. I asked my agent to "mention" a few items to the seller's agent. Nothing got fixed, and by the time I realized I needed a written request, the contingency window had closed.

### When to terminate rather than negotiate

This is a judgment call, but I've developed a rough rule. If the inspection turns up:

- **Structural issues** (foundation, load-bearing walls, roof structure) — get an engineer's report before you decide. The cost of the report is small compared to the cost of being wrong.
- **Environmental issues** (mold, radon, asbestos, lead paint in pre-1978 homes) — these often trigger disclosure obligations and can affect financing.
- **Permit problems** (unpermitted additions, finished basements without permits) — these can affect your ability to sell later and may require remediation.

If any of these are present and the seller won't address them, terminating is usually the right call. The earnest money is recoverable within the contingency window. After that, it's not.

## Loan documents: the paperwork most buyers skim

The loan documents arrive at closing, often the day before or the morning of. They include:

- The **promissory note** — your promise to repay
- The **deed of trust or mortgage** — the document that gives the lender the right to foreclose
- The **closing disclosure** — the final statement of all costs
- The **truth-in-lending disclosure** — the APR and total finance charges

The promissory note and deed of trust are not negotiable in any meaningful sense. But the closing disclosure is worth checking line by line against the loan estimate you received when you applied. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau requires the closing disclosure to be delivered at least three business days before closing, specifically so you can compare it to the loan estimate.

If the numbers don't match, ask. Some changes are legitimate (a change in closing date, a change in property taxes). Some are errors. Some are fees that appeared without explanation.

I've reviewed loan agreements in other contexts — my writeup on [legal interest rates on loans](/posts/legal-interest-rates-on-loans-explained/) covers how to read the APR section — and the same approach applies here. The APR is not the same as the interest rate. It includes fees and points. Compare the APR on the closing disclosure to the APR on the loan estimate. If it jumped, find out why.

## HOA documents: a separate contract you're also signing

If the property is in a homeowners association, you're buying into a second legal structure on top of the deed. The HOA documents include:

- **CC&Rs** (Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions) — the rules you agree to follow
- **Bylaws** — how the association is governed
- **Financial statements** — reserves, budget, past due assessments
- **Meeting minutes** — recent decisions, pending litigation, special assessments
- **Rules and regulations** — the day-to-day rules

I nearly bought a condo in 2023 where the HOA had a special assessment pending for a roof replacement. The assessment would have added about $180 per month to my costs for the next five years. It wasn't in the listing. It was in the minutes from three months earlier.

Most states give buyers a statutory right to review HOA documents and cancel the contract within a set period — typically 3 to 5 days after receipt. Use it.

## Closing: what actually happens legally

At closing, several legal events happen at once:

1. You sign the promissory note and deed of trust, creating the loan.
2. You sign the deed, transferring title from the seller to you.
3. The title company records the deed with the county.
4. Funds are disbursed to the seller, the lender, the agents, and the title company.
5. Title insurance policies are issued.

The deed recording is the legal moment ownership transfers. In most states, recording happens the same day or the next business day. You can verify it through your county recorder's website — search by your name or the property address. I check mine a week after closing, every time.

If the deed isn't recorded, you own the property in theory but not in the public record. That's a problem if there's ever a dispute over ownership.

## What happens when the deal falls apart

I've been through one deal that fell apart at the appraisal stage. The property appraised $18,000 below the contract price. The seller wouldn't come down. I terminated within the appraisal contingency window and got my earnest money back — but it took 11 days and three phone calls to the escrow agent.

The lesson: the legal right to terminate is one thing. Getting your money back is another. The contract should specify how and when the deposit is returned. In my case, the contract said "promptly," which the escrow agent interpreted as "within 30 days." It came back in 12.

If a deal falls apart over something more contentious — a seller who claims you breached the contract, or an escrow agent who won't release funds without both parties' signatures — you may need to escalate. That's where the legal process shifts from transactional to adversarial. It's not unlike the situations I've written about in [what to do if you're being sued](/posts/what-to-do-if-youre-being-sued-a-first-time-defendants-guide/) — the earlier you get a lawyer involved, the more options you have.

## The honest caveats

Two things I want to be clear about.

First, the process varies enormously by state. Attorney review periods are mandated in some states (New Jersey, New York, and a handful of others) and unheard of in others. Title insurance is customary in some states and rarely used in others — in Iowa, for example, title insurance is largely replaced by a state-run abstract system. Closing practices differ: some states use escrow companies, some use attorneys, some use title companies. My experience is not universal.

Second, the cost of getting it wrong is asymmetric. The downside of skipping attorney review is not "you save $500." It's "you buy a house with a lien, an unpermitted addition, or a clause that lets the seller keep the appliances." The downside of an owner's title policy is not "you save $2,000." It's "you pay $40,000 to defend a title claim." The math is not close.

The third caveat is that a real estate attorney is not a substitute for your own reading. In both of my purchases, I read every document before signing. The attorney caught things I missed. I caught things the attorney missed — because I was the one who had walked the property, read the inspection report, and knew what I was actually buying.

## A practical checklist I now use

Before you sign anything:

- Get a flat-fee quote from a real estate attorney (expect $300–$800 for a standard review in 2026)
- Read the entire purchase agreement, not just the price and closing date
- Identify every contingency and its deadline
- Confirm the earnest money amount, holder, and return conditions
- If there's an HOA, request the full document package and review it
- Budget for owner's title insurance (roughly 0.5%–1% of purchase price)

Before closing:

- Compare the closing disclosure to the loan estimate line by line
- Confirm the title commitment has no exceptions you don't understand
- Verify the deed description matches the property you inspected
- Confirm the closing disclosure was delivered at least three business days before closing

After closing:

- Verify the deed was recorded with the county recorder
- File the title insurance policy somewhere you'll find it
- Keep the closing disclosure and all loan documents together

If you're also juggling paperwork on the rental side — breaking a lease, getting a deposit back, or moving out of an apartment — the logic is similar but the rules are different. My guide to [how to read a lease agreement before signing](/posts/how-to-read-lease-agreement-before-signing/) covers the rental equivalent of this checklist.

One last thing: if you're drafting anything yourself — a repair addendum, a letter to the seller, a request for documents — run it through a plain word count before you hit send. The [word counter](https://word-counter.search123.top/) on our site is what I use to make sure I'm not sending a two-page letter when a three-paragraph one would do. Shorter is almost always better in real estate correspondence, and attorneys bill by the hour.
