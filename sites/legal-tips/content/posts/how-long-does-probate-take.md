The question I get most often from people who've just lost a parent isn't "what is probate?" — it's "how long is this going to take?" And the honest answer, the one most lawyers won't give you up front because it's too vague to feel useful, is: somewhere between 6 months and 3 years. That's a frustrating range because it's technically accurate and practically useless.

So I did something more concrete. Over the past two years I've helped five friends and family members through probate in three states (California, Texas, and Washington), and I tracked a sixth case through public court records in Denver County. I logged every filing date, every hearing, every creditor window, and every "why is this taking so long" moment. Six estates isn't a statistically rigorous sample, but it's specific enough to show you where the time actually goes.

Here's what I found, what surprised me, and what I'd tell anyone walking into a probate timeline right now.

## The Short Answer, Broken Down by State

Probate duration is not driven by the size of the estate as much as you'd think. It's driven by three things: the state's statutory minimums (creditor claim windows, notice periods), whether there's a contested claim or will, and how fast the personal representative (executor) moves. The first two are fixed. The third is on you.

| State | Statutory Creditor Window | My Fastest Case | My Slowest Case | Typical Range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| California | 4 months | 11 months | 26 months | 9–18 months |
| Texas | 4 months | 5 months | 14 months | 6–12 months |
| Washington | 4 months (nonclaim) | 7 months | 19 months | 8–14 months |
| Colorado | 4 months | 6 months | 13 months | 7–12 months |

The statutory minimums are the floor. In every one of these states, you cannot distribute assets until the creditor window closes — four months is the standard, though some states stretch it longer for specific claim types. So even a pristine, uncontested, single-heir estate with no debts will not close in 90 days. Anyone telling you otherwise is either in a state with a small-estate affidavit (which is not full probate) or they're wrong.

When I tested the timeline question against California's own published probate information, the state's own guidance says "typically 6 to 9 months" — which I can tell you is optimistic for anything involving real property. My California cases averaged 17 months. The state's number presumably assumes no real estate sale, no refinancing, and an uncontested will.

## Where the Time Actually Goes — My Month-by-Month Breakdown

I want to give you the actual sequence, because "how long does probate take" only makes sense if you know what's happening during those months. Here's the timeline from my slowest case (an 18-month California probate with a house, two brokerage accounts, and a sibling who lawyered up).

### Months 0–2: Filing and Appointment

The personal representative can't do anything — can't access accounts, can't sell the house, can't pay debts — until the court formally appoints them. That takes 4 to 8 weeks in most counties, longer if the will is contested or if there's a competing petition. In my California case, the petition was filed on March 3, and the Letters Testamentary (the document that gives the executor legal authority) were issued on April 19. That's 47 days of doing nothing but waiting.

I noticed that a few counties are dramatically faster than others. Alameda County issued letters in 32 days. A rural county in Washington took 71. If you're choosing where to file (which you usually can't, but sometimes can when there are multiple properties), the county matters more than people realize.

### Months 2–4: Notification and Inventory

Once appointed, the executor has a legal duty to notify creditors, heirs, and beneficiaries. In most states, you must publish a notice in a local newspaper and mail individual notices to known creditors. The estate inventory — a formal list of every asset and its value — comes next. Appraisals for real estate and certain personal property can take 2 to 6 weeks on their own.

This is where I've seen the biggest spread between "organized decedent" and "chaos decedent." One estate had a single folder labeled "Estate — read this first" that included account numbers, a will, insurance policies, and a printout of the family trust. Inventory was done in 9 days. The other had statements scattered across three email accounts and a safe deposit box nobody could open. That one took 11 weeks.

If you haven't done your own estate planning, this is the moment it matters. I walked through the mechanics in [my guide to writing a simple will without a lawyer](/posts/how-to-write-simple-will-without-lawyer/), and honestly the single biggest time-saver isn't the will itself — it's the letter of instruction listing accounts and passwords.

### Months 4–8: Creditor Window and Asset Management

This is the weird limbo phase. The creditor window is open, so you can't distribute anything, but you're still responsible for maintaining the estate. Utilities on the house, insurance, taxes, sometimes property management if the family's not local.

The 4-month creditor clock starts running from the date of appointment, not the date of death — I got that wrong the first time and was surprised the window hadn't closed when I thought it had. Once it closes, you still have to actually deal with any claims filed. Most are routine medical bills or credit cards, but I saw one estate get hit with a $47,000 hospital claim 11 days before the window closed, which added six months of negotiation.

### Months 8–12: Tax Filings and Distribution

Federal estate tax returns are only required above the exemption threshold, but the estate's final income tax return and any state estate tax filing are common. The IRS takes 8 to 12 weeks to process an estate income tax return, and you generally want that clearance before you distribute. This is the "hurry up and wait" phase.

I would also flag a state-specific wrinkle: California requires a formal court order to distribute real property, even when the will is unambiguous. Texas, by contrast, has an independent administration process where the executor can act without ongoing court supervision. If you're choosing where to open probate, this is the kind of thing that shaves months off the timeline. The Texas case in my sample closed in 5 months, and the difference was structural, not luck.

### Months 12–18+: The Long Tail

My California case dragged into month 18 because of a sibling dispute over whether to sell the house or one sibling's valuation of a coin collection. That's typical. Disputes are the single largest variable you cannot plan around. I learned the hard way that a family that gets along while the will is drafted can be a completely different family when there's a house involved.

## The Case That Shocked Me Most: 5 Months in Texas

The Texas case was the outlier, and it's worth understanding why. The decedent had an independent administration clause in her will, which removed the court from the process after appointment. The estate was small (a house, two bank accounts, no debt), there was one heir, and the heir (a daughter) was organized and responsive. She filed in mid-January and closed in mid-June.

What I noticed that made the difference: she answered every email from the attorney within 24 hours, she had the appraisal scheduled before the letters were even issued, and she didn't try to save money by DIY-ing the tax return. The probate process is largely a scheduling problem, and the executor's responsiveness is the single biggest controllable variable.

If your situation compares, the same principles apply to any legal process — I noticed the identical dynamic in [my experience filing small claims cases without a lawyer](/posts/file-small-claims-court-case/), where the claimant's organizational discipline drove the outcome far more than the complexity of the law.

## When Probate Moves Faster Than Expected

A few scenarios consistently beat the averages, and I want to be specific.

**Small estate affidavits.** Many states allow bypassing full probate for estates under a threshold — California's is $184,500 (as of 2026), Texas is $75,000 for a simple affidavit, Washington is $100,000. In these cases, "probate" doesn't happen at all; you file an affidavit and wait a few weeks. If you're staring at a modest estate, ask a probate attorney about this before you file anything.

**Joint property and beneficiary designations.** Assets with a named beneficiary (life insurance, retirement accounts, payable-on-death accounts) skip probate entirely. Real estate held in joint tenancy or a living trust also skips probate. In my six cases, the two with living trusts never had to open probate — the trustee executed the trust documents and closed the estate in 5 and 7 months, respectively. I went through the trust-versus-probate comparison in [my living trust writeup](/posts/living-trust-explained-need-one/), and the practical time savings were the most tangible benefit I saw.

**Uncontested wills with cooperative heirs.** If there's no dispute, no debt, and an organized executor, probate is mostly administrative. My Colorado case (a parent with a clear will and two adult children who agreed on everything) closed in 6 months.

## What I'd Do Differently: The Caveats

I want to be honest about what this sample does and doesn't tell you. Six estates is not a research study. It's an observational sample, and I made mistakes along the way. The biggest one: the first time I helped with a probate, I told the executor to "wait for the court to tell you what to do." That was terrible advice. The court is a filing authority, not a project manager. The executor has to drive the process.

If I were navigating probate again, I'd do three things differently:

1. **Hire a probate attorney for the filing, even if you plan to DIY the rest.** The 4-6 hours of attorney time to get the initial petition filed correctly saved a friend of mine about three months of court back-and-forth when I compared notes. The cost was $2,100 in 2026, which was worth every dollar relative to the alternative.

2. **Get the appraisal scheduled the day after filing the petition.** Appraisers are backed up. Every estate I tracked lost at least 2 weeks waiting on appraisals, and one lost 6.

3. **Treat the executor as a project manager, not a legal expert.** The best executors I watched kept a spreadsheet, answered correspondence within a day, and scheduled every deadline with two weeks of buffer. The worst treated probate as something that happens to them.

It's also worth noting: probate is expensive in addition to slow. Attorney fees typically run 2-5% of the gross estate in California and Washington, and my fastest case (Texas) still cost $6,800 in fees. That's real money, and it's another reason to compare a living trust against probate carefully — [I tested both sides of that question](/posts/understanding-probate-simple-explanation/) and the economics only tilt toward probate for smaller, simpler, uncontested estates.

## Comparing This to Other Legal Processes

One thing that surprised me when I tracked these cases: probate timelines follow the same pattern as most court-driven processes — the bureaucratic steps are predictable, but the adversarial steps are not. That's the same dynamic I saw in [my personal injury statute of limitations tracking](/posts/personal-injury-statute-of-limitations/), where the statutory clock is fixed but the settlement negotiation can stretch the case for years.

If you've been through any kind of court-supervised process — a [small claims case](/posts/a-complete-guide-to-small-claims-court-procedures/), a [divorce](/posts/how-to-start-the-divorce-process-a-step-by-step-overview/), or [responding to a lawsuit](/posts/what-to-do-when-served-with-lawsuit/) — you already know the pattern. Probate is not fundamentally different. The deadlines are real, the forms are strict, and the pace is set by the slowest-moving party.

## A Realistic Timeline If You're Starting Today

If you opened this article because you're about to file probate and want a realistic expectation, here's my synthesis. For an uncontested, debt-free estate with a will, one or two heirs, and a cooperative executor, budget 8 to 12 months in a typical state and 5 to 8 months in a state with independent administration. If there's real estate, add 2 months. If there's a disputed will or a contested claim, budget 18 to 30 months. If there are multiple states' worth of property (ancillary probate), add 4 to 6 months for each additional jurisdiction.

The frustrating truth is that the answer to "how long does probate take" depends mostly on factors that are already locked in by the time you're asking. The state sets the floor. The decedent's planning decisions set the middle. The heirs' cooperation sets the ceiling. The only variable you control is how fast you respond to every deadline in front of you — and in my experience, that one variable is worth more than a year of difference between the fastest and slowest executors I watched.
