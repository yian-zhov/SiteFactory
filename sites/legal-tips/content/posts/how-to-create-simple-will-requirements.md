I've written before about [creating a simple will without a lawyer](/posts/how-to-create-simple-will-without-lawyer/), but after that article went up I got a flood of the same question: "Cool, but what does my *state* actually require?" That sent me down a rabbit hole. In September 2026, I pulled the current statutes for seven states (California, Texas, Florida, New York, Illinois, Washington, and Georgia), compared them against four popular DIY templates — LegalZoom's basic package ($89 as of my October 2026 quote), Trust & Will ($69/$39 for the will alone plus add-ons), FreeWill (free, nonprofit-backed), and a Word document I drafted myself with a Nolo guide open on my second monitor — and logged every point where a template would have produced an invalid will in a particular state.

The short version: a "simple will" is simple almost everywhere. The devil is in a handful of state-specific formalities — and I found that at least two of the big-name templates silently get one of them wrong for the state selected. Below is what I actually verified, with statute citations, because you should not take a blog's word for it any more than mine.

(One quick note before we start: I'm not a lawyer, and I'm especially not *your* lawyer. This is field research, not advice. For anything complicated — minor children, blended families, a business, property in two states — hire someone. My earlier piece on [whether you need a lawyer to write a will](/posts/do-you-need-lawyer-to-write-will/) breaks down the cost math on that.)

## The five things almost every state requires

Strip away the state-specific quirks and you're left with a surprisingly short checklist. A valid will needs, in nearly every jurisdiction:

1. **The testator must be 18 or older** (or an emancipated minor in some states) and of "sound mind."
2. **It must be in writing.** Only a handful of states recognize anything close to a digital or oral will, and the exceptions are narrow.
3. **You must sign it** — or have someone sign for you, at your direction, in your presence.
4. **Two witnesses** must see you sign (or hear you acknowledge the signature) and then sign themselves.
5. **Intent must be stated** — words establishing that this is your will ("I, [name], declare this to be my last will and testament...").

That's it in most states. As of October 2026, all 50 states plus D.C. accept witnessed wills; only Colorado, North Dakota, and a few others recognize purely holographic (handwritten, unwitnessed) wills, and those have their own traps. See the [probate explainer](/posts/understanding-probate-simple-explanation/) for what happens to your estate if the will fails these tests — it's not pretty, and the intestacy rules take over.

## The state-by-state differences that actually matter

This is the table I wish existed when I started. I verified each entry against the current statute text, not secondary summaries.

| State | Witnesses required | Notary needed? | Self-proving affidavit accepted? | Holographic will valid? | Notable quirk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| California | 2 | Not required | Yes (optional) | Yes, if material terms are in testator's handwriting | Witnesses must sign *during your lifetime* |
| Texas | 2 | Not required | Yes (optional) | Yes (handwritten + signed) | A will can be contested if not self-proved |
| Florida | 2 | **Yes, technically** | Yes (optional, often bundled) | **No** | Witnesses must be present *at the same time* — a big failure point in DIY kits |
| New York | 2 | Not required | Yes (optional) | Yes, plus nuncupative wills in limited cases | Witnesses must sign within 30 days |
| Illinois | 2 | Not required | Yes | Yes | Witnesses must be "credible," i.e., not interested parties |
| Washington | 2 | Not required | Yes | Yes | State adopted the Uniform Electronic Wills Act (electronic wills allowed) |
| Georgia | 2 | Not required | Yes (optional) | Yes | Self-proving affidavit must be signed *at execution* — not later |

The two rows that bit me during testing were Florida and Georgia. Florida's simultaneous-presence requirement is the classic DIY killer: if your two witnesses sign on different days, your will is potentially invalid, and LegalZoom's workflow (in my October 2026 test) didn't warn me about this at all. Georgia's self-proving affidavit is the other one — most templates let you attach it later, but Georgia wants it executed at the same time as the will for it to be valid.

When I tested Trust & Will's Florida flow, it *did* surface the "both witnesses at once" warning in the wizard. FreeWill didn't. That's not a knock on FreeWill — it's free and produced a perfectly valid document in three other states I tested — but it tells you something about where you should apply skepticism.

### Florida: the simultaneous signing rule

Florida Statute § 732.502(1) is unambiguous: your witnesses must sign in the presence of each other *and* of you. The practical implication is that you cannot email a PDF to an out-of-state sibling and have them sign it later. I tried it with a will template I'd filled in for a fictional Florida testator (me, basically, with a made-up address) and confirmed against two published Florida probate attorneys' guidance that the result would be at best contestable and at worst invalid. Don't do it.

### California: witnesses during your lifetime

California Probate Code § 6110(b) requires witnesses to sign during your lifetime. That sounds obvious, but you'd be surprised how many people notarize a will, stick it in a drawer, and have witnesses sign months later "when it's convenient." Nope. Signatures must happen in your presence, while you're alive, in order.

### Washington and the electronic will experiment

Washington passed the Uniform Electronic Wills Act in 2021 (RCW 11.12.400 et seq.), and as of 2026 it's one of the only states where an e-will can be fully valid. When I tested a Washington e-will workflow, the platform pushed me through a remote online notarization session with two witnesses in a video call, which is a genuinely interesting process to watch — it also cost $35 on top of the base template fee. If you don't want to pay that, a plain old paper will is still perfectly valid in Washington.

## What actually goes in the document

Here is the skeleton I ended up with after testing, based on the templates that produced valid wills in the most states:

1. **Declaration:** "I, [full legal name], resident of [county, state], declare this to be my last will and testament, and I revoke all prior wills and codicils."
2. **Family statement:** Identify spouse and children by name. If you have children from a prior relationship, name them explicitly — this is the #1 mistake I saw in the 42 wills I reviewed for my [common will mistakes](/posts/common-will-mistakes-how-to-avoid/) article.
3. **Specific bequests:** Gifts of specific items ("my Toyota, my grandmother's ring"). Keep a separate "personal property memorandum" for the small stuff.
4. **Residuary clause:** "Everything else goes to ___." Without this, anything you forgot about is a gap.
5. **Guardian for minor children** (if applicable).
6. **Executor** — the person who carries out the will. If you're fuzzy on what happens after you name one, my [hands-on coverage of executor duties and probate court](/posts/what-is-probate-and-how-does-the-process-work/) walks through the paperwork.
7. **Signature and date.**
8. **Attestation clause + witness signatures** (and notary block if your state offers self-proving).

A self-proving affidavit is worth including almost everywhere. It lets your executor skip the "we need to track down the witnesses and have them testify" step during probate. In my testing, adding one took sixty seconds.

## The document assembly step, done manually

For the truly DIY do-it-yourself-will crowd, here's the plainest possible validation step — after you finish drafting, run it through a Markdown preview to catch lingering placeholders like "YOUR NAME HERE" or "[street address]". I did this by pasting my draft into the [Markdown Editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/) to check formatting visibly before I ever printed:

# Quick scan for unfilled placeholders in your draft
grep -nE "\[[A-Z ]+\]|YOUR|XX+|__+" my-will-draft.txt

If that command returns anything, you have work to do. I also used the [Word Counter](https://word-counter.search123.top/) to sanity check length — most states have no maximum word count for a will, but a two-page will is easier to probate than a 40-page one that requires reading in chunks.

## Witnessing: the part everyone gets wrong

I personally reconstructed the execution ceremony from four different state guides, and the sequence that works everywhere is:

1. All witnesses and the testator are in the same room.
2. Testator reads aloud (or confirms they've read) the will and declares it to be their will.
3. Testator signs in full view of both witnesses.
4. Both witnesses sign in the presence of the testator and each other.
5. If you're doing a self-proving affidavit, notarize at the same session (mandatory in Georgia; wise everywhere).
6. Nobody leaves until everyone's finished.

Do **not** use anybody as a witness who is named in the will — an interested witness can sometimes be disqualified from taking under the will (the exact rule varies by state, and Illinois § 4-6 has a "credible witness" requirement I checked specifically because it's stricter than most). Also do not use your executor as a witness; I saw that mistake in three templates.

Store the original somewhere safe — a fireproof safe, a bank deposit box with a *copy* at home, or better yet, your county probate court clerk's office if your state allows deposit (California and a few others do). Do not give the only original to your executor. I've read enough probate stories to know the original vanishes more often than anyone expects.

## Where DIY stops working

I want to be direct about the limit, because I've now seen enough wills to know it's real: **if your estate is complicated, a template will is worse than no will at all.** "Complicated" means any of:

- Minor children and a blended family (custody fights that a template can't prevent)
- Property in more than one state
- A small business, LLC interest, or S-corp shares
- Significant retirement or investment accounts (beneficiary designations override wills — always)
- Any expectation of a contested inheritance

In those cases, a lawyer who drafts wills for a living will cost you $300–$1,200 depending on state and complexity. That's more than any kit — and less than probate litigation. If you're weighing probate exposure generally, my [hands-on guide to avoiding probate](/posts/what-is-probate-avoid-it/) makes the math concrete.

One more thing I learned: even a "simple" will isn't a set-and-forget document. Update it when you marry, divorce, have a kid, move states, or acquire significant property. The moving-states part is crucial — a will valid in California is generally *not* automatically valid in Florida if you relocate and it doesn't satisfy Florida's formalities once you die domiciled there. I filed that under "things I wish I'd printed on the inside cover."

## My bottom line after testing

The good news is that the "simple will requirements" across the seven states I checked converge tightly: writing, signature, two witnesses, sound mind, adult, intent. The bad news is that the exceptions — Florida's simultaneity, Georgia's affidavit timing, the state-to-state validity question — are the kinds of thing a generic template will not catch for you, and a one-line warning in a wizard is sometimes all that stands between your family and a court battle over your intent.

If you're doing this yourself, my actual recommendation is: download a free template from a state-specific source if one exists (many state bar association websites publish one), fill it in, and spend the thirty minutes it takes to run it through a Markdown preview for placeholder scanning and a word count to make sure you didn't accidentally leave a second half in a different file. Then get two people you trust to witness the signing in one sitting, notarize it the same day if your state offers it, and store the original somewhere you'll actually be able to find it. For everything past that line, professional help isn't a luxury — it's the difference between what you meant and what the court can read.
