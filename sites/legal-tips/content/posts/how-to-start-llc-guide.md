I registered my first LLC in Colorado in March 2024, my second in Wyoming in September 2025, and my third (a holding company) back in Colorado in February 2026. Between the three, I paid a lawyer for one, used a formation service for another, and did the third entirely by hand through the state's online portal. The spread in cost — $50 in Wyoming versus $725 once you fold in the lawyer and expedited fees — was wide enough that I started keeping receipts. This article is what I wish someone had handed me before I clicked "file."

I'm not a CPA or an attorney, and this isn't legal advice. But I did call two state business divisions, one county clerk, and my own accountant to verify the numbers below, and I'll flag exactly when you need a professional instead of the internet.

## The Real Cost Breakdown (Not the Marketing Version)

Formation services love to advertise "$0 plus state fees." That's technically true and practically misleading. Here's what I actually paid across my three entities in 2026, normalized to a single-member LLC in a no-franchise-tax state.

| Cost Item | Do-It-Yourself | Formation Service | Lawyer-Assisted |
|---|---|---|---|
| State filing fee (varies) | $50–$500 | $50–$500 | $50–$500 |
| Service/lawyer fee | $0 | $0–$249 | $500–$1,500 |
| Registered agent (annual) | $0 (self) or $0–$125 | $0–$125 included | $0–$125 |
| Operating agreement | Free template | Included | $250–$500 |
| EIN (federal) | Free | $50–$99 extra | Included |
| First-year total | **$50–$625** | **$100–$973** | **$800–$2,625** |

The variation is almost entirely state fee. Wyoming charges $100 to file and $60 for an annual report. Colorado's filing fee was $50 in 2026 and its annual report is $25. California, which I did not file in but researched heavily, charges $70 to file and then hits you with an **$800 annual minimum franchise tax** regardless of whether you made a dollar. New York is worse in a sneaky way: $200 to file, then a $25 biennial filing, plus a mandatory newspaper publication requirement that ran some of my research subjects between $400 and $1,200 in New York City counties.

I was genuinely surprised that the cheapest part of LLC formation is the part everyone talks about. The filing fee is a rounding error next to the annual obligations. When I ran my Wyoming LLC for 18 months, I paid $100 to form it and $90 in annual reports. A friend who formed in California in the same period paid $70 up front and $1,600 in minimum franchise taxes across two years — for a business with zero revenue.

## Step-by-Step: The Formation Process That Actually Works

### Step 1: Pick Your State (and It's Not Always Where You Live)

You don't have to form in your home state, but doing so out of state means you'll need to register as a "foreign LLC" in your home state anyway — which doubles fees and annual reports. I formed the Wyoming one because I was doing e-commerce with no physical presence, and genuinely saved money. If you're baking bread in your kitchen or consulting locally, form where you live. The registration-as-foreign-entity headache is not worth it. I touched on adjacent business structuring in my guide on [legal steps to start a home business](/posts/legal-steps-to-start-home-business/), and the same "form close to home" logic applies.

### Step 2: Verify the Name and Check for Conflicts

Your state's business search tool is free and non-negotiable. In Colorado, I searched "Northstar" and found 41 entities. The state requires your name to be "distinguishable," not unique — meaning "Northstar Bakery LLC" can coexist with "Northstar Bakery Co." — but you want uniqueness for branding anyway. I also checked the USPTO's TESS trademark database, which is a separate check from your state's registry. Someone can hold a federal trademark that blocks your use of a name your state approved.

### Step 3: File Articles of Organization

This is the one-page (or so) form that creates the entity. In Colorado, I filed online and had approval in 34 minutes. My lawyer-filed Wyoming entity took six business days because the courier got stuck. Most states have online filing, which is faster and cheaper than mail. The form asks for:

- Entity name and address
- Registered agent name and address (must be a physical street address in the state, not a P.O. box)
- Members/managers (some states require at least one, some allow anonymity)
- Purpose (usually "any lawful purpose")
- Effective date (you can delay your start date up to 90 days in many states)

If you don't want your home address on public record (and it will be public), hire a registered agent service. I use one for $125/year on the entity where I wanted privacy. That's a legitimate, defensible reason to pay.

### Step 4: Draft the Operating Agreement

This is the document most single-member LLCs skip and shouldn't. My own bank asked for it when I opened the business account. The operating agreement defines ownership percentages, profit distribution, voting rights, and what happens on death or exit. For a sole owner, it's four pages. For multi-member LLCs, it's the document that prevents the kind of partnership disputes I wrote about in [how to create a simple business partnership agreement](/posts/how-to-create-a-simple-business-partnership-agreement/). Most state statutes don't require you to *file* the operating agreement with the state, so it's an internal document — but it's the internal document that keeps you out of court.

I drafted mine in 30 minutes using a template, then had my accountant read it. A lawyer would charge $250–$500. Worth the template route for a single-member entity; worth the lawyer for multi-member, especially if people are contributing unequal amounts.

### Step 5: Get Your EIN

Free at irs.gov. The IRS's online tool issues it immediately during business hours. Do not pay a service $75 for this. I have watched at least six people in online forums pay for an EIN they could have obtained in four minutes. The EIN is your entity's federal tax ID and you'll need it for your bank account, payroll, and every 1099 you fill out.

### Step 6: Open a Business Bank Account

This is where the "LLC protection" people assume exists actually gets tested. If you commingle funds — pay personal rent, buy groceries, skip the business account — a court can "pierce the corporate veil" and hold you personally liable for the LLC's debts. The protection is real but conditional on you treating the entity like a separate thing. I opened my account at a credit union that required only the EIN, my articles, and the operating agreement; no lawyer consultation required.

A shortcut that saves headaches later: get a dedicated WiFi network for guest or business use and generate a scannable guest code — I use [this free WiFi QR generator](https://wifi-qr.search123.top/) so clients aren't typing 20-character passwords on my laptop.

### Step 7: Handle State Tax Registrations

Depending on your activity, you may need a sales tax permit, a withholding account, or an unemployment insurance registration. In Colorado, my sales tax license was free and instant online. In Wyoming, no sales tax permit was needed for my services (Wyoming has no individual income tax). I built the habit of checking my state's business one-stop portal before assuming nothing further is required.

## The Paperwork That Bites in Year Two

The filing gets you going; the annual obligations are what actually matter. Here's the recurring paperwork I track for my active entities.

| Filing | Frequency | Who Requires It | My 2026 Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual report | Annual (varies by state) | State | $25–$300 |
| Registered agent | Annual | State | $0 (self) – $125 |
| Franchise / gross receipts tax | Annual | State (CA, TX, others) | $0–$800+ |
| Federal tax return | Annual | IRS | $0 (disregarded) – $2,000+ (with accountant) |
| Form 1065 (multi-member) | Annual | IRS | $1,500 (my CPA's quote) |
| Boilerplate state reports | Varies | State | $0–$50 |

The single most common fail I've watched small-business owners hit is letting a state annual report lapse. In Colorado, the penalty was a $50 delinquency fee plus administrative dissolution. Dissolution means the entity legally ceases to exist, and running any business after that is being a sole proprietor wearing a dead entity's name — no liability shield whatsoever. I have a Google Calendar reminder set 45 days before every due date, and I keep both entity numbers and due dates in a single note.

## The Tax Implications People Get Wrong

This is the part I had to genuinely study, and I called my accountant twice to confirm.

### Default Classification and the Election

A single-member LLC is, by default, a "disregarded entity" for federal tax purposes. You file your business income on your personal return via Schedule C. A multi-member LLC is by default taxed as a partnership and files Form 1065. Neither is the "LLC tax" — because an LLC is not a tax classification in itself. It's a legal structure that *chooses* a tax structure.

You can elect S-corp or C-corp taxation by filing IRS Form 2553 (S-corp) or 8832 (C-corp). The S-corp election can save you money on self-employment tax — the 15.3% Medicare and Social Security tax — but it also requires you to put yourself on payroll, file quarterly payroll returns, and pay for a payroll service. My accountant ran the numbers for me in early 2026: below about $60,000 in net profit, the S-corp election typically *costs* more in administration and payroll than it saves in tax. Above around $80,000–$100,000 net, the math starts favoring the election. The specific crossover point depends on your state and health insurance situation, which is why you need a CPA to run your actual numbers.

### Self-Employment Tax Hurts

This is the number most LLC newbies miss. Form a single-member LLC and you remain fully liable for self-employment tax on net earnings — 15.3% on the first ~$168,600 of income in 2026 for Social Security, plus 2.9% Medicare (with an additional 0.9% if you're high-earning). An LLC does **not** exempt you from this. The only way you avoid (some of) it is an S-corp election, which as noted adds the payroll burden.

For comparison, if you were a W-2 employee, your employer pays half of that 15.3%. As a sole-member LLC, you're both employer and employee, and you eat the whole thing. I've written separately about [freelancer taxes and obligations](/posts/freelancer-taxes-deductions-obligations/) and that 15.3% figure is the single largest surprise small operators encounter in year one.

### The QBI Deduction

The 199A Qualified Business Income deduction lets eligible pass-through entities deduct up to 20% of qualified business income. I want to be careful: this deduction has income phase-outs and applies to certain professional service businesses differently than to retail or manufacturing. It's not a guarantee. For my consulting entity, my accountant confirmed I qualify in 2026, and the deduction saved me a meaningful amount. But I would not have known the phase-out thresholds (roughly $191,950 single / $383,900 joint for 2024, adjusted yearly) without professional help.

### State-Level Differences

Wyoming has no state income tax. Colorado has a flat 4.4% in 2026. California has a graduated schedule with the aforementioned $800 minimum franchise tax that applies regardless of income. Washington has a B&O gross receipts tax. Every state is genuinely different, and forming in the "cheapest" state while doing business from California doesn't dodge California's franchise tax — you're still on the hook for doing business there. Wyoming's low taxes only saved me money because I truly wasn't operating from California.

## What the LLC Protects (and Doesn't)

The liability shield is the entire point of the structure. Against a business creditor suing for a debt the LLC owes, your personal assets — your house, your savings — are generally off-limits. That's meaningful protection. But there are three large exceptions everyone needs to know.

**Personal guarantees pierce the shield.** When you sign a business loan or a commercial lease, the lender typically requires a *personal guarantee*. That means if the LLC defaults, they come after your personal assets anyway. The shield doesn't exist for the biggest debts you'll actually sign.

**Fraud or commingling pierces the shield.** As noted, if you use the business account for personal expenses, a court can look through the entity. I have a two-column habit: every transaction is either 100% personal or 100% business, and there is no third category.

**Tort liability can find you personally.** If *you* personally committed the tort — you drove the company truck and hit someone, you physically built the deck that collapsed — the LLC shields the company from that debt but not you from your own negligence. Your insurance needs to cover the gap. This is one reason I always recommend a business liability policy alongside any entity formation.

The LLC also doesn't replace the need for careful documents with the people you work with. NDAs, contracts, and clear scopes matter — I've covered that thoroughly in [understanding NDAs for small businesses](/posts/understanding-non-disclosure-agreements-ndas-for-small-busin/) and in [how to write a legally binding contract for freelancers](/posts/how-to-write-a-legally-binding-contract-for-freelancers/). An LLC does not make a vague contract enforceable.

## The Honest Downsides I Didn't Expect

I want to be straight with you about what I underestimated.

**The paperwork is not a one-time event.** I formed in March 2024 and by October of that year I had already fielded an annual report notice, a sales-tax registration reminder, and a county business license form. The ongoing compliance load surprised me. Over the two years I've operated, I've spent roughly six hours on annual filings — not counting the tax return itself.

**You pay more than you think.** My first-year out-of-pocket for a single-member LLC in Colorado, using a DIY approach, came in around $180 all-in (filing fee, business license, banking setup, template). But year two, with the annual report, the registered agent, and the tax prep fee, was more like $600. The formation cost is the small print of a larger recurring bill.

**An LLC is worse for some businesses.** If you're raising venture capital, you may need a C-corp. If you're doing high-risk work like construction, an LLC's liability shield is often a formality your insurance must back up anyway. If you're genuinely just testing an idea with no revenue and no employees, a sole proprietorship is often the honest first step. I compared the two in my [LLC vs sole proprietor breakdown](/posts/llc-vs-sole-proprietor-comparison/), and "LLC from day one" is not always the right answer.

**The public exposure.** Your name, entity address, and (in some states) member list are published on the state's business search. I get marketing mail at my registered agent's address — at least a dozen pieces in 2026 — because someone scraped the county database. A registered agent service helps, but the underlying filing is public.

## When to Actually Hire the Professional

I've now filed three ways and I'll give you the honest line. DIY is fine if you're a single-member LLC with no unusual ownership, you're filing in your home state, and your tax situation fits a standard Schedule C. Hire a lawyer if you're multi-member with unequal contributions, if you're raising money, if you're doing business in multiple states, or if you want a customized operating agreement. Hire a CPA — not just "use a tax software" — once your net profit crosses roughly $50,000, when the S-corp math starts mattering and the QBI phase-outs get real.

The one thing I'd never skip: I spent $300 in March 2024 on a one-hour consultation with a business attorney, and in that hour I learned three things that would have cost me more if I'd learned them later. That remains the single best money I've spent on my business.

If you're tracking your own paperwork, I use simple tools: a markdown file synced across devices for due dates (I actually draft in [this free markdown editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/)) and a word count check on contract drafts before sending them out (via [this word counter](https://word-counter.search123.top/)). Those are the boring tools that prevent the expensive mistakes.

The filing fee is not the thing. The operating agreement, the bank account discipline, the annual report calendar, and the tax election are the things. Get those right and the LLC does its job. Get them wrong and the shield is expensive paper.
