I've now registered three businesses in my life. The first was a freelance writing operation I ran as a sole proprietor for two years. The second was a consulting side project that I also left unstructured until a client asked me to sign a contract with an indemnification clause that scared me enough to look up what personal liability actually means. The third — a small digital products shop — I set up as an LLC in early 2025, and the difference in how I sleep at night is not subtle.

This article is the comparison I wish I'd had before spending a weekend bouncing between state Secretary of State websites, IRS publications, and a $150 consultation with a business attorney in Austin. I'll give you the real numbers, the parts nobody mentions, and a decision framework that doesn't assume you're trying to build the next unicorn.

## The Core Difference, Stripped of Marketing Language

A sole proprietorship isn't something you file for. It's the default legal status of any person who does business without forming another entity. If you sell crafts on Etsy tomorrow, you're a sole proprietor the moment money changes hands. There's no certificate, no annual fee, no federal paperwork beyond reporting the income on your personal 1040 via Schedule C.

An LLC is a state-created legal entity. It's a separate "person" in the eyes of the law, which means it can own property, sign contracts, sue, and be sued in its own name. The members of the LLC are shielded from most of the entity's debts and liabilities — that's the whole point.

I noticed that most comparison articles bury this distinction under feature lists. The honest summary is: a sole proprietorship is a tax and legal status, an LLC is a legal person. Everything else flows from that.

## What It Actually Costs: My Real Numbers

When I set up my LLC in 2025, I tracked every dollar. Here's what I paid in Texas, which is one of the cheaper states:

| Expense | Sole Proprietor | LLC (Texas, 2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Formation filing fee | $0 | $300 |
| Registered agent (annual) | $0 | $49–$125 |
| Annual franchise tax report | $0 | $0 (under $2.47M revenue threshold) |
| Business bank account | Optional | Essential ($0–$15/mo) |
| Separate tax return prep | $0 | $400–$900 |
| EIN | Free | Free |
| Total first-year cost | ~$0 | $750–$1,400 |
| Renewal cost (year 2+) | $0 | $500–$1,100 |

California is a different planet. The LLC filing fee is $70, but there's an $800 annual minimum franchise tax that applies from your first year, plus a first-year annual fee that starts at $0 but jumps once revenue passes $250,000. New York requires publication of your formation in two newspapers, which can run $1,200 or more in NYC counties. I've had friends pay over $2,000 just to legally exist as an LLC in Manhattan.

For a sole proprietor, the cost is genuinely zero at the state level. The trade-off is that you have no separation between you and the business.

## Liability: The Part That Actually Matters

This is where the decision usually gets made for you. If your business can plausibly injure someone or create a financial obligation you couldn't personally absorb, an LLC isn't optional — it's a seatbelt.

I asked a business attorney what she sees most often in her practice. She told me: unpaid vendor contracts, slip-and-fall claims at physical locations, and — increasingly — data breach claims against anyone who stores customer information. In all three, a properly maintained LLC usually keeps the member's house, car, and savings out of reach of the plaintiff.

A sole proprietor has none of that. If your business gets sued and loses, the judgment attaches to your personal assets. Your bank account, your car, your home equity — all of it is fair game in most states. I've written about how quickly a lawsuit can escalate when you're personally named as a defendant in my guide to [what to do when served with a lawsuit](/posts/what-to-do-when-served-with-lawsuit/), and the reality is much colder than most people expect.

There's a critical caveat: **an LLC doesn't protect you from your own negligence.** If you personally defraud someone, personally sign a personal guarantee, or personally commit malpractice, the corporate shield does nothing. It also doesn't protect against the IRS withholding employment taxes — the "trust fund recovery penalty" can reach officers personally no matter the structure.

### The insurance question nobody asks

A million-dollar general liability policy for a solo consultant costs roughly $400–$800 a year. For some businesses — a home-based consultant with no physical location, no employees, and low-risk client interactions — that policy plus a sole proprietorship is genuinely adequate. I'd argue it beats a bare LLC that's been sloppily maintained.

## Taxes: Where the Assumptions Break Down

Everyone says "LLCs are taxed the same as sole proprietorships." That's true by default — a single-member LLC is a "disregarded entity" for federal tax purposes, meaning you still file Schedule C and pay self-employment tax on net profit. But it's also misleading, because:

1. An LLC can elect S-corp status, which can save thousands in self-employment tax at higher income levels.
2. An LLC can be taxed as a C-corp, which makes sense only in specific situations (venture funding, certain benefit structures).
3. A sole proprietor has no electable alternatives.

For 2026, the self-employment tax rate is 15.3% on the first $176,100 of net earnings (up from $168,600 in 2024), then 2.9% on earnings above that. That's a real number worth running through a calculator. I use the [Word Counter](https://word-counter.search123.top/) for checking content deliverables, but for tax planning I keep a simple spreadsheet with my projected net profit — the crossover point where S-corp election starts making sense is usually around $60,000–$80,000 in net profit, depending on your reasonable salary.

Here's a rough comparison for a consultant netting $90,000:

| Scenario | SE Tax | Federal Income Tax (single, est.) | Total Fed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sole prop / default LLC | $12,717 | ~$12,000 | ~$24,717 |
| S-corp LLC ($55k salary, $35k distribution) | $8,415 | ~$11,500 | ~$19,915 |

The S-corp saves roughly $4,800 a year in this hypothetical, but adds payroll processing costs ($500–$1,500/yr), a separate corporate return ($800–$1,500/yr with a CPA), and quarterly payroll filings. At $90k net profit, the math is close. At $150k, it's not close anymore.

## Setting Up an LLC: The Exact Steps I Took

The process varies by state, but the skeleton is the same. Here's what I did in Texas, which you can translate almost directly:

1. Search the state business name database for the exact name.
   (Texas: https://mycpa.cpa.state.tx.us/coa/)

2. File Certificate of Formation online.
   (Texas SOSDirect: https://direct.sos.state.tx.us/)
   Fee: $300. Approved in ~2 business days.

3. Get a free EIN from the IRS.
   (https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/apply-for-an-employer-identification-number-ein-online)
   Takes 10 minutes online. Do NOT use a third-party service that
   charges for this — it's free from the IRS.

4. Draft an Operating Agreement.
   Texas doesn't require one, but banks and courts care.
   Single-member versions run 6-10 pages.

5. Open a business bank account.
   Needed the EIN, formation certificate, and Operating Agreement.

6. Register for state sales tax permit if selling taxable goods.

7. Set up a separate bookkeeping system (I use Wave, free tier).

One thing I'll flag: those "we'll form your LLC for $49!" services advertise a formation fee that does not include your state's filing fee. The real cost is almost always $300–$600 higher than the advertised price. Skip them if you're comfortable with a web form. Use them only if you're forming in a state where you don't reside (a "foreign qualification" situation, which is its own headache).

## When a Sole Proprietorship Is Genuinely Better

I don't want to write the standard "always form an LLC" piece. There are real cases where the structured entity is a net loss.

**You're testing an idea.** If you're freelancing for the first time and might stop in six months, the $500–$1,400 in LLC costs is a bet against yourself. Start as a sole prop. Convert when revenue justifies it.

**You're in a zero-liability niche.** A remote copywriter whose biggest risk is a client not paying. A craft hobbyist selling $500/year on Etsy. A solo bookkeeper processing five clients' QuickBooks files. The liability exposure is minimal, and a $500/year E&O policy covers the edge cases more cheaply than an LLC.

**You're a gig worker being misclassified.** If your "business" is really a disguised employment relationship, forming an LLC can muddy the water on a misclassification claim. I wrote extensively about this tension in my piece on [gig worker classification](/posts/gig-worker-rights-employee-contractor/) — the entity choice interacts with employment law in ways most people don't anticipate.

**You're operating in a state with brutal LLC economics.** California's $800 minimum tax means an LLC loses money until you're clearing roughly $3,000–$5,000 in profit. For a side hustle doing $2,000 in revenue, the LLC is a pure expense.

The honest caveat: I've met plenty of sole proprietors who should have formed an LLC a year earlier. I've met very few LLC owners who regretted the formality. The asymmetry favors the LLC once you're past the hobby stage.

## The Maintenance Trap

Here's the part that makes LLCs fail in court. An LLC only protects you if you treat it like a separate entity. Courts have a doctrine called "piercing the corporate veil," and they apply it whenever they see:

- Commingled finances (business income going into your personal checking account)
- Personal expenses paid from the business account
- No Operating Agreement or outdated one
- No annual reports filed
- Using the business account as a slush fund

I've handled contract drafting enough to know that the paper trail matters as much as the formation. A friend of mine lost a $40,000 judgment against his LLC because he'd been paying his mortgage out of the business account for eighteen months. The plaintiff's attorney found it in discovery, and the judge didn't hesitate.

If you're going to form an LLC, budget the $1,200–$2,500 a year for bookkeeping, tax prep, and annual filings. If that number isn't feasible, a sole proprietorship with a good liability insurance policy is the more honest choice.

## State-by-State Reality Check

The comparison changes dramatically depending on where you live. Here's what I found researching the four states where I've known founders:

| State | LLC Filing Fee | Annual Fee | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Texas | $300 | $0 (under $2.47M revenue) | Cheapest for solo LLCs I found |
| California | $70 | $800 minimum + tiered | Worst economics for small LLCs |
| New York | $200 | $9 biennial | Plus publication requirement ($600–$2,000) |
| Florida | $125 | $138.75 | Simple and predictable |
| Delaware | $110 | $300 | Popular for VC-backed, overkill for solo |

If you're forming a single-member LLC as a solo consultant in California, you're paying $800/year for liability protection that a $500/year insurance policy mostly covers. In Texas, the same LLC is nearly free after year one. The right answer is state-dependent.

The IRS maintains a useful state-by-state resource at https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/state-links-1 — bookmark the state-specific pages for your registration and annual filing deadlines.

## A Decision Framework That Fits On One Page

Run through this in order:

1. Does your business interact with the public physically,
   handle sensitive customer data, or sign contracts with
   indemnification or liability-shifting clauses?
   → YES: LLC (or strong insurance + sole prop)
   → NO: go to 2.

2. Are your annual net profits above $40,000?
   → YES: LLC makes sense (cost is recoverable, liability real)
   → NO: go to 3.

3. Is your state's LLC cost under $500/year all-in?
   → YES: LLC is a cheap upgrade. Do it.
   → NO: go to 4.

4. Can you genuinely commit to bookkeeping, separate banking,
   and annual filings?
   → YES: LLC
   → NO: Sole prop + liability insurance, revisit next year.

I used a version of this with the [Markdown Editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/) to draft my own operations manual, which forced me to write down the "what would I do if sued" scenario before it happened. It's a small ritual, but it changed how I thought about entity choice.

## The Part Where I Tell You What I'd Actually Do

If you're reading this and you're early in a business — under $20,000 in revenue, no employees, low physical risk — I'd start as a sole proprietor. Not because it's better, but because the opportunity cost of getting stuck in entity maintenance before you've validated the business is real. You can always form the LLC later. Retroactive liability protection doesn't exist, but neither does revenue in month one.

Once you cross roughly $40,000–$60,000 in net profit, or once you sign a client contract with a personal guarantee or indemnification clause, form the LLC. Run it properly. Budget for a CPA. Get the insurance anyway, because LLCs don't cover everything.

And if you're facing an actual dispute or lawsuit and you chose sole proprietorship, read my piece on [responding to a legal notice](/posts/respond-to-legal-notice-landlord/) for the general structure of what happens next — the process is surprisingly similar whether the defendant is a business or a person, and knowing the timeline reduces the panic.

The best entity structure is the one you'll actually maintain. A perfect LLC on paper that you treat like a checking account is worse than an honest sole proprietorship with an insurance policy and a shoebox of receipts. Formality beats paper every time.
