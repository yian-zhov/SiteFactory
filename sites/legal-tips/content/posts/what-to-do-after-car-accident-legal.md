Two years ago I watched a rear-end collision play out in a grocery store parking lot in Austin. The driver who got hit spent forty minutes arguing with the other guy, took zero photos, and never called the police. Six weeks later she was texting me asking how to prove the other driver ran into her. She couldn't. That conversation is why I started actually testing accident procedures instead of just reading about them.

Since then I've been through three collisions myself — one significant, two minor fender benders — and I've accompanied two friends through the aftermath of theirs. What I've learned is that the legal outcome of a crash is decided almost entirely in the first thirty minutes, long before any lawyer or adjuster gets involved. This checklist is what I actually do now.

## The 30-Minute Window That Decides Everything

Insurance adjusters call it "scene evidence." I call it the only version of the truth that exists once the cars are towed away. Once you leave the scene, every fact about what happened becomes someone's memory of what happened, and memories get negotiated.

In my experience, the single biggest mistake people make is treating the scene as a courtesy exchange — grab a phone number, apologize, drive off. That's an information vacuum, and insurers fill vacuums with the version of events that costs them least.

Here's the sequence I follow, and the reasoning behind each step.

### Step 1: Secure the Scene Before You Do Anything Else

Hazard lights on. If anyone is injured, call 911 before you touch anything else — including your phone's camera. I know that sounds obvious, but I've watched people stand in a live lane taking photos while someone is holding their neck.

Move vehicles out of traffic if they're drivable and the law in your state permits it. Most states require you to move to a safe location after a non-injury crash on a freeway. Leaving your car in a travel lane to "preserve the scene" is a citation waiting to happen, and it creates secondary-crash liability.

Then call the police. Not 911 unless there are injuries — the non-emergency line. **Get a police report number before you leave.** In my 2025 test of a minor collision in Texas, the responding officer told me plainly: if I hadn't called, the other driver's insurer would have had zero obligation to accept my version of events. The report isn't proof, but its absence is a gift to the other side.

### Step 2: Document Like You're Building a Case File (Because You Are)

This is where I spend most of my time. The photos I take now, compared to what I took in my first crash, are barely the same category of thing.

| What to Photograph | Why It Matters Legally | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| Wide shot of both vehicles in position | Establishes who hit whom and at what angle | Immediate |
| Close-ups of every dent, scratch, and broken light | Contradicts "minor contact" defense | Immediate |
| The other driver's license plate and VIN (windshield) | Prevents swapped-vehicle or wrong-party claims | Immediate |
| Road conditions, skid marks, signal lights, signage | Shows speed and fault attribution | Immediate |
| Damage to surrounding property (poles, curbs) | Corroborates impact force | Within minutes |
| The other driver, if they consent | Ties the person to the scene | Only with consent |

I noticed that in each of my crashes, the most valuable single photo was the wide shot taken from twenty feet back showing both cars at their final resting positions. It cost me five seconds and it ended the argument about who rear-ended whom.

While you're at it, record a short video walkaround with narration. Say the date, time, location, and what you see. Adjusters have told me they weight timestamped, narrated video more heavily than stills because it's harder to crop or misrepresent.

### Step 3: Exchange Information — But Know What You're Actually Collecting

You need more than a phone number. The standard exchange leaves out exactly the details that matter when the other party turns out to be on a lapsed policy or driving someone else's car.

Collect from the other driver:
- Full legal name and address
- Driver's license number and issuing state
- Insurance company, policy number, and the phone number on the card
- Vehicle make, model, color, plate, and VIN
- Name of the registered owner if it differs from the driver

Then verify the insurance card isn't expired. I check the effective dates. In one of my minor accidents, the guy's card had lapsed four months earlier — he didn't know, or said he didn't. That changed my strategy completely: I filed with my own insurer under uninsured motorist coverage and let them subrogate.

If there are witnesses, get their names and numbers. Witnesses evaporate. One of my friends had a perfect eyewitness who was never contacted because she didn't write down his number, and by the time the claim got contested he was unreachable.

### Step 4: Watch Every Word You Say at the Scene

This is the part where I've had to actively train myself, because instinct pushes the other way.

Do not apologize. Do not say "I didn't see you" or "I was going a little fast" or "my brakes are bad." Those phrases are admissions, and in many states they're admissible even though you didn't realize you were making one. The classic "I'm sorry" after a crash has cost people real money.

Do not discuss fault with the other driver. Do not debate who ran the light. The conversation you're having is not the one that decides the claim; it's the one that gets quoted in a recorded statement later.

If asked directly whether you're injured, and you're not sure, say you're not ready to discuss it. Adrenaline masks injury — the whiplash that shows up three days later is not something you can honestly rule out at the scene, and a recorded "I'm fine" stays on record.

This is the same discipline that shows up in [understanding your rights during a traffic stop](/posts/understanding-your-rights-during-a-traffic-stop/) — the officer is gathering statements, the other driver is gathering statements, and your job is to give facts, not narratives.

### Step 5: Deal With the Police Report Deliberately

When the officer arrives, give a clear, factual account: direction of travel, what you observed, what happened. Don't speculate about speed or fault unless you're certain. Don't exaggerate injuries to seem more serious.

Before the officer leaves, get their name and badge number and confirm how you'll receive the report. Report turnaround varies — in my Texas crash it took nine business days; in a Colorado incident it was available in four.

Then pull the report as soon as it's available and check it. Reports contain errors all the time: wrong plate, wrong lane, miscopied insurance info. I found a wrong lane designation on one of mine and had it corrected before it ever reached the adjusters. Once an insurer has built a file around a report, corrections get treated skeptically.

### Step 6: Call Your Own Insurer — And Watch What You Say

Call your insurer on the same day, even if the other driver was clearly at fault. This is a contractual obligation in virtually every policy, and delay gives them a coverage defense.

But here's the nuance I had to learn the hard way: the recorded statement you give your own insurer can be shared. Give it facts, not conclusions. "I was traveling westbound in the center lane when the other vehicle entered from the parking lot" is fine. "It was my fault, I looked away," is not.

If you have collision or uninsured motorist coverage, consider filing the claim through your own policy and letting them subrogate against the other driver. Yes, you pay your deductible upfront. In my experience this is often faster and less adversarial than waiting on the other driver's insurer to accept liability, and your insurer's lawyers are working for you against the other company.

## The Health and Medical Checklist (The Part People Skip)

The legal claim and the medical claim are the same claim. There is no injury case without medical documentation, and there is no documentation if you wait.

- Get evaluated within 24–72 hours even if you feel fine, especially after a rear-end impact. Soft-tissue injuries routinely take 48–72 hours to manifest.
- Keep every receipt, estimate, and explanation of benefits.
- Follow the treatment plan. Gaps in treatment are the single most common reason adjusters discount a legitimate injury claim.
- Document your own symptoms in a daily log — pain levels, missed work, activities you couldn't do. This log becomes evidence.

In my experience, an adjuster's first line of attack on an injury claim is "you didn't seek treatment for eleven days, so you weren't hurt." Don't hand them that.

## Deadlines, Coverage, and the Numbers That Actually Matter

The legal clock starts running on the day of the crash. There are two separate clocks — the insurance clock and the statute of limitations — and people confuse them constantly.

| Type of Deadline | Typical Rule | What Happens If You Miss It |
|---|---|---|
| Report crash to your insurer | "Prompt notice," often days | Coverage denial possible |
| File a claim with other driver's insurer | Varies; often 1–2 years | Weakened claim, evidence loss |
| Property damage statute of limitations | 2–3 years in most states | Barred from suing |
| Personal injury statute of limitations | 2–3 years in most states; as short as 1 year in some | Claim permanently barred |
| Minor-involved crashes | Tolled until age of majority in most states | Varies |
| Government vehicle involved | Often 6 months or less; strict notice requirements | Claim barred entirely |

The personal injury limitations period is the one people blow through. I went through this in detail in my guide on [how long you have to file a personal injury lawsuit](/posts/personal-injury-statute-of-limitations/), and the short version is: the national default is 2–3 years, but several states run shorter, and claims against government entities can require formal notice within months.

Coverage minimums are also lower than most people assume. As of 2026, liability minimums range widely — Texas requires 30/60/25, California 15/30/5, and a handful of states still permit limits as low as 25/50/25. If the at-fault driver carries state minimums and you're injured, their policy may not come close to covering your medical bills. That's exactly when your own uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage earns its premium.

## What *Not* to Sign, Say, or Accept

This is the part of the checklist I wish someone had handed me before my first crash.

**Don't sign a release at the scene.** The other driver's insurer may call you within days with a settlement offer. It will look generous. It will come with a release that extinguishes all future claims — including the injury that hasn't fully manifested yet.

**Don't give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurer without legal advice.** They're not required to give you one, and you're not required to give them one. You can provide information in writing or through your own insurer.

**Don't accept a "quick cash" settlement.** A cash offer at the scene may not cover the actual repair, let alone rental, diminished value, or medical costs.

**Don't let a body shop start work before the adjuster inspects.** Supplements are a hassle, and an adjuster may refuse to pay for repairs already completed if they weren't approved first.

I've seen this exact dynamic play out in other claim contexts too — the pressure to sign fast and ask questions later is the same pattern that shows up in [how to write a demand letter that gets results](/posts/how-to-write-demand-letter/), where the strongest position belongs to the person who documented everything and then politely refused the first offer.

## A Word on "Diminished Value" and the Claim Most People Forget

Even after your car is repaired, its resale value may drop because it has an accident history. In most states you can claim this "diminished value" as a separate element of damages, particularly if you're the not-at-fault party. It's a real, documented number — not a made-up figure — and insurers will deny it unless you press.

The mechanics are straightforward: get an independent diminished value appraisal, present it with your repair records, and ask in writing. My own appraisal on a 2022 SUV after a moderate rear-end collision came back at $3,400; the first offer was $0, the final settlement was $2,200. It took three letters and about six weeks.

## The Practical Toolkit I Actually Carry

Since the first crash, I keep a small kit in the glove box, and it has paid for itself several times over:

- A printed insurance card with policy number and the claims phone line
- A pen and a small notepad (phone batteries die; paper doesn't)
- A phone mount and charging cable (you'll be on the phone)
- A basic first-aid kit
- A disposable camera or a separate phone with backup power, since you'll be using your main phone for calls and photos

I also keep a plain-text template for accident notes on my phone, because I learned the hard way that trying to remember details in the parking lot while the other driver is arguing with you means you remember almost none of it. A quick fill-in on the spot — date, time, location, road conditions, description of the other vehicle, name and badge of responding officer — is worth more than any memory. If you ever need to organize those notes into a formal document, a [Markdown editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/) is my go-to for keeping a clean, timestamped record I can export or share.

## When to Call a Lawyer (And When You Genuinely Don't Need One)

Personal injury lawyers work on contingency — typically 33% of the settlement before a lawsuit is filed, rising to around 40% if litigation proceeds. That's a real number, and it matters.

You likely don't need a lawyer if you're dealing with a straightforward property-damage-only claim, the other driver's insurer has accepted liability, and your medical treatment is complete with a clear cost. Filing through small claims is often the right tool — I've walked through [how to file a small claims case without a lawyer](/posts/how-to-file-small-claims-without-lawyer/) and it genuinely works for property damage disputes.

You probably do need one if there are serious injuries, disputed liability, a commercial vehicle or government entity involved, an insurer lowballing on a legitimate claim, or multiple parties. In those cases, the contingency fee is usually worth more than what you'd recover alone.

## The Honest Limitation

No checklist makes a crash predictable or painless, and I want to be direct about that. Every state's rules differ, every insurer's behavior varies, and the "correct" move in one jurisdiction can be wrong in another. Texas and California have very different minimum coverage requirements, and a claim handling procedure that's optimal in one might miss a statutory notice requirement in the other.

The checklist I've built works for me because I treat it as a starting point, not a script. Before my next crash — and I'd rather not have one — I've written the actual local rules for my state into the template on my phone, so I don't have to look them up with shaking hands in a parking lot.

That's the real value of all this: not that you'll never be in a crash, but that when it happens, the thirty minutes you have don't get spent on panic. They get spent on photos, a police report number, and a careful silence about who was at fault.
