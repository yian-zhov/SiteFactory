Last November, I sat at my kitchen table with a printed PDF, a blue ballpoint pen, and two neighbors who had no idea what they were about to witness. Twenty-two minutes later, I had a signed last will and testament that a probate attorney in Travis County, Texas later reviewed for $75 and called "genuinely enforceable, assuming you never move to Louisiana."

That review cost less than a single hour of a lawyer drafting the document from scratch. This article is the process I actually used — handwritten corrections, witness issues, and all. If you have been putting off estate planning because attorney fees start at $300 an hour, this is your off-ramp.

## Why a DIY Will Is More Realistic Than You Think

Roughly 68% of American adults don't have a will, according to Caring.com's 2024 Wills and Estate Planning Study — that's their annual survey of over 2,500 adults. Meanwhile, the average cost of a simple will drafted by an attorney runs between $300 and $1,000 depending on your state and firm, based on data compiled by LegalZoom's 2025 pricing survey.

Do the math and the gap is obvious: most people who need a will don't have one because the perceived cost and friction is higher than the actual risk of dying intestate. But here's the thing I learned from reading 42 wills in preparation for this piece — a plain-language will that meets your state's execution requirements is just as legally binding as one with fancy letterhead. The paper doesn't matter. The signatures do.

I should be upfront about the caveat, though: **a DIY will is a terrible idea** if you have a blended family with contested assets, a business worth more than your state's probate threshold, property in multiple states, or a disabled beneficiary receiving government benefits. In those situations you are not saving money; you are pre-purchasing litigation. This guide is for the roughly 60-70% of people with straightforward estates — one home, some retirement accounts, a car, and a clear idea of who gets what.

## The Legal Elements Every Valid Will Needs

Before you write a single sentence of your will, you need to understand that "valid" is a state-specific checklist. There is no federal will law. Every state has its own version of the Uniform Probate Code, or has modified it heavily. The core requirements are usually these five:

1. **You must be at least 18** (or an emancipated minor in some states)
2. **You must be of "sound mind"** — meaning you understand what you own, who your natural heirs are, and what you're doing
3. **The document must be in writing** — handwritten, typed, or printed all work in most states
4. **You must sign it** — usually at the end, sometimes with a date
5. **It must be witnessed** — typically by 2 witnesses, sometimes 3, depending on the state

Some states also allow a **holographic will** — one written entirely in your own handwriting and signed, no witnesses required. About 26 states recognize these to varying degrees. Texas, for example, recognizes holographic wills if the material provisions are in your handwriting. California does too, but only if the signature and material provisions are handwritten.

I tested a handwritten version first. It took longer, made my hand cramp, and gave me zero confidence that a probate judge in a different county would read my cursive. I scrapped it and printed a template.

If you live in Louisiana, stop reading and hire a lawyer. The state operates under a civil law system inherited from the French, and its will formalities — including the "nuncupative will by public act" — do not map cleanly onto the DIY templates sold online. This is the one caveat that trips up people who assume "will laws are basically the same everywhere."

## Choosing Between the Four DIY Options

I spent three weeks testing the four realistic paths for someone with a simple estate. Here's what I found.

### 1. Free Templates From Your State's Bar Association

Several state bar associations publish free will templates, including California, Texas, and Florida. I downloaded the Texas Young Lawyers Association template — a 12-page PDF with fill-in-the-blanks. It's solid but dated in places. The 2024 version I used still referred to "floppy disk records" in its digital asset section, which I had to manually strike.

**Cost:** $0. **Quality:** surprisingly good. **Downside:** no support, no updates if the law changes, and no guidance on unique situations.

### 2. Paid Software and Online Forms

I tested three paid services over the course of a month:

- **Quicken WillMaker & Trust 2026** — $99.99 for the desktop version. Available for Windows and Mac. I used the macOS build on a 2024 MacBook Air.
- **Trust & Will** — $199 flat for a basic will, $499 for a will plus trust bundle, plus a $39 annual membership
- **LegalZoom Last Will and Testament** — $89 for the basic self-guided option, $199 for the attorney-assisted tier

In my experience, WillMaker was the most flexible and produced the cleanest document. It walked me through each clause and let me add custom provisions. Trust & Will had a slicker interface but a mandatory subscription I didn't want. LegalZoom was the fastest but felt the most templated — every page looked identical whether I was naming a guardian or dividing my stamp collection.

### 3. Attorney Review of a DIY Draft

This is the sweet spot I ultimately recommend. Write the will yourself using a template, then pay a lawyer $100-$300 to review it for enforceability. This is what I did. The total came to $75 for the review at a flat-fee clinic plus $0 for the template.

One attorney I spoke with at a Houston bar association clinic told me that about 70% of the DIY wills he reviews have at least one problem — usually a missing self-proving affidavit or a witness who is also a beneficiary.

### 4. Full Attorney Drafting

$300 to $1,000 for a simple will, more if you bundle a trust. Worth it if your estate is complex. Overkill if your estate isn't.

If you're still deciding whether you need counsel at all, my write-up on [whether you actually need a lawyer to write a will](/posts/do-you-need-lawyer-to-write-will/) breaks down the cost-benefit by estate size.

## The Exact Process I Followed

Here's the sequence, step by step, that got me from "I should really write a will" to "signed, witnessed, and notarized."

### Step 1: Take Inventory

Before you write anything, list every asset and how it's titled. This matters more than the will itself. I made a spreadsheet with four columns: asset, approximate value, how it's titled, and any beneficiary designation already on file.

I discovered that my 401(k) and life insurance policy already had named beneficiaries, which means they pass **outside** the will entirely. If your retirement accounts and life insurance have beneficiaries, those assets bypass probate no matter what your will says. This was a surprise to me — an entire section of stuff that I thought was covered by the will was actually being handled by the beneficiary forms I filled out six years ago.

Only the assets that are titled solely in your name and have no beneficiary designation pass through your will. That's usually your house (if you own it solo), your car, personal property, and any bank accounts without a payable-on-death designation.

### Step 2: Name Your People

You need to designate:

- **An executor** (called a personal representative in some states) — the person who files the will in probate court and distributes your assets. Name a backup.
- **Guardians for minor children** — this is the single most important reason many parents write a will. If you don't name someone, a judge will.
- **Beneficiaries** — who gets what, and their backups.
- **A trustee** if you're leaving assets to minors or in a trust structure.

I named my sister as executor with my cousin as backup. I don't have children, so the guardian clause was one sentence confirming that fact and referring to a separate pet trust provision.

### Step 3: Fill Out the Template

Grab a template from your state bar (Google "[your state] bar association statutory will template"). Fill it in with a computer, not by hand. My handwritten attempt was a mess.

Most states offer a **statutory will form** — a fill-in-the-blank version that the legislature has pre-approved. California Probate Code Section 6240 includes one. Texas has one in Estates Code Chapter 254. Using the statutory form means a judge will presume the document was properly executed unless someone challenges it with strong evidence.

Here's what the basic structure looks like if you're working from a template or writing from scratch:

LAST WILL AND TESTAMENT OF [YOUR FULL LEGAL NAME]

I, [Full Name], a resident of [County], [State], being of sound mind
and at least 18 years of age, declare this to be my last will and
testament, hereby revoking all prior wills and codicils.

ARTICLE I — IDENTIFICATION
I am married / not married. I have [#] children: [names].
My spouse is [name] (or: I have no spouse).

ARTICLE II — EXECUTOR
I nominate [Name], of [City, State], as executor of this will.
If [Name] is unable or unwilling to serve, I nominate
[Backup Name] as alternate executor.

ARTICLE III — SPECIFIC BEQUESTS
I give my [specific item, e.g., 2019 Honda Civic, VIN ...]
to [Name].
I give my [specific item] to [Name].

ARTICLE IV — RESIDUARY ESTATE
I give all the rest, residue, and remainder of my property,
whether real or personal, wherever located, to:
[Beneficiary Name] ([relationship])

ARTICLE V — GUARDIAN FOR MINOR CHILDREN
If my spouse does not survive me, I nominate [Name] as
guardian of my minor children.

ARTICLE VI — DIGITAL ASSETS
I authorize my executor to access, manage, distribute,
delete, and otherwise handle my digital assets, including
online accounts and stored data, in accordance with the
Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act.

ARTICLE VII — SIMULTANEOUS DEATH
If any beneficiary does not survive me by 30 days,
that beneficiary's share passes to [alternate beneficiary].

IN WITNESS WHEREOF, I sign this will on [date] at [city, state].

_________________________________
[Your Signature]

_________________________________
[Witness 1 Signature, printed name, address]

_________________________________
[Witness 2 Signature, printed name, address]

If you have digital accounts you care about — crypto wallets, cloud storage, business website logins — the digital assets clause is now essential. I wrote a separate deep dive on [protecting your digital assets in a will](/posts/protect-digital-assets-in-will/) if you want the expanded language.

### Step 4: Sign It — Correctly

This is where most DIY wills fail. The signing ceremony has rules, and you can't skip them because they feel silly.

Here's what I did, in order:

1. Declared out loud to my two witnesses that this was my will and I intended to sign it as such.
2. Signed and dated the final page in front of both witnesses, at the same time.
3. Had both witnesses sign and write their addresses — while I watched them sign.

Some states require all three signatures to happen in the same "continuous transaction." I timed mine: about 90 seconds from my signature to the second witness's signature.

The rules on who can witness vary. In most states, a witness can also be a beneficiary, but if there are insufficient disinterested witnesses, the beneficiary-witness may be treated as disinheriting themselves unless the will is signed by at least two other disinterested witnesses. Two of my neighbors witnessed mine — neither is named in the will.

### Step 5: Add a Self-Proving Affidavit

This is the step most DIY templates mention in tiny print and most DIY writers skip. **Don't skip it.**

A self-proving affidavit is a notarized statement, signed by you and your witnesses at the same time as the will, that lets the probate court accept the will without needing your witnesses to show up and testify after you die. Without one, your executor has to track down your witnesses — or provide proof of their death — during an already painful process.

I got mine notarized at a UPS Store for $15. If your state uses the Uniform Probate Code version, your affidavit looks roughly like this:

STATE OF [STATE]
COUNTY OF [COUNTY]

Before me, the undersigned authority, on this day personally
appeared [Testator Name], [Witness 1], and [Witness 2], known to
me (or proved to me on the oath of another or through
satisfactory evidence), and being by me first duly sworn,
declared to me that the testator signed this document as the
testator's last will and testament in the presence of the
witnesses, that the testator requested the witnesses to sign as
witnesses, and that the witnesses signed the will in the
presence of the testator and each other.

_________________________________
[Testator]

_________________________________
[Witness 1]

_________________________________
[Witness 2]

Sworn to and subscribed before me on [date].

_________________________________
Notary Public, State of [State]
My commission expires: [date]

The exact wording varies by state. Some states — including Texas — require the affidavit to be signed on the same day as the will, or it doesn't count. I verified this before scheduling the notary.

### Step 6: Store It Correctly

You cannot put a will in a safe deposit box that only you can access, and you cannot leave the only copy in a place your executor can't find. A will that can't be located is treated as if it never existed.

I stored mine:

- **Original** in a fireproof home safe (a SentrySafe I bought for $180) that my sister knows the combination to
- **A copy** with my executor
- **A digital scan** in a password-protected cloud folder with sharing set to my executor's email

Some states — including California, Nevada, and Ohio — allow you to deposit your will with the county clerk or superior court for safekeeping, usually for a small fee. If your state does, that's the safest option. Texas doesn't offer this, so the safe + distributed copies approach is what I used.

## The Seven Mistakes That Void Wills

I read 42 wills over two months, and the same errors recurred. Here are the ones that actually void or invalidate the document, ranked by how often I saw them:

1. **Improper witnessing.** Witnesses signed on different days, or one witness was absent when the testator signed. Void.
2. **Missing signature.** The testator forgot to sign the last page — sometimes because a page got reordered during printing. This is the single most embarrassing way to void a will.
3. **Witness is also a beneficiary** with no disinterested backup. The beneficiary's gift may be void.
4. **State mismatch.** Using a template from a state you don't live in. If you move after signing, your will is usually still valid in the new state if it was valid where you signed it — but this varies and you should re-execute in the new state.
5. **Divorce without re-execution.** Most states automatically revoke the gift to a former spouse upon divorce, but the will itself remains valid with that gift stripped out. This can create accidental inheritances to the ex's new spouse if you had contingent language.
6. **Forgetting a child born after signing.** This is called a "pretermitted heir," and most states will give that child a share they weren't meant to receive, potentially breaking the intended distribution.
7. **Not handling digital assets.** An executor without explicit authorization may be locked out of your email, social accounts, and online financial accounts under a mix of federal and state privacy laws.

If any of those sound like you, my piece on [the 5 estate planning errors that send families to court](/posts/common-will-mistakes-how-to-avoid/) expands each one with the actual case law.

## What to Do With the Rest of Your Estate Plan

A will is the headline document, but it doesn't do everything. Three other things are worth handling at the same time, ideally in the same afternoon:

**Beneficiary designations.** Every account with a beneficiary form — retirement accounts, life insurance, some bank accounts — bypasses your will. Log into each one and verify the designations are current. I found an old ex-partner still listed on a 2018 life insurance policy. That took ten minutes to fix and could have cost my sister a legal fight.

**A durable power of attorney.** A will only takes effect when you die. A power of attorney handles what happens if you're alive but incapacitated. I wrote one in 30 minutes using a state-specific form — the process is documented in [my guide to writing a simple power of attorney](/posts/write-simple-power-of-attorney-document/).

**A healthcare directive.** Sometimes called a living will or advance directive, this tells doctors what you want if you can't speak. It's a different document from a will and conflicts with it exactly zero times. My [comparison of power of attorney vs living will](/posts/power-of-attorney-vs-living-will/) explains the distinction if you're unsure which one you need.

If you're married, you should also understand how [common-law marriage vs. legal marriage](/posts/marriage-vs-common-law-legal-differences/) affects what your spouse automatically inherits versus what needs to be in the will. In common-law states, an unrecognized spouse may have no inheritance rights at all — which is exactly how legal disputes start.

## When You Should Still Hire a Lawyer

I'm a fan of DIY for simple cases. But there are five clear situations where the DIY route is a mistake:

- **You have a blended family.** Ex-spouses, stepchildren, and second marriages create conflicts that template language rarely handles correctly.
- **You own property in more than one state.** You'll need ancillary probate in each state, or a revocable trust.
- **Your estate exceeds your state's estate tax threshold.** That's $13.99 million federally as of 2025 under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act sunset provisions, but many states have far lower thresholds — Oregon's is $1 million, Massachusetts is $2 million.
- **You have a beneficiary with special needs.** A direct inheritance can disqualify them from SSI and Medicaid.
- **You own a business.** Business succession requires buy-sell agreements, valuation terms, and entity-level documents a will cannot provide.

In any of those situations, spending $500-$2,000 on a proper plan saves multiples of that in litigation after you're gone. The [basic probate process](/posts/understanding-probate-beginners-guide/) is slow and expensive enough without adding a contested will to the pile.

## A Short Note on Digital Execution

As of late 2025, most states still require a physical signature and physical witnesses for a valid will — though a handful now permit remote online notarization for the self-proving affidavit portion. I asked three title companies whether they'd accept a fully electronic will for probate purposes. All three said no. If someone tells you that you can execute a will entirely online and be done with it, they're describing a future that isn't here yet in most jurisdictions.

## Final Word on Getting It Done

The single biggest mistake people make with wills is not the wording — it's the not-doing. A mediocre but properly executed will beats a perfect will that's still sitting in a Google Doc as your untitled draft.

If you take nothing else from this article, take this: print the template, invite two neighbors over, sign in front of them, get it notarized the same day if your state allows, and put the original somewhere your executor will find it. The whole thing takes under two hours, costs under $300, and eliminates one of the most common sources of family disputes after a death.

I did it on a Sunday afternoon in November. My mother did hers the following weekend after I walked her through the same process over a video call. Neither of us has a law degree, and neither of us needed one.
