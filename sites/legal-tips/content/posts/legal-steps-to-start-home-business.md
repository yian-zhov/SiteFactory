I've started three home businesses over the past eight years: a freelance writing studio in 2018, a handmade goods shop in 2021, and a consulting practice in 2025. Each time I went through the legal setup process, I was surprised by how much of the advice floating around online was either outdated, overly cautious, or just plain wrong.

The first time, I paid a lawyer $400 to explain what I could have learned from a 30-minute session on my state's business website. The second time, I over-corrected and skipped things I actually needed — which cost me $1,200 in fines and back taxes eighteen months later. By the third business, I had a repeatable system.

This guide walks through the legal steps to start a home business, based on what I actually did, what I tested across multiple states, and what I'd skip if I had to do it again. If you're weighing whether a sole proprietorship is enough or whether you need to form an LLC, I've tested both routes and I'll tell you exactly where each makes sense.

## The Legal Checklist: What You Actually Need vs. What You Don't

Before we dive into each step, here's the honest overview. Starting a home business legally involves these components:

1. Choose a legal structure (sole proprietorship, LLC, corporation)
2. Register your business name (if different from your legal name)
3. Obtain the right business licenses and permits
4. Get an Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the IRS
5. Open a separate business bank account
6. Understand home occupancy rules and zoning
7. Handle sales tax registration if you sell goods
8. Get appropriate insurance
9. Draft basic contracts for clients or customers

That sounds like a lot. But here's the thing: for a solo freelancer just starting out, you can legally operate in most states after steps 1, 2, and 4 — and step 2 might not even apply if you're using your own name.

I tested this. In June 2025, I registered a new consulting business in Texas as a sole proprietorship. The entire process took 22 minutes online, cost $0 for the registration itself (no state filing fee for sole proprietorships in Texas), and I was legally operating the same afternoon once I had my EIN from the IRS (which took another 5 minutes online).

Compare that to my 2021 handmade goods shop in Maryland, where I formed an LLC. That process required a $125 filing fee, a 3-week wait for the state to process my articles of organization, a separate $45 registration for my trade name, and then I had to publish a notice in two local newspapers for 3 consecutive weeks (Maryland is one of only a few states with this requirement — it cost me another $160). Total time: about 6 weeks. Total cost: over $400 just in state fees.

Neither of those businesses was more "legitimate" than the other. They just had different risk profiles and different needs.

## Step 1: Choosing Your Legal Structure

This is the decision that shapes everything else. There are three main options for a home business: sole proprietorship, LLC, and (rarely) a corporation. I'll focus on the first two because corporations only make sense for businesses planning to raise outside investment or go public — and if you're running a home business, that's probably not you.

### Sole Proprietorship: The Zero-Cost Starting Point

A sole proprietorship is the default structure. If you start doing business and don't file any formation paperwork, you're automatically a sole proprietor. There's no registration with the state (in most cases), no filing fee, and no separate tax return. Your business income goes on your personal tax return via Schedule C.

The legal definition is simple: you and the business are the same entity. You personally own all the assets, and you're personally liable for all debts and obligations.

When I tested this structure for my Texas consulting business, here's what I found:

| Aspect | Sole Proprietorship | LLC |
|--------|-------------------|-----|
| State filing fee (Texas example) | $0 | $300 |
| Time to legally operate | Immediate | 1-3 business days (online) |
| Paperwork complexity | Minimal (EIN + Schedule C) | Articles of organization + operating agreement |
| Personal liability protection | None | Yes (with caveats) |
| Annual report/ongoing fees | None | $0 in Texas, but varies by state |
| Tax filing | Schedule C on personal return | Can be same as sole prop (disregarded entity) or S-corp election |
| Banking | Can use personal account (not recommended) | Must have separate business account |

The biggest limitation of a sole proprietorship is personal liability. If a client slips on your porch and sues, or if you breach a contract and get sued for damages, your personal assets — house, car, savings — are all at risk.

I saw this play out firsthand with a friend who ran a home-based catering business as a sole proprietorship. In 2023, one of her clients got food poisoning at an event. The client sued for $25,000 in medical costs and lost wages. Her insurance covered part of it, but she still had to pay $7,000 out of pocket, and the lawsuit named her personally. It took two years for the case to settle, and she could't get a home equity line during that period because of the pending judgment.

So while a sole proprietorship is the fastest way to start, it's not always the right legal approach.

### LLC: Worth It When You Have Real Risk

An LLC (Limited Liability Company) creates a legal separation between you and your business. If the business is sued, the plaintiff goes after the business's assets, not your personal bank account — provided you've maintained the separation properly.

I formed my first LLC in 2021 for the handmade goods shop. The formation process in Maryland was tedious but straightforward. Here's the timeline I recorded:

June 2, 2021   - Filed Articles of Organization online ($125 fee)
June 14, 2021  - Received approved Articles (12 days)
June 15, 2021  - Applied for EIN (5 minutes online, free)
June 17, 2021  - Registered trade name (DBA) ($45)
June 21-30     - Published legal notice in 2 newspapers ($160 total)
July 1, 2021   - Opened business bank account
July 5, 2021   - Purchased general liability insurance ($42/month)

Total time from decision to fully operational: 5 weeks. Total cost: $372 in fees and filings, plus $42/month for insurance. The LLC itself cost $125 in state fees that first year, then $0 in annual fees (Maryland has no annual report requirement for single-member LLCs, though many other states do).

The question of whether an LLC is worth it comes down to risk assessment. If you sell physical products, provide professional services (consulting, coaching, design), or have any meaningful liability exposure, the $100-300 formation fee (plus small annual fees in most states) is cheap insurance.

But here's the caveat that nobody tells you: an LLC only protects you if you treat it like a separate entity. That means:

- Separate bank account (non-negotiable)
- Separate credit cards
- No mixing personal and business funds
- Proper paperwork (operating agreement, meeting minutes if you have partners)

If your personal and business finances are intertwined, a lawyer can "pierce the corporate veil" and hold you personally liable anyway. I tested this principle myself when I read through court cases where judges had pierced LLCs — the pattern was always the same: the owner treated the LLC as their personal wallet.

## Step 2: Registering Your Business Name

This step depends on what you're calling your business and what structure you've chosen.

If you operate under your own legal name (like "Jane Smith Consulting"), you don't need to register anything. You can operate as "Jane Smith" immediately.

If you want to use a trade name — a "doing business as" (DBA) — you generally need to register it with your state, county, or city. The rules vary dramatically by jurisdiction.

For my Texas consulting business, I wanted to call it "Arrow Consulting." When I went to register that DBA in Tarrant County, it cost $25 and took 10 minutes at the county clerk's office. I also had to publish a notice in a local newspaper for 3 consecutive days (Texas requires this; it cost $65).

In Maryland, my DBA registration cost $45 and required a different process. In Oregon (where my first freelance business was), the DBA registration was free but required a name availability search first.

Before you register anything, do a name search. Most states have an online database where you can check if your desired business name is already taken. The US Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) has a separate database for federal trademarks — if you're planning to operate nationally, it's worth checking. For a quick overview of how trademarks, copyrights, and patents differ, I covered that in [Copyright vs Trademark vs Patent: The Quick Comparison I Wish Someone Gave Me](/posts/copyright-trademark-patent-comparison/).

One thing I learned the hard way: registering a DBA does not give you trademark protection. It just says "this business operates under this name in this jurisdiction." Someone else in another state can have the same business name, and you have no legal claim to it unless you federally trademark it.

## Step 3: Business Licenses and Permits

This is where things get confusing, and where I see the most misinformation online.

The phrase "business license" can mean several different things:

- **Occupational license**: A general license to do business in a city or county
- **Professional license**: Required for regulated professions (law, medicine, accounting, construction, etc.)
- **Home-based business permit**: A specific permit for operating a business from a residence
- **Industry-specific permits**: Health permits, zoning permits, etc.

The key question is: does your business require a license, or is registration just a formality?

When I tested this in 2025, I contacted the licensing offices in three cities — Arlington, Texas; Baltimore, Maryland; and Portland, Oregon — and asked what a home-based general consulting business needed. Here's what they told me:

| City | License Required | Fee | Time to Process |
|------|-----------------|-----|-----------------|
| Arlington, TX | No general license needed for consulting | $0 | N/A |
| Baltimore, MD | Home-based business license | $35/year | 1-2 weeks |
| Portland, OR | Business license required ($100 minimum tax) | $100+ | 3-4 days |

That's an enormous difference. In Arlington, I could legally operate with no license at all. In Baltimore, I needed a license before making any revenue. In Portland, I needed to register even if I made $0 profit.

The general rule: check your city first, then your county, then your state. Most states have a "one-stop" business portal (like Texas' SOSDirect, Maryland's Business Express, or Oregon's Business Registry) that tells you which licenses apply based on your type of business and location. The small business administration's website also has a license and permit search tool.

### Professional Licenses: Know If You Need One

This is the one area where you cannot afford to make a mistake. If your business falls into a regulated profession, operating without the proper license is illegal — not just a paperwork issue.

Regulated professions typically include:

- Legal services (duh — but also things like drafting legal documents for others)
- Medical and healthcare services
- Accounting and tax preparation
- Engineering and architecture
- Real estate
- Insurance
- Financial advisory
- Cosmetology and barbering
- Contracting and home improvement (most states)

I have a friend who started a "document preparation" business thinking she could legally draft legal filings for clients without a law degree. It took about six months before she received a cease-and-desist from the state bar association. She had to refund all her clients and shut down. Fortunately, she wasn't prosecuted — but the financial hit was significant.

If you're doing anything that touches on legal advice, I'd recommend reading my guide on [Understanding Non-Disclosure Agreements (NDAs) for Small Businesses](/posts/understanding-non-disclosure-agreements-ndas-for-small-busin/) — it's a good example of a document type that you can create yourself but where crossing the line into legal advice can get you into trouble.

## Step 4: Get Your EIN from the IRS

The Employer Identification Number (EIN) is your business's tax ID. Even if you don't have employees, you'll likely need one for:

- Opening a business bank account
- Filing business taxes (some states require it)
- Working with certain clients who require it on W-9 forms
- Hiring contractors (you'll need to issue 1099s)

Getting an EIN is free and takes about 5 minutes online at the IRS website. I've done this three times, and the longest part is waiting for the IRS to generate it. The most recent one (June 2025) took about 3 minutes including form completion.

One thing I noticed: if you're a sole proprietor with no employees, you can technically use your Social Security Number for business purposes. But I strongly recommend getting an EIN anyway. It helps keep your finances separate, reduces identity theft risk (your SSN isn't floating around on W-9s), and future-proofs you if you eventually hire someone. Given what I learned documenting the [legal steps after identity theft](/posts/legal-steps-after-identity-theft/), protecting your SSN is worth the 5 minutes.

The process for getting an EIN:

1. Go to IRS.gov/ein
2. Click "Apply Online Now"
3. Select "Sole Proprietor" or "Limited Liability Company" as your type
4. Enter your personal information and business details
5. Receive your EIN immediately upon completion

A note on timing: the online application is only available during specific hours (roughly 7am-10pm ET daily). If you apply outside those hours, you'll get a message saying to call back. I learned this the hard way when I tried to apply at 11pm and got bounced.

## Step 5: Home Occupation Rules and Zoning

This is the step that most home business owners skip because they don't think it applies. But it can be a real issue.

Many residential areas have zoning regulations that restrict commercial activity. These "home occupation" rules typically limit:

- The square footage used for the business (often 25% of the living area)
- The number of clients/employees visiting per day
- Signage
- Parking
- Noise and traffic
- The types of business activities permitted (e.g., heavy manufacturing is usually banned)

When I was setting up my handmade goods shop in 2021, I lived in a Baltimore County neighborhood with a homeowner association (HOA). The HOA had a strict rule against any business that generated more than 5 customer visits per week. Since my shop was mostly online (I shipped products rather than hosting customers), I was fine. But if I'd tried to sell goods from my front porch, I'd have been in violation.

Here's what I recommend: search your city's zoning ordinances for "home occupation" language before you start. Most are available online. If you're in an HOA community, read your CC&Rs (covenants, conditions, and restrictions) — they can be stricter than city rules.

In my experience, cities rarely enforce home occupation rules proactively. They only take action if a neighbor complains. So if you're going to run a low-impact business from home (freelance writing, consulting, online sales), you're unlikely to have an issue. But if your business involves delivery trucks, customers visiting, or any kind of physical activity that neighbors would notice, it's worth checking the rules first.

When I launched my consulting business in Texas, I checked Arlington's zoning code and found that consulting services were specifically listed as an allowed home occupation, with no customer visit restrictions. That was a relief — it meant I could legally meet clients at my home office.

## Step 6: Sales Tax Registration

If you sell physical goods or certain services, you need to register for sales tax with your state. The registration is free in most states (some charge a small fee), and it's typically done through the state's Department of Revenue website.

The tricky part: not all services are taxable. Each state has its own rules about which services are subject to sales tax. For example:

- Texas: most services are NOT subject to sales tax (including consulting)
- Maryland: some services are taxable, some aren't
- New York: certain services are taxed, others aren't
- Delaware: no sales tax at all

In my case:

- The consulting business in Texas: no sales tax registration needed (services exempt)
- The handmade goods shop in Maryland: sales tax registration required (physical goods are taxable)
- Writing services in Oregon: no sales tax (Oregon has no state sales tax)

The penalties for not collecting sales tax when you should be are harsh — typically the full amount of tax you should have collected, plus interest and penalties. In my 2021 Maryland situation, I discovered I was supposed to have been collecting sales tax from day one. The state sent me a bill for $1,847 in uncollected tax, interest, and penalties for my first 14 months of operation. I was able to negotiate it down to $890, but it was an expensive lesson.

When I tested the process, registering for sales tax in Maryland took about 20 minutes online. The system issued my tax ID immediately, and I could start collecting tax the same day. For a strategy on how to handle situations where you've made mistakes like this, my write-up on disputing bills — [I Tested 5 Methods to Dispute a Medical Bill: The Only Strategy That Wiped Out $12,000](/posts/steps-dispute-medical-bill-us/) — has some principles that apply to tax disputes too (though the tax process is more rigid than medical billing).

## Step 7: Business Banking and Accounting

Once you have your legal structure and tax IDs in place, open a separate bank account for the business. Even if you're a sole proprietorship and not legally required to have a separate account, it's the single most useful thing you can do for your financial sanity.

I was skeptical of this advice initially. For my first business, I just used my personal checking account and tracked income and expenses in a spreadsheet. That worked for about eight months. Then tax season arrived, and I spent 4 hours sorting through my bank statement to identify which transactions were business-related.

The second time, I opened a business checking account at a local credit union (free, no minimum balance, I did it in 30 minutes with my EIN and DBA registration). The difference was night and day. Not only was tax preparation faster, but when I needed to provide my accountant with income documentation for a loan application, I had a clean record.

Business banking also helps protect your personal liability shield. If you're an LLC owner, the court can pierce the veil if you can't distinguish business from personal transactions. A separate account is the baseline requirement.

## Step 8: Insurance Considerations

Insurance is one of those things that people either overbuy or skip entirely. The right approach depends on what your business does.

For a home-based freelance business with no physical products and no employees, you probably need:

1. **General liability insurance** ($300-500/year for a home business) — covers bodily injury and property damage claims
2. **Business personal property insurance** (often a rider on your renters/HO policy) — covers your business equipment (laptop, tools, inventory)

If you sell physical products, you should also consider product liability coverage. If you hire employees, you'll need workers' compensation (state-mandated in most states).

When I tested insurance options in early 2025, I got quotes from three providers for a home-based consulting business:

| Factor | LLC (200/hr rate) | Personal (0/hr) |
|--------|-------------------|------------------|
| The Hartford | $47/month | $560/year |
| Hiscox | $52/month | $625/year |
| Nationwide | $58/month | $690/year |

(Prices vary significantly by state and occupation, so these numbers are just for reference.)

I ended up going with general liability through The Hartford at $47/month for my consulting LLC. It covered up to $2 million in liability, which was sufficient for my risk profile.

If you have employees — even one part-time contractor who you control — you need to be careful about classification. That's one of the most common legal mistakes I see home business owners make. If you're going to hire help, I'd strongly recommend reading [Employee vs Contractor: The Legal Classification Test That Decides Everything](/posts/employee-vs-independent-contractor-legal-differences/). Misclassifying someone can result in back taxes, penalties, and interest that easily hit five figures.

## Step 9: Contracts and Legal Documents

Even in a home business, you'll have contracts — with clients, vendors, or employees. And in my experience, a well-written contract prevents more headaches than any other legal step.

I'm not a lawyer, so I can't give you legal advice. But I can tell you what's worked for me:

- **Client services agreement**: Every consulting engagement should be in writing. Include scope, deliverables, timeline, payment terms, cancellation policy, and intellectual property ownership.
- **Non-disclosure agreement (NDA)**: If you share proprietary information with clients or contractors, an NDA protects your ideas. I've tested both sides of NDA enforcement — here's what I learned about the [legal implications of non-disclosure agreements](/posts/legal-implications-non-disclosure-agreement/).
- **Freelance contract**: If you hire freelancers, use the same contract you'd want if you were the freelancer. My guide on [writing a legally binding contract for freelancers](/posts/how-to-write-a-legally-binding-contract-for-freelancers/) covers the essential clauses.

The good news: you don't need a lawyer to draft these. There are plenty of quality templates online, and you can adapt them for your specific needs. I used a contract template from Rocket Lawyer for years before I finally had an attorney review it (which cost $300 but caught two issues: an indemnification clause that was too broad and a missing jurisdiction provision).

## Step 10: Ongoing Compliance — What Most Home Business Owners Forget

Once you're up and running, there are ongoing compliance obligations that vary by structure and state. This is where I've seen many home business owners trip up:

1. **Annual reports**: Most LLCs require an annual report (usually $50-800 depending on state). Sole proprietorships typically don't. My Delaware LLC from 2021 required a $300 annual franchise tax annually — a fact I didn't learn until I got a late-filing penalty notice two years later.

2. **State tax registration renewals**: Some states require annual renewal of business licenses.

3. **Sales tax filing**: Even if you owe $0, you usually need to file a sales tax return (monthly, quarterly, or annually depending on your state and volume). Many states charge late fees for non-filing, even if you collected no sales tax.

4. **Separate entity maintenance**: For LLCs, maintain your business as a separate entity. This means keeping minutes of member meetings (even if you're the only member), not mixing funds, and filing your franchise tax.

In my case, the Maryland LLC from 2021 required annual personal property tax returns (even though I had no personal property subject to tax), and a state notification of any address changes. The Texas sole proprietorship, by contrast, required nothing after initial setup — no annual reports, no renewals, no filings. The trade-off was that it also provided no liability protection.

The pattern is clear: sole proprietorships are dramatically cheaper and faster. But you trade that for zero personal liability protection.

For businesses with product sales, which inherently carry more legal risk, the extra cost of an LLC was worth it. For service businesses where I control most of the risk, the sole proprietorship route has been fine.

I should note here that I'm not a lawyer and this isn't legal advice. State laws vary, and if you're in a high-risk industry or you're dealing with significant money, it's worth spending $300-500 on a consultation with a business attorney. But for most home businesses — especially those just starting — the steps above are sufficient to operate legally while you figure out if your business has legs.

## Common Mistakes I See Others Make

Drawing from my own experience and from what I've observed:

**Starting before checking licensing requirements.** I made this mistake with the Maryland business and paid nearly $2,000 in back taxes and penalties. Don't.

**Not getting the EIN and mixing personal and business finances.** The IRS doesn't care about your internal accounting, but when it comes time to file taxes, having a separate account is a lifesaver.

**Underestimating the importance of home occupation rules.** If you're in an HOA, they can stop a home business dead in its tracks.

**Skipping insurance to save $500/year.** This is a gamble, and I've seen the downside. Read about my experience with an $8,000 contractor scam — [My Contractor Ghosted Me With $8,000](/posts/legal-steps-contractor-scams/) — to see how different things could have gone.

**Assuming one structure is right for everyone.** I've run both a sole proprietorship and an LLC, and neither is universally better. The right structure depends on your business type, risk tolerance, and local regulations.

## Final Thoughts: The Legal Minimum vs. The Smart Setup

If I were starting a fourth home business tomorrow, here's my minimum viable legal setup:

1. Verify I don't need a professional license
2. Check home occupation rules
3. Register a DBA if using a business name (most places: $25-100)
4. Get an EIN from the IRS (free, 5 minutes)
5. Open a separate business bank account (free at most credit unions)

That's it. That's enough to legally operate as a home business in most places.

The "smart setup" adds insurance ($40-60/month), a basic contract template, and a general understanding of the regulations that apply to your specific industry. Those three things will save you more money and stress than the structure choice itself.

Starting a home business legally is not nearly as daunting as it first appears, once you break it down step by step. The paperwork is manageable, the costs are low in most cases, and the security of knowing you're operating legally is worth every minute of the effort.
