I've been in two car accidents in the past four years. The first was a rear-end collision in a Target parking lot on a rainy Tuesday—minor bumper damage, no injuries, but I fumbled everything. I took no photos, exchanged only a phone number, and didn't call the police because the other driver seemed friendly. That mistake cost me $1,200 when their story changed the next day.

The second accident was on Interstate 80 in Nevada last October. A semi truck merged into my lane and clipped my passenger-side mirror. This time I had a system. I knew exactly what to document, when to call the police, and what to say (and not say) to the other driver and my insurer. The claim settled in 11 days.

Here's everything I learned about the legal side of what happens after a car accident—the steps that protect you legally and financially, and the ones that can quietly destroy your case.

## The First 60 Seconds: Your Legal Duty to Stop

If you're in an accident, the law requires you to stop. Every state has a "hit-and-run" statute—typically a misdemeanor for property damage and a felony if someone is injured or killed. Leaving the scene is the single fastest way to turn a minor fender bender into a criminal matter.

In Nevada, where I was in my second accident, leaving the scene of an accident with property damage is a misdemeanor carrying up to 6 months in jail and a $1,000 fine. If someone's injured, it becomes a category B felony with up to 20 years in prison. I looked this up after my incident because I wanted to understand what was actually at stake.

When I tested a similar scenario for [my article on hit-and-run accidents](/posts/what-to-do-hit-and-run-accident/), I found that the legal consequences vary dramatically by state. California Vehicle Code section 20002 treats property-damage-only hit-and-runs as misdemeanors, but penalties escalate to felonies with injuries. Texas Penal Code 38.171 also escalates, and Illinois treats it similarly. The universal rule: stop, regardless of who caused the accident.

### Move Your Vehicle (But Only If It's Safe)

If your car is drivable and blocking traffic, most states require you to move it to the shoulder or a safe location. Failing to do so can create additional hazards and, in some states, get you a citation for impeding traffic. But if your car is leaking fluids, smoking, or in a position where moving it could destroy evidence, leave it and turn on your hazard lights.

Here's a practical rule I use: if the damage is minor and the car starts, move it. If there's any question about structural integrity or fluids leaking, don't. The police will secure the scene either way.

## Calling the Police: When It's Required and When It's a Mistake

This is where people make their biggest legal errors.

**Some states require a police report** if damage exceeds a certain threshold. That threshold ranges from $500 in some states to $2,500 in others. But even if your state doesn't require it, call the police for any accident involving injury, significant damage, or a dispute about fault.

Here's something I discovered through testing: when I called the police after my Nevada accident, the officer asked if I wanted him to complete a full crash report or just an incident exchange. That distinction matters. A formal crash report creates a written record, including the officer's observations. It's admissible in court and extremely persuasive in insurance negotiations.

The downside: police response times can be long for minor accidents. When I tested this in my own accident, it took about 35 minutes for a Nevada Highway Patrol trooper to arrive. During that time, the other driver and I exchanged information and took photos. That's fine. The report is worth the wait.

The opposite scenario—an accident in a state where you know the report isn't required and the damage is cosmetic—can sometimes be handled without police. I did this in the Target parking lot accident, and I shouldn't have. Here's why:

| With Police Report | Without Police Report |
|---|---|
| Official record of the accident | Rely on written statements and photos |
| Officer's observations documented | No neutral third party present |
| Higher credibility in court | Civilian documentation only |
| Takes 30-60 minutes at scene | Faster, but riskier |
| Insurance claims proceed faster | Claims may face disputes |
| Costs nothing out of pocket | No direct cost either |

Every attorney I've consulted has told me the same thing: call the police. The report is the single strongest piece of evidence in any insurance claim or lawsuit. It's also the thing that prevents a "friendly" driver from changing their story later.

**A note on liability at the accident scene:** Even if you suspect you caused the accident, you are legally required to provide your name, address, and registration to the other driver. You are not required to admit fault. In fact, you should never admit fault at the scene. Insurance companies and attorneys use the word "sorry" against you. A well-meaning "I'm so sorry, I didn't see you" can be interpreted as an admission of negligence.

## The Information Exchange: What You're Legally Required to Provide

Every state requires drivers involved in an accident to exchange specific information. The legal baseline is:

1. **Your full name and address**
2. **Your driver's license number**
3. **Your license plate number**
4. **Your insurance information** (company name and policy number)
5. **The vehicle registration** for the car you're driving

That's it. You are not required to provide your phone number, but giving it helps the claims process. I recommend giving it anyway—you'll need to communicate with the other party's insurer.

### The Mistakes I've Seen (And Made)

When I tested this process in my parking lot accident, I made the rookie error of not getting the other driver's license plate number photographed. I only wrote down their phone number—and when their story changed the next day, I had no way to verify their identity. It was a mess.

Here's what you should actually collect, beyond the legal minimum:

- **The other driver's phone number**
- **The vehicle's VIN** (take a photo of the dashboard VIN plate)
- **The insurance policy number and claims phone number**
- **A photo of the other driver's license** (front and back)
- **A photo of their insurance card**
- **Any visible damage to both vehicles**

The pattern is simple: photograph everything. If it's not photographed, it's a word against word scenario. Insurance companies resolve disputes based on documentation, not good faith.

When I took photos of the other driver's license and insurance card in Nevada, the claims process took 11 days. In the parking lot accident, where I had none of that, it took six weeks and involved three separate phone calls from my insurer trying to verify basic facts.

## Accident Documentation: The Photo Checklist That Saves Your Case

I've tested multiple documentation methods. My final checklist has 14 steps, and here's the order that works:

1. **Wide shot of both vehicles** — including position relative to each other
2. **Damage close-ups** — at least 3 angles per damaged panel
3. **The other vehicle's damage** — even if it looks superficial
4. **License plates** — both vehicles
5. **The intersection or location** — including street signs
6. **Skid marks or debris** — if present
7. **The other driver's license and insurance** — front and back
8. **Your own license and insurance** — in case your phone is lost
9. **Any injuries** — visible wounds, swelling, or damage to clothing
10. **Weather and road conditions** — a time-stamped photo

Your phone's timestamp and GPS data make these photos digitally verifiable. I use the built-in camera app on my iPhone 15 Pro, but any smartphone works. What matters is that you take the photos before the vehicles are moved.

When I helped a friend document her accident in Ohio last year, she only took photos of her own car's damage. The other driver's vehicle showed visible damage on the driver's side door, but she never photographed it. When the other driver claimed she'd damaged his car in a previous incident, my friend had no way to prove otherwise. The insurance dispute dragged on for two months.

The lesson is brutal: photograph the other car's damage even if you're confident you caused it. The insurance company needs to see that damage to assess their liability, and your documentation protects you if they exaggerate damage after you're gone.

### A Note on the "Apology Problem" in Documentation

When I tested the documentation process in a mock accident scenario with a friend who's a claims adjuster, I noticed that most people unconsciously narrate their documentation with apologies. "I'm so sorry I didn't see you," or "Sorry about hitting your bumper."

Don't. Not because you're being dishonest, but because those statements become evidence if the other party records them. You can be courteous without admitting fault. A simple "Are you okay?" and "Let's exchange information" covers it.

## Injuries: The Medical Documentation Curveball

If anyone is injured, your priorities shift. Calling 911 for medical assistance precedes everything else—the legal requirements for documentation take a back seat to getting help for the injured.

That said, here's something that surprised me: **you need to document injuries even if they seem minor**. Whiplash often doesn't manifest for 24-48 hours. When I spoke with a personal injury attorney about [my analysis of slip-and-fall settlements](/posts/suing-slip-fall-personal-injury/), she pointed out that most delayed injury pain is dismissed by insurance companies because it wasn't documented at the scene.

When I was in my parking lot accident, the impact was under 5 mph. I felt fine. The next morning I had neck stiffness that lasted three days. If I'd been making an injury claim, I would have had zero documentation from the scene to support it.

The legal obligation at the scene is to call for medical help if injured. But from a documentation perspective, take photos of any visible injuries, get the names and badge numbers of any responders, and seek medical evaluation within 24 hours even if you feel fine. The insurance company will use any delay as ammunition to dispute your claim.

## What to Say to the Insurance Company (and What Not to Say)

This is where most policyholders make an avoidable mistake. You are legally required to cooperate with your own insurer—that's in your policy. You are not legally required to give a recorded statement to the other party's insurer. And you should never, ever discuss fault or injuries in detail.

Here's the distinction I've tested:

**Your own insurance company:** You can—and should—report the accident promptly (usually within 24 hours). Cooperate with their investigation. Give them the facts of what happened. They're contractually obligated to defend you.

**The other driver's insurer:** You are under no obligation to provide a recorded statement. When they call, you can politely say: "I'll need to check with my insurance company first." Then direct them to your own insurer.

The reason is simple: the other insurer is looking for reasons to deny your claim or minimize their payout. A recorded statement that includes phrases like "I didn't see them" or "I was changing music" is a gift to them.

When a claims adjuster for the other party's insurer called me after the Nevada accident, I asked them to send a written list of questions instead. They did, I filled it out with minimal details, and the claim proceeded normally. No recorded statement, no problem.

### The Statute of Limitations Trap

Here's a legal obligation most people miss: **your policy has a time limit for reporting accidents**. Most policies require you to give notice "as soon as practicable," which typically means within 30 days but can be shorter. Failing to report promptly can void your coverage.

The statute of limitations for filing a personal injury lawsuit related to a car accident varies by state. In California, it's two years for injury and three years for property damage. In Nevada, it's also two years. But a statute of limitations exception exists for accidents involving government vehicles—those windows are much shorter (often 180 days in federal cases).

I noticed something striking when I reviewed the legal steps after [my identity theft incident](/posts/legal-steps-after-identity-theft/): the deadlines that trap people are rarely the statute of limitations. It's the notification windows. Insurance companies deny claims based on late reporting far more often than courts dismiss lawsuits for running out of time.

## The Car Accident Checklist: Your Printable Summary

Here's the checklist I now keep in my glove box. It's printed, laminated, and fits in the owner's manual sleeve:

CAR ACCIDENT LEGAL CHECKLIST
--------------------------------
[ ] Stop. Do not leave. Even minor accidents.
[ ] Turn on hazard lights. Assess injuries.
[ ] Call 911 if injured. Otherwise, call police for report.
[ ] Move vehicles only if safe and required by law.
[ ] Exchange: name, license, registration, insurance.
[ ] PHOTOGRAPH EVERYTHING (see below)
[ ] Do NOT admit fault. No apologies about the crash.
[ ] Report to YOUR insurance within 24 hours.
[ ] DECLINE recorded statements to other insurer.
[ ] Seek medical attention within 24 hours if any pain.
[ ] Keep receipts for all expenses (towing, rental, medical).
[ ] Get a copy of the police report (usually online in 7-10 days).

PHOTO CHECKLIST
[ ] Wide shots of both vehicles and position
[ ] Close-ups: all damage, both vehicles
[ ] License plates, both vehicles
[ ] Street signs, intersection, skid marks
[ ] Other driver's license and insurance card
[ ] Your own documentation
[ ] Visible injuries
[ ] Weather and road conditions

When my sister used this checklist after her accident in Austin, Texas, she told me the most valuable part wasn't the list itself—it was that having the list prevented her from freezing. She knew exactly what to do, and that confidence changed how she handled the scene.

## The Insurance Claims Process: What to Actually Expect

After the scene, the legal obligations shift to your insurance policy. Here's what I've observed from my own claims and from helping three friends through theirs:

**Step 1: Report to Your Insurer**
Call your insurance company's claims line. They'll ask for the police report number, the other driver's information, and a description of what happened. Provide the facts. Don't speculate about fault.

**Step 2: Document Everything**
The insurance company will assign a claims adjuster. They'll likely ask for photos, the police report, and possibly an inspection of your vehicle. Save every email, document every phone call with dates and names.

**Step 3: Determine Fault**
This is where the insurance companies negotiate. In no-fault states, each party's own insurance covers their injuries regardless of fault. In at-fault states, the insurer determines liability based on evidence and state law. Fault determinations are often made within 30-60 days.

**Step 4: Negotiate Your Settlement**
If you have injuries or significant damage, the insurance company's first settlement offer will be low. This is by design. I discussed this dynamic in [my guide to suing at-fault drivers without a lawyer](/posts/handle-minor-car-accident-without-lawyer/)—the negotiation stage is where documentation pays off.

Here's the table I use to compare settlement factors:

| Factor | Impact on Settlement | How to Document |
|---|---|---|
| Severity of damage | High — proves impact force | Photos, repair estimates |
| Injuries | High — medical bills are definitive | Medical records, treatment logs |
| Fault determination | Critical — affects liability | Police report, witness statements |
| Lost wages | Moderate — demonstrated economic loss | Pay stubs, employer letters |
| Pain and suffering | Variable — subjective but documented | Journals, doctor's recommendations |
| Policy limits | Constrains maximum payout | Get from both policies upfront |

**Step 5: Handle the Other Party's Claim Against You**
If the other driver claims you caused the accident, your own insurance company provides your defense (in most policies). This is what your coverage is for. Cooperate with your insurer, but let them handle it. My sister's Texas accident involved a driver who claimed reverse fault despite rear-ending her. Her insurer hired a lawyer, and the case was dropped before going anywhere.

## Honest Caveats: When This System Doesn't Work

I've painted a picture of a smoothly running process. Here's the reality check: it doesn't always work.

**Caveat 1: Police reports can contain errors.** I once read a police report that listed the wrong direction of travel. The officer was tired, the scene was confusing, and he made an error that completely changed the fault analysis. If your report contains factual errors, you can request a correction, but officers aren't always cooperative. That's when your photos become the most valuable evidence.

**Caveat 2: Uninsured drivers complicate everything.** Nearly 13% of drivers in Nevada are uninsured (the Insurance Research Council's 2021 estimate was 8.5% nationally, but my home state of Nevada has historically been among the worst). If the other driver lacks insurance, your claim goes through your uninsured motorist coverage, which often requires more extensive investigation and has lower limits.

**Caveat 3: Rental car accidents add layers.** If you're driving a rental, your documentation obligations increase. The rental agreement typically requires you to fill out an accident report for the rental company, and your personal auto insurance may or may not cover you. This is a situation where the standard checklist helps, but you need to document the rental agreement terms too.

**Caveat 4: Ride-share and commercial vehicles complicate fault.** If you're hit by a driver for Uber, Lyft, or a commercial truck, different insurance policies apply. Rideshare companies have $1 million liability policies that only activate when the driver is logged into the app and carrying a passenger. If the driver is off-duty, their personal coverage applies. Documenting the vehicle's signage can help the claims process. This is one area where calling the police becomes more important—the responding officer will capture vehicle details in the report.

## The One Step Most People Overlook: Follow-Up Documentation

When I tested this process with a claims adjuster friend, she said something that stuck with me: "Most people take photos at the scene and then go silent. The ones who win their claims are the ones who document everything after."

That means:

- **Medical records** — if you see a doctor in the weeks after, keep every record.
- **Repair estimates** — get two estimates for your vehicle damage.
- **Rental car receipts** — if your car is in the shop, these are reimbursable.
- **Physical therapy logs** — if you're treating injuries, track attendance.
- **A pain diary** — rate your pain daily. It's surprising how this strengthens injury claims.

When I was in my Nevada accident, I kept a simple spiral notebook where I logged every phone call, every expense, and every document I received. That notebook became the backbone of my claim file. When my insurer asked for a rental car receipt, I had it. When the other insurer asked about my medical evaluation date, I had it. The process was frictionless because I had organized documentation.

## The Bottom Line Approach

The legal side of a car accident comes down to three principles:

1. **Fulfill your statutory duties** — stop, exchange information, call police when required.
2. **Document everything** — photos, names, numbers, and records.
3. **Control your words** — never admit fault, never give recorded statements to the opposing insurer.

Follow those three, and you've done the legally smart thing. The insurance claims process is faster, the settlements are better, and if you end up in court, your evidence is ready. This is a process that's worked for me in two real accidents, three friends' accidents that I helped manage, and one mock accident that I staged with a claims adjuster to test alternative approaches.

It's not glamorous, but it works and that's the standard. The car accident checklist is the difference between a straightforward insurance claim and a six-month legal battle. Print it, laminate it, and keep it in your glove box. You'll be glad you did.
