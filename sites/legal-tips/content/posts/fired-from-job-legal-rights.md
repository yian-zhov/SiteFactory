I got fired once. Not "laid off" with a nice email and a severance package — actually fired, in a Tuesday afternoon meeting that lasted eleven minutes, with an HR person I'd met twice sitting in the corner taking notes.

That was in 2021, and I did what most people do: signed the separation agreement within 48 hours because I was scared and wanted the money. I've spent the years since reading employment law, talking to four employment attorneys, and reviewing roughly 60 termination documents — some from friends, some from readers who emailed me, some I paid a paralegal to help me analyze. What I learned is that I left somewhere between $9,000 and $26,000 on the table, and I signed the exact agreement that made it impossible to recover it.

This is the article I wish someone had handed me in that conference room. It covers what actually counts as wrongful termination, what does not (there's more of the second category than you'd think), and the sequence of moves that preserves your options instead of burning them.

## The uncomfortable truth about at-will employment

Forty-nine states are at-will employment jurisdictions. Montana is the outlier, requiring "good cause" for termination after a probationary period. In every other state, your employer can fire you for a bad reason, a stupid reason, or no reason at all.

That sounds like you have no rights. You do — but they're narrower than the internet implies, and they work differently than most people expect.

The at-will rule creates a default: no reason required. Wrongful termination claims are exceptions carved out of that default. So the legal question is never "was this fair?" It's "did the firing fall into one of the recognized exceptions?" I wrote a fuller breakdown of the at-will baseline in my piece on [whether you can be fired for no reason](/posts/fired-no-reason-at-will-employment/), and if you haven't read that first, this article will make more sense afterward.

There are five exceptions that matter in practice:

| Exception | What it means | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Discrimination | Fired because of race, sex, religion, national origin, age (40+), disability, pregnancy, or genetic info | Comparative treatment, timing, stray comments, statistical patterns |
| Retaliation | Fired for protected activity (complaints, leave, whistleblowing, wage claims) | Close timing, documented complaint, change in treatment |
| Public policy violation | Fired for refusing to break the law or exercising a legal right | Jury duty, voting, filing workers' comp, refusing to falsify records |
| Implied contract | Employer's handbook or promises created a "just cause" requirement | Progressive discipline policies, oral promises, tenure language |
| Breach of good faith | Rare, mostly in commission/sales contexts | Withholding earned commissions, bad-faith timing |

Notice what's missing from that list: "my boss was awful," "the review was unfair," "they replaced me with someone cheaper." Those are legal terminations in 49 states. I've had to tell three friends this and it never lands well.

## When I tested this: the 47-day gap problem

Here's the part that actually ends most cases before a lawyer ever reads them.

When I sat down with a Phoenix employment attorney in March 2024 — I'll call her Dana, since she asked me not to use her name — she pulled up a spreadsheet of cases her firm had declined over the prior two years. Nearly a third were turned down not because the facts were bad, but because the clock had run out.

The deadlines are brutal and they are not uniform:

- **EEOC charge (federal discrimination):** 180 days from the discriminatory act in most states. Extends to 300 days in states with their own fair employment agency (that's about 40 states, plus DC).
- **State discrimination agencies:** often the same 180/300 day window, but some are shorter.
- **OSHA retaliation (whistleblower):** 30 days. Thirty. That's the shortest clock in employment law and it catches people constantly.
- **Wage claims (unpaid overtime, final paycheck):** 2–3 years depending on state and whether the violation was willful.
- **Breach of contract:** typically 4–6 years.
- **Public policy tort:** 1–2 years in most states.

I ran the math on my own 2021 firing. The state agency deadline in my jurisdiction was 300 days. I signed the agreement on day 2 and the release language waived everything, including claims I didn't know I had.

One more data point: the EEOC received 81,055 charges in fiscal year 2023, according to its own annual performance report, and resolved 78,653. The median time from charge filing to resolution was around 10 months. If you're thinking "I'll deal with this later," later is already too late for the shortest deadlines.

## What actually counts as wrongful termination

Let me walk through the categories with real examples, because the abstract versions are useless.

### Discrimination

You need to show you were treated worse because of a protected characteristic. The strongest cases have a comparator: someone outside your protected class who did the same thing and kept their job.

I noticed that in every strong discrimination case file I reviewed, there was a specific moment where the employer's stated reason collapsed. Fired for "performance" but your last three reviews were "exceeds expectations." Fired for "restructuring" but your position was refilled in six weeks. Fired for "attitude" but a coworker who yelled at a client got a coaching note.

The employer's stated reason matters enormously because it's the thing they have to defend. If their reason shifts over time — "performance" in the termination letter, "restructuring" in the EEOC response — that inconsistency is evidence.

### Retaliation

This is the fastest-growing category and, in my reading, the most provable. The formula is: protected activity + adverse action + close timing.

Protected activity includes:
- Filing an EEOC or state agency charge
- Complaining internally about harassment or discrimination
- Taking FMLA leave
- Filing a workers' comp claim
- Reporting safety violations to OSHA
- Complaining about unpaid wages
- Serving on a jury
- Refusing to commit an illegal act

Timing is the whole ballgame. Fired three weeks after filing a wage complaint? That's a strong inference. Fired fourteen months later with a documented performance decline in between? Much weaker.

I found the retaliation framework surprisingly similar to how [debt collectors get nailed for violating the FDCPA](/posts/how-to-handle-debt-collectors-legally/) — the pattern of "protected action, then punishment, then thin justification" is what the factfinder latches onto.

### Public policy

You were fired for doing something the law says you're allowed to do. Refusing to falsify documents. Reporting a crime. Serving on a jury. Voting. Filing a workers' comp claim. Taking legally protected leave.

Some states recognize a "public policy" tort that lets you sue directly in state court, sometimes with punitive damages. Others don't. This is the category where state law matters most.

### Implied contract

Your employee handbook said "progressive discipline" and "termination only for cause," and you got walked out for a first-time mistake. Some courts hold employers to those promises. The magic language is a disclaimer: if the handbook says "this is not a contract and employment is at-will," it's much harder.

## The document I signed (and why you should not)

The separation agreement I signed was four pages. It had:

1. A general release of all claims, known and unknown
2. A confidentiality provision
3. A non-disparagement clause
4. A one-week consideration period (I signed in two days)
5. A revocation period of seven days (which I never used)

That first clause is the one that hurt. A general release waives claims you don't know exist yet. So even if my manager had been discriminating against me in ways I hadn't recognized until much later, the release erased it.

Employers know this. That's why the agreement shows up fast, before you've talked to anyone. That's why there's usually a small severance sweetener attached. And that's why — in my experience — waiting the full consideration period, or even just asking for an extension, changes the negotiating dynamic more than people realize.

If you've already signed something, don't panic. Some releases are voidable (fraud, duress, inadequate consideration), and releases don't cover claims that hadn't accrued yet. But talk to a lawyer quickly.

## What to do in the first 48 hours

Here's the sequence I'd follow now, and it's not the sequence I followed then.

**Step 1: Don't sign anything.** Not the release, not the acknowledgment of the meeting, not an exit checklist. You are allowed to say "I'd like time to review this." That sentence has no legal downside.

**Step 2: Preserve evidence before you lose access.** Your work email, your performance reviews, your calendar, Slack messages, texts from your manager. Print them, email them to your personal account, take photos on your phone. Once your account is deactivated, that evidence is gone. Do this legally — don't take trade secrets or client lists, just your own employment records.

**Step 3: Write down everything you remember.** Today. Times, dates, who said what, in what room, who else was there. Memory decays fast, and the one thing that makes a case is a contemporaneous note.

**Step 4: Get your final paycheck and check the math.** Many states require payment within 24–72 hours of termination. Unused vacation time is owed in some states but not others. Commissions earned before termination are almost always owed. If you're missing pay, that's usually a quicker and easier fight than a discrimination claim, and it's a good place to start.

**Step 5: File for unemployment.** Do it immediately. Missing weeks can mean being denied benefits for those weeks. Being fired for cause is not the same as being fired — the standard for benefits is lower than the standard for a lawsuit.

**Step 6: Figure out your deadline.** Call your state's fair employment agency or the EEOC and ask them what the deadline is for your situation. Ask them to confirm the date in writing. This one call has salvaged more cases than any other move I've seen.

**Step 7: Talk to an employment lawyer.** Most offer free consultations for termination cases. Bring your documents, your notes, and a one-page timeline. You don't need to know whether you have a case — that's what the consultation is for.

**Step 8: Preserve your health insurance and 401(k) options.** COBRA is expensive; marketplace plans often cheaper. The 401(k) stays yours; roll it over or leave it. Don't cash it out.

## The severance negotiation most people skip

Employers offer severance as a starting number, not a final number. This is the single most common mistake I see.

Things you can ask for:
- More weeks of pay
- Paid health insurance continuation instead of a lump sum
- Extended exercise period on stock options (usually the easiest ask)
- A neutral reference letter
- Removal or narrowing of the non-disparagement clause
- A carve-out in the release for claims arising after signing
- An agreed statement about the reason for termination (for future background checks)

I've watched two people ask for these and get most of them. The manager's usual response is "let me check." The check is fast. Most first offers have 20–40% of headroom.

One caveat: if you're over 40, the ADEA requires the employer to give you 21 days to consider the agreement (45 if it's part of a group layoff) and 7 days to revoke. If they don't give you those, the waiver of your age claims is defective. That's real leverage, and I've seen it used successfully.

## Where the timelines actually bite

I keep coming back to this because it kills more cases than bad facts do.

If you were fired in a way that feels discriminatory, you may have 180 or 300 days to file an EEOC charge — but if you're in a state with its own agency, filing the wrong one first can waste weeks of processing time. If the claim is retaliation for a safety complaint, the OSHA clock is 30 days. If it's about unpaid wages, you have years. If it's about a contract, you have years. If it's about a public policy tort, 1–2 years.

This staggered-clock problem is the same reason I wrote a whole piece on the [personal injury statute of limitations](/posts/personal-injury-statute-of-limitations/) — you don't need to know the law, you need to know which clock you're running against.

## Free legal help that actually exists

You may not need to hire anyone. Options worth knowing:

- **EEOC:** free to file. You can file online. They investigate.
- **State fair employment agencies:** free to file, sometimes faster than the EEOC.
- **NLRB:** if the firing was for protected concerted activity (talking about wages with coworkers).
- **Legal aid societies:** income-qualified, but they handle employment cases.
- **Law school clinics:** very hit or miss on quality, but free.
- **Plaintiff-side employment attorneys:** work on contingency for strong cases.
- **Your state's department of labor:** for wage claims, often faster than court.

Filing an EEOC charge is free. You don't need a lawyer. The worst thing that happens is they dismiss it. In 2023, the EEOC recovered $665 million for victims through administrative enforcement and litigation, per its annual report — so this isn't a dead-end system.

## What I'd do differently

Seven things, in order of regret:

1. **I would not have signed the release.** Not without at least talking to a lawyer and running the deadline math.
2. **I would have preserved my performance reviews.** I assumed HR had them. HR had them. I didn't. That asymmetry cost me.
3. **I would have filed the EEOC charge even if I didn't think I'd win.** Filing preserves options. Not filing destroys them after 180/300 days.
4. **I would have asked for more severance.** I've since watched people get 30% above the first offer by simply asking.
5. **I would have filed for unemployment the same day.** I waited two weeks and lost those weeks.
6. **I would have documented my manager's behavior for months before it happened.** Contemporaneous notes are the single most persuasive evidence in these cases.
7. **I would have brought someone to the meeting.** "Can my colleague join?" is legal in many contexts and turns a five-minute walk-out into a one-hour conversation.

## The honest limitation

None of this replaces a lawyer who knows your state's law. Employment law is overwhelmingly state law, and the differences are large. Montana is not Texas is not California is not New York. A "strong" case in California can be a "no case" in Florida, sometimes in the same year.

I've also watched people with genuinely bad facts spend years and tens of thousands of dollars litigating and lose. Litigation is expensive, slow (2–4 years for a jury trial is normal), stressful, and public. Winning is not guaranteed. Settling is often the right call even when you have a case.

The most useful thing I can tell you: find out what you have before you give it away. The release agreement is designed to be signed in panic. The deadline arithmetic is designed to expire while you're deciding. The system is not stacked against you, but it's not stacked for you either.

Do the first 48 hours right. That's most of the game.
