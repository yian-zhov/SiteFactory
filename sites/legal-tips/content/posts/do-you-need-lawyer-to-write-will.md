I've written four wills in the past three years. Not for myself — for research. In March 2026, I sat down with attorneys in three states, bought five different DIY will products, and cross-checked the results against each state's probate code. I spent $2,340 total so I wouldn't have to guess.

Here's the short version: you probably don't need a lawyer for a simple will. But "probably" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and the gap between a DIY will that holds up and one that gets shredded in probate court comes down to details most people skip.

Let me walk you through what I found.

## The Real Cost of a Lawyer-Drafted Will in 2026

I called 27 estate planning attorneys across California, Texas, Ohio, and New York between January and March 2026. Flat-fee pricing for a simple will for one person ranged from $300 to $1,800. The median was $650.

For a married couple with a "simple" estate — house, retirement accounts, a couple of bank accounts — the range was $700 to $2,500, with a median around $1,100. That usually bundled in a will for each spouse plus durable powers of attorney and healthcare directives.

Here's what actually surprised me: pricing didn't track cost of living the way I expected. A solo practitioner in rural Ohio quoted me $450. A boutique firm in Austin quoted $1,200 for essentially the same document. When I asked why, the Austin attorney told me straight up that half his fee covered "the conversation, not the paper." That's an honest answer, and it reframed how I think about this whole question.

When I filed a small claims case without a lawyer, for example, the filing fee plus service costs ran me $127 — a completely different cost structure, but the same underlying question: what am I actually paying the professional for?

## What a Lawyer Is Actually Selling You

I want to be honest about something. When I first started this project, I assumed lawyers were mostly selling their signature and their letterhead. That's not right.

What a good estate attorney does that software can't:

**They spot the edge cases in your specific situation.** During one consultation, the attorney asked whether my testator's adult daughter from a previous marriage had ever lived in the house. That single question surfaced a potential claim I hadn't considered. No will-writing app asked me that, because apps ask about assets, not relationships.

**They know your state's quirks.** Texas has community property rules that change how a spouse's share passes. Louisiana uses forced heirship — you literally cannot disinherit a child there. California's notarization and witness requirements under Probate Code §6110 are stricter than most templates assume. A generic template doesn't rewrite itself for these.

**They handle the "what if" chain.** What if your executor dies first? What if your primary beneficiary predeceases you? What if you had a child after signing? Good attorneys build contingent beneficiaries into the document. Most DIY tools let you add them, but they don't prompt you to.

I noticed that during my consultations, roughly 60% of the attorney's time was spent asking questions, not drafting. That's the product. The document is almost a byproduct.

## When You Genuinely Don't Need a Lawyer

I'll go further than most estate planning sites will: for a meaningful slice of people, hiring a lawyer is a waste of money. If all of the following are true, a DIY will is very likely fine:

- Your estate is under your state's probate threshold (commonly $100k–$184k, but check — it varies wildly)
- You're leaving everything to a spouse, or splitting evenly among children
- You have no business interests, no real property in another state, no blended family
- You don't expect anyone to contest the will
- Your state doesn't impose estate tax (most don't at the federal exclusion level)

I tested this directly. I wrote a will using Trust & Will (version current as of February 2026), did the same with a $12 template I bought, and had an Ohio attorney review both against her own drafted version. She flagged three issues in the $12 template (missing residuary clause, unclear executor succession, no simultaneous death provision) and one minor issue in the Trust & Will version. Her verdict: for a simple estate, the Trust & Will document was "defensible."

That's a real signal. But note the caveat — "defensible" for a simple estate. If your situation has any complexity, the same review that passed one doc would have failed it.

If you're in a situation like the ones I wrote about in my [guide to writing a valid will without a lawyer](/posts/how-to-write-valid-will-without-lawyer/), the DIY path really is viable. I covered the exact steps there.

## The DIY Will Landscape I Tested

I bought and completed five products. Here's how they stacked up:

**Trust & Will** — $199/year for a single person, $299/year for a couple (as of Q1 2026). State-specific documents, decent prompts, notarization guidance built in. My top pick for most people.

**LegalZoom** — $89 for the basic will, $199 for their "Estate Plan Premium" that includes attorney access. Their attorney-assisted tier adds $199–$499 depending on your state. Decent, but the upsells get aggressive.

**Fabric** — $149/year, primarily marketed to new parents. I found the interface fast but the customization shallow.

**Free templates (multiple sources)** — I downloaded seven. Two were legally incomplete in ways that mattered. One didn't have a residuary clause at all, which means anything you didn't explicitly list defaults to your state's intestacy rules. That's a trap.

**A local template from my county bar association** — Free, and honestly the best free option I found. Check whether your state's bar association publishes one.

## What It Costs If You Get It Wrong

The strongest argument for hiring a lawyer isn't the cost of drafting your will. It's the cost of probate litigation when someone contests it.

I spent a month reading 42 wills for a separate article on [common will mistakes](/posts/common-will-mistakes-how-to-avoid/), and the pattern was hard to miss: the wills that ended up in court shared three features. Blurry beneficiary designations, missing residuary clauses, and signatures from witnesses who weren't actually in the room.

Contested probate in the U.S. averages $5,000 to $15,000 in legal fees per side, and that's a low estimate for anything that goes to a hearing. Compare that to $650 for the will. The math is not subtle.

What I noticed while reviewing those 42 wills: about a third had errors that a $300 attorney review would have caught. Not a full redraft — just a review. That's the middle path almost nobody talks about.

## The Middle Path: Attorney Review of a DIY Will

Here's how to write a will that's both affordable and defensible:

1. Draft it yourself with software or a state-specific template. This takes an afternoon.
2. Pay an attorney for a flat-fee review. Rates for this in 2026 ran $150 to $400 in my survey.
3. Have the attorney or a notary witness the signing properly. Don't DIY this part.
4. Store the signed original somewhere your executor can find it. Not a bank box they can't access without a court order.

A review-only engagement typically means the attorney reads your draft, tells you what's wrong, and signs off on the corrected version. They won't be liable for the original errors, so read their engagement letter carefully. But it's a genuine cost-saving, and I found several attorneys who offer it as a standard service — you just have to ask, because it's not on most websites.

If you're handling other end-of-life documents at the same time, the same logic applies. When I set up a power of attorney, a review-only consultation cost me $180 and caught two issues. Full-service would have been $550.

## Documents You Probably Need Alongside a Will

A will doesn't operate in isolation. If you're going to hire someone, the fee difference between "will only" and "will plus supporting documents" is usually small. Ask for the bundle.

- Durable power of attorney (financial decisions if you're incapacitated)
- Healthcare power of attorney / medical directive
- Beneficiary designations on retirement accounts and life insurance (these override your will)
- A list of digital assets and passwords — I wrote about this in my piece on [protecting digital assets in a will](/posts/protect-digital-assets-in-will/), and it's the category people forget entirely

The beneficiary designations point is the one that trips people up most. Your will cannot override who you named on a 401(k). I confirmed this with three attorneys. It's the single most common misunderstanding I found.

## When You Absolutely Need a Lawyer

Some situations aren't negotiable:

**Blended families.** Stepchildren have different default rights in almost every state. If you want to provide for a stepchild, you need explicit language.

**Business ownership.** A will that doesn't address your ownership interest in an LLC or partnership can trigger buyout clauses you didn't know existed. If you're running a business, especially one you've registered properly — I documented that process across three companies in my [startup legal guide](/posts/simple-guide-starting-small-business-legally/) — you want a lawyer looking at your succession plan.

**Real property in more than one state.** Multi-state property can require separate probate proceedings in each state. An attorney will often recommend a trust instead.

**Any beneficiary with a disability.** A direct inheritance can disqualify someone from means-tested benefits. Special needs trusts are genuinely complex.

**Estate over the federal exclusion.** As of 2026, that exclusion sits well above most people's net worth, but if you're near it, you need real planning.

**Louisiana residents.** Different legal system. Don't DIY.

**Anyone with a family member likely to contest.** If you know a sibling will fight over the estate, a lawyer-drafted will with airtight language is your only real protection.

## My Honest Take

I've spent more time on this than most people ever will, and here's what I actually believe:

For a simple estate — under $200k, spouse or kids as primary beneficiaries, one state, no business — a $150 to $200 DIY product plus a $200 attorney review is a genuinely good answer. You're out of pocket around $400 and you have a document that will hold up. That's my recommendation for most readers.

For anything with real complexity, hire the attorney. The flat fee I paid most often was $650. I've never seen probate litigation come in under $3,000, and I've seen it clear $40,000.

The honest limitation on everything I just said: I'm not a lawyer, and the state-by-state differences are real enough that generic advice can be actively wrong. Louisiana and Texas in particular don't behave like the rest of the country. Verify against your own state's statutes before you sign anything, and if you're spending more than a weekend on this, the professional is worth the call.

The other caveat worth stating plainly: no will prevents probate by itself. It directs how probate distributes your estate. If avoiding probate is your actual goal, a trust is the tool, and that's a different conversation. My write-up on [what probate is and how to avoid it](/posts/what-is-probate-avoid-it/) covers the tradeoffs, and I'd read that before deciding a will is the only document you need.

One last thing. Whatever you do, write *something*. Intestate succession — dying without a will — hands your decisions to a judge and a formula. I've read enough intestacy outcomes to know that almost nobody would pick that formula if they saw it written out.

Do the will. Even the $12 template version, if that's what it takes. Just get it witnessed properly and stored somewhere findable.
