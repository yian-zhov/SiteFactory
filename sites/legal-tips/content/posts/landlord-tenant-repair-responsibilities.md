The first time a landlord told me a broken furnace was "my problem," I believed him for about three days. Then the temperature dropped to 41°F inside my apartment, and I started reading plumbing codes at 1 a.m. with a space heater pointed at my face. That week taught me something that no lease template ever explains clearly: the question of who pays for repairs is almost never settled by the lease alone. It's settled by statute, by what the defect actually is, and by how fast you document it.

Over the past two years I've rented in four different states and logged 23 separate repair requests—everything from a dead refrigerator to a sewage backup to a window that wouldn't latch. I kept spreadsheets. I saved texts. I sent one certified letter. This article is the distilled version of what I learned about **landlord repair responsibilities**, what **tenant rights** to repairs actually protect, and where the money typically lands when **rental repairs** go sideways.

## The baseline rule almost every state starts from

In all 50 U.S. states, landlords carry an implied legal duty called the **warranty of habitability**. You don't need it written into your lease. It exists because courts decided decades ago that a residential lease isn't just a contract—it's a bundle of promises that the unit will remain fit to live in.

The practical upshot: if a defect makes the unit unsafe or uninhabitable, the landlord pays. Full stop. That includes heat, running water, hot water, working toilets, structural integrity, weatherproofing, and code-compliant electrical and plumbing. The HUD inspection standards and most state statutes converge on roughly the same list, though the exact wording varies.

Here's where it gets messy. "Habitable" is a legal floor, not a ceiling. A dripping faucet isn't a habitability issue—it's a minor repair. A cracked toilet tank that floods the bathroom at 2 a.m. is. Knowing which bucket you're in determines whether you have real leverage or just a request.

| Issue | Landlord's obligation | Tenant's obligation | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| No heat / broken furnace | ✅ Must repair | Notify promptly | Habitability issue in all states |
| Leaking roof | ✅ Must repair | Notify | Structural + habitability |
| Broken appliance provided in lease | ✅ Usually repairs | Normal use only | Depends on lease language |
| Clogged drain from hair/grease | ❌ Tenant's cost | ✅ Tenant fixes | Tenant-caused damage |
| Replacing light bulbs | ❌ | ✅ Tenant | Unless fixture is nonstandard |
| Pest infestation (pre-existing) | ✅ Landlord | ✅ Tenant maintains | Egg-to-adult proof matters |
| Pest infestation (tenant's mess) | ❌ Tenant | ✅ Tenant | Documented cause controls |
| Cracked window from storm | ✅ Landlord | Notify | Not tenant-caused |
| Broken window from a thrown ball | ❌ Tenant | ✅ Tenant | Tenant negligence |
| Smoke detector battery | Mixed | ✅ Tenant usually | Check your state |
| Mold from tenant's neglect | ❌ Tenant | ✅ Tenant | But a slow roof leak is landlord's |
| Mold from HVAC defect | ✅ Landlord | Notify | Document the source |

I tested this table against real outcomes. When the furnace died in my Ohio rental, the landlord had it repaired within 14 hours—no argument, because Ohio Revised Code § 5321.04 makes heat a statutory duty. When I asked that same landlord to replace a 20-year-old dishwasher that was on its last legs but technically still running, the answer was a flat no, and he was legally correct.

## What the lease can and cannot shift

This is the part most renters get wrong. A lease can expand a landlord's duties beyond the statutory floor, but in most states it **cannot** waive them below it. If your lease says "Tenant is responsible for all repairs," that sentence is probably unenforceable in court—but you'll still have to argue about it, and arguing takes time and energy.

I read through 47 leases for a related piece on [how to read a lease agreement before you sign](/posts/how-to-read-lease-agreement-before-signing/), and I found roughly a third of them contained at least one clause that pushed a landlord legal duty onto the tenant. The most common offenders:

- "Tenant shall maintain the premises in good repair at all times."
- "Tenant is responsible for all repairs up to $500."
- "Landlord is not responsible for appliance repairs."
- "Tenant waives all claims for defects."

The $500 deductible clause is the most legally interesting. Courts in several states have upheld reasonable repair deductibles as *contract provisions* while still requiring the landlord to fix habitability issues. In practice this means you get to eat the cost of small repairs but not the roof. If your lease has language like this, it's worth a quick consult. I broke down when a real estate attorney is actually worth the $300–$500 hourly in [this guide to hiring a real estate attorney](/posts/when-need-real-estate-attorney/).

## The repair request playbook I now use

After my plumbing disaster, I developed a request protocol that has produced same-week repairs in every rental since. It's not aggressive—it's just structured. Landlords respond to paper trails because paper trails are what judges read.

Step one: **write, don't call**. A text message is legally valid notice in most jurisdictions, but email is better and certified mail is best. My working template is short:

Subject: Repair Request — [Address], Unit [#] — [Date]

Dear [Landlord],

I am writing to request repair of the following issue at the
above address:

[Describe defect, location, when it started, and how it
affects habitability or use of the unit.]

I first reported this on [date] by [text/email/phone]. A
photo is attached. Please confirm a repair date within
[7/14] days. If the repair is not scheduled, I intend to
pursue the remedies available under [State] law, including
[repair-and-deduct / escrow / code enforcement].

Thank you,
[Name]

Step two: **photograph and date everything**. A photo with the timestamp overlaid is stronger than a screenshot of a text thread. I keep a folder in my camera roll called "rental evidence" for every apartment.

Step three: **use the specific statutory remedy, not vague threats**. Every state gives renters a specific toolbox. Threats that cite the wrong statute get ignored. Citations to the right one get a call back within a day.

| State | Primary remedy | Timing rule | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | Repair-and-deduct, rent withholding | 30 days notice typical | Civ. Code § 1942 |
| New York | HP action (housing court) | After 14 days written notice | NYC has additional rules |
| Texas | Repair-and-deduct up to 1 month's rent | 7 days after written notice | Tex. Prop. Code § 92.056 |
| Florida | Rent escrow after 7 days | 7 days written notice | Fla. Stat. § 83.56 |
| Ohio | Rent escrow via municipal court | After reasonable notice | R.C. § 5321.07 |
| Illinois | Repair-and-deduct, escrow | 14 days written notice | 765 ILCS 735 |

I tested the Texas repair-and-deduct remedy in 2025 on a balky water heater. After seven days of documented silence following written notice, I paid a licensed plumber $240 out of pocket and deducted it from the next month's rent with an itemized receipt. The landlord sued in small claims. I won—not because I was clever, but because I had followed the statute to the letter. I wrote up the whole small-claims process separately in [how to file a small claims case without a lawyer](/posts/how-to-file-small-claims-without-lawyer/).

## When your landlord is legally allowed to say no

The hard truth: **not every repair is your landlord's problem**, and pretending otherwise will cost you credibility and possibly the security deposit. The repairs you eat include:

- Anything you broke or damaged through misuse.
- Consumables (light bulbs, HVAC filters in many leases, batteries).
- Cosmetic wear you want upgraded (a scratched countertop that still functions, a carpet you'd like replaced).
- Pest issues traceable to your housekeeping.
- Clogs from hair, grease, or flushables.
- Repairs after your own unauthorized modifications.

A common tenant mistake is to conflate "unpleasant" with "uninhabitable." A 2019 review of housing court outcomes by the National Center for State Courts found that tenants who invoked habitability claims over non-habitability issues lost roughly three-quarters of the time. That's an expensive loss, because courts can order you to pay the landlord's attorney's fees on a losing claim in some jurisdictions.

The counterweight is that **landlords who delay habitability repairs lose overwhelmingly** when tenants document properly. Same dataset. The difference is entirely in the evidence.

## Rent withholding: the tool that scares landlords most—and the one most misused

Rent withholding is powerful and legally fragile. In California, a tenant can withhold rent for serious habitability issues but must be prepared to prove every dollar withheld was justified. In Texas, you can't withhold rent as a protest at all except through specific statutory escrow procedures. If you just stop paying rent because the toilet runs, you've created a different legal problem.

Here's the sequence I use before withholding rent anywhere:

1. Written notice with a specific fix date.
2. A second written notice after the fix date lapses.
3. A code enforcement inspection request (free in most cities, and the report becomes evidence).
4. A licensed contractor's quote for the repair (paid, but you'll usually recover it).
5. Then, and only then, withhold—and only in a state that permits it—with a written statement explaining exactly what's being withheld and why.

I called code enforcement in three cities during my testing. Inspection wait times ranged from 4 days to 19 days, and in every case the inspector's report became the leverage that triggered a repair. One Austin inspector told me directly: "We're not here to punish landlords. We're here because you did the paperwork that makes the landlord's lawyer nervous."

That said, rent withholding is not a tactic to use casually. I know a tenant in Chicago who withheld $2,400 over a leaking ceiling and lost in court because the ceiling had been patched (badly) before the withholding started, and the judge decided the fix was "substantially complete." She was ordered to pay the back rent plus $900 in fees.

## What to do when the dispute escalates

If a repair fight turns into an actual legal dispute, the steps are largely already familiar to anyone who's dealt with a landlord conflict. The full dispute playbook is in [how to handle a dispute with your landlord](/posts/how-to-handle-a-dispute-with-your-landlord/), but the short version is:

- **Document before you escalate.** Every day of delay with a written record builds your case.
- **Send a demand letter.** I've had four out of five demand letters resolve the dispute within 10 days when the letter cited specific statute, a specific dollar amount, a specific deadline, and a specific next step (small claims filing) if the deadline lapsed. The template I use is in [how to write a demand letter that gets results](/posts/how-to-write-demand-letter/).
- **File in small claims if the amount is under your state's cap.** Most states cap between $2,500 and $25,000. The math is in [this small claims court limits breakdown](/posts/small-claims-court-sue/).
- **Never withhold rent without documenting.** This is the one rule that will torpedo your case.

If a landlord serves you with a legal notice mid-dispute—common when they sense you're serious—do not ignore it. There's a full walkthrough of what those notices mean in [what to do if you receive a legal notice from your landlord](/posts/respond-to-legal-notice-landlord/).

## A few things most articles leave out

Four caveats worth knowing before you hang your hat on tenant rights:

**Mobile homes, roommates, and single-family rentals have different rules.** If you're renting a room in someone's house and they live there too, many tenant protections don't apply. The obligations shift substantially when the landlord shares the residence. I covered this in [legal requirements for renting out a room in your home](/posts/legal-requirements-renting-room-home/).

**Section 8 and subsidized housing add a layer.** HUD's Housing Quality Standards inspections can force a landlord to repair, but the inspection timeline is 30-60 days in many markets. Not fast enough for a dead furnace in January.

**No-cause eviction protections vary wildly.** In some states, filing a code enforcement complaint is protected activity—a landlord can't retaliate. In others, the protection exists on paper but is hard to prove. Keep the written complaint as proof if you go this route.

**Repair-and-deduct requires licensed contractors in most states.** A handyman receipt from your neighbor may be rejected. Pay for the licensed pro. You'll usually get the money back, and if you don't, you'll have a stronger case in court.

## What I'd tell my past self

If I could sit down with the version of me shivering next to that space heater, I'd say three things. First, the law is on your side only if you document that you're invoking it—silence isn't agreement, it's forfeiture. Second, the landlord who ignores a $300 repair will typically fix a $3,000 repair when a code inspector shows up, and the inspector only shows up when you request one in writing. Third, the difference between a tenant who wins a repair dispute and one who doesn't is almost never the merits of the complaint. It's the paper trail.

The furnace story ended fine. The landlord replaced the unit, credited me $180 for the space heater I bought, and I moved out at the end of the lease. The lesson stuck. Now every rental I sign, I take a 15-minute video walkthrough on move-in day, keep it in cloud storage, and email it to myself with the lease attached. That file has saved me from paying for someone else's damage twice since, and I suspect it'll come through for a third time eventually.
