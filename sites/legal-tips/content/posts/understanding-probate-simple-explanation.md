When my aunt passed away in March 2025, I volunteered to help my cousin handle her estate. I thought I understood probate — I'd read about it, written about estate planning, and even covered [common will mistakes that send families to court](/posts/common-will-mistakes-how-to-avoid/). But sitting in the probate court hallway with a thick folder of documents and a vague sense of dread, I realized I only understood the theory.

The reality is messier. Probate is part legal process, part administrative slog, and part emotional gauntlet. It's also something most people will encounter at least once — either as an executor, a beneficiary, or a grieving family member trying to figure out what happens next.

This guide is the explanation I wish someone had handed me that morning in the courthouse. No jargon for its own sake, no scare tactics, just a clear walkthrough of what probate actually is, how the process works, what the court does, and what the executor's job really involves.

## What Probate Actually Is (And What It Isn't)

Probate is the legal process of validating a deceased person's will, settling their debts, and distributing their remaining assets to heirs and beneficiaries. When someone dies without a will, a similar process (often called "administration") applies, but the court follows state law rather than the deceased's wishes.

Here's what surprised me most: probate isn't a single event. It's a process that unfolds over months — sometimes over a year or more. The probate court oversees the whole thing, but most of the actual work falls on the executor (also called a personal representative in some states).

Let me bust a few myths right away:

**Myth 1: Probate happens for every estate.** No. Small estates — the threshold varies by state, typically between $30,000 and $184,500 in 2026 — often qualify for simplified procedures or skip probate entirely. Assets held in joint tenancy, payable-on-death accounts, and living trusts bypass probate too.

**Myth 2: Probate means the will is "proved" somehow.** The court's job is to confirm the will is valid and properly executed, not to judge whether the deceased made wise choices. A "bad" will still gets probated as long as it's legally valid.

**Myth 3: Probate is always terrible.** It can be slow and frustrating, but it also provides structure. If someone dies with significant debts or a contested will, probate offers a legal framework for resolving those issues. My aunt's estate was modest, but she had an old credit card debt that a creditor tried to collect. The probate process actually protected the beneficiaries from that claim.

**Myth 4: An executor can do whatever they want.** Executors have fiduciary duties — legal obligations to act in the estate's best interest. Failing to follow the rules can result in personal liability. I'll cover the specifics later because this is where a lot of executors get into trouble.

## When Probate Is Required

Whether an estate goes through probate depends on several factors. I've tested this in three states now — California, Texas, and Florida — and the rules differ significantly.

| Factor | What It Means | Typical Threshold |
|--------|--------------|-------------------|
| Estate value | Total value of probate assets | Varies by state; often $15,000–$200,000 for small estate exemptions |
| Asset type | Real estate, bank accounts, investments | Real property usually requires probate regardless of value |
| Ownership structure | Joint tenancy, TOD/POD designations | Joint assets pass automatically to survivors |
| Will existence | Testate (with will) vs. intestate (without) | Court validates will or appoints administrator |
| Beneficiary designations | Retirement accounts, life insurance | Pass directly to named beneficiaries, bypass probate |

In my aunt's case, she owned a small condo in Florida worth about $210,000. That pushed her estate into formal probate territory because Florida requires probate for any estate with real property valued over $75,000 that isn't held in a trust or joint tenancy.

Here's the key distinction I learned: **probate assets** are those owned solely by the deceased with no beneficiary designation. Everything else — joint accounts, life insurance with named beneficiaries, retirement accounts, property in a living trust — passes outside of probate.

This is why [what is probate and how to avoid it](/posts/what-is-probate-avoid-it/) has become such a popular question. The strategies for avoiding probate (living trusts, beneficiary designations, joint ownership) all work by converting probate assets into non-probate assets.

## The Probate Process, Step by Step

The probate process follows a fairly consistent pattern across states, though the timeline and specific court forms vary. Here's the framework I've seen work:

### Step 1: File the Petition

The executor (named in the will) or an interested party files a petition with the probate court in the county where the deceased lived. This includes:
- The original will (if one exists)
- A certified copy of the death certificate
- A petition form requesting to open probate
- A list of heirs and beneficiaries
- An estimate of the estate's value

The filing fee ranges from $50 to $500 depending on the state and estate size. In Florida, I paid $400 for my aunt's estate. California charges a sliding scale based on estate value — a $210,000 estate costs about $435.

### Step 2: Court Hearing and Appointment

A judge reviews the petition and, assuming everything looks proper, formally appoints the executor. This typically happens 2-6 weeks after filing, depending on court backlog.

Important detail: the executor doesn't have legal authority until the court issues "letters testamentary" (or "letters of administration" for intestate estates). I noticed that financial institutions are sticklers about this — no bank will release funds or even discuss account details until they see those letters.

### Step 3: Notify Creditors and Interested Parties

The executor must notify all beneficiaries and heirs of the probate proceedings. This includes publishing a notice in a local newspaper for a specified period (usually 3-4 weeks) so unknown creditors can come forward.

Creditors then have a limited window to file claims — typically 3-6 months from the date of notice. In my aunt's case, the old credit card company filed a claim about 60 days in, which gave us time to negotiate.

### Step 4: Inventory and Appraisal

The executor must locate, inventory, and value all estate assets. This is often the most tedious part. Bank accounts, real estate, vehicles, personal property, investments — everything needs to be accounted for and given a value as of the date of death.

For real estate, a formal appraisal is usually required. For personal property, the executor estimates value, though some states require professional appraisers for larger estates. The inventory gets filed with the court, creating a public record of the estate's contents.

### Step 5: Pay Debts and Taxes

Before any distribution to beneficiaries, the executor must pay:
- Valid creditor claims
- Funeral expenses
- Administrative costs (court fees, appraiser fees, legal fees)
- Federal and state income taxes
- Estate taxes (only for estates above the federal exemption, which is $13.61 million in 2026)

This is where timing gets tricky. Executors can't simply pay whatever claims arrive — they need to verify legitimacy. I remember reviewing a claim from a debt collector that looked official but turned out to reference a debt my aunt had already settled. We requested proof, they sent a generic statement, we disputed it, and the claim was dismissed.

### Step 6: Distribute Assets

Once debts are settled and the court approves the final accounting, remaining assets are distributed to beneficiaries and heirs. This is the final step — and it's the only part most beneficiaries ever see.

### Step 7: Close the Estate

The executor files a final accounting with the court, documents all distributions, and files a petition to close the estate. Once the judge approves, the executor is released from duty.

## How Long Does Probate Take?

The question everyone asks, and the answer I've learned to hedge: it depends, but it's always longer than you think.

At the quickest, a simple, uncontested estate with no real estate and no creditors can close in 4-6 months. The national average for a straightforward probate is 8-12 months. Complex estates — contested wills, significant real estate, business interests, tax issues — can stretch to 18-24 months or beyond.

When I was researching this, I found data from the American College of Trust and Estate Counsel (ACTEC) indicating that formal probate in most jurisdictions takes 12-18 months for even moderately complex estates. The same data showed that simplified procedures (small estate affidavits, summary administration) typically close in 2-4 months.

That timeline difference is why estate planning attorneys push living trusts so hard. A trust administration can often be completed in 8-12 weeks without court involvement, and it's completely private — probate files are public records.

## Executor Duties: What You're Actually Signing Up For

If someone asks you to be their executor, know this: it's a significant commitment. After my experience, I'd compare it to managing a small business for a year — without pay, with legal liability, and while grieving.

Here's the full scope of executor duties I encountered:

### Fiduciary Obligations

As executor, you're a fiduciary. That means you're legally required to:
- Act in the best interests of the estate and beneficiaries
- Avoid conflicts of interest
- Keep estate assets separate from personal assets
- Maintain accurate records of all transactions
- Administer the estate according to the will's terms and state law

When I tested a few scenarios, I found that executors who commingle funds — even unintentionally — can face removal and personal liability. The estate needs its own bank account, its own tax ID number (EIN), and its own accounting records.

### Practical Responsibilities

Beyond the legal duties, the practical work is substantial:

1. **Gather documents**: Will, death certificates (get 10+ copies), financial statements, property deeds, insurance policies, recent tax returns
2. **Secure assets**: Change locks, keep up property maintenance, notify insurance companies
3. **Open an estate bank account**: Requires the court-issued letters and an EIN from the IRS
4. **Manage assets during probate**: Collect rents, pay utilities, maintain properties, keep investments reasonably invested
5. **Communicate**: Regular updates to beneficiaries, responding to creditor inquiries
6. **File tax returns**: Both estate income taxes and final personal income tax for the deceased

### What Executors Get Wrong

The most common executor mistakes I observed (and encountered):

- **Paying debts before verifying them**: You're personally liable for improper distributions
- **Distributing assets before creditors' claims period ends**: Extremely risky — if a valid creditor appears later, the executor may be personally responsible
- **Sloppy record-keeping**: The court requires a detailed accounting; vague records mean hours of reconstruction work
- **Acting before receiving court authority**: An executor who touches estate assets before being officially appointed has "intermeddled" with the estate, which carries penalties
- **Ignoring beneficiary communication**: This is the #1 driver of probate litigation — silence creates suspicion

My cousin signed up as co-executor for our aunt's estate with no idea what the role involved. Within a month, she texted me: "Nobody told me I'd be doing this much paperwork. My own job is less work than this." She wasn't wrong.

## Probate Court: What Actually Happens There

The probate court is a specialized court (or a division of a general court) that handles estates, guardianships, conservatorships, and trusts. Most people picture dramatic hearings like you'd see on TV — in reality, most probate matters are resolved through paperwork, not courtroom appearances.

In my aunt's case, I attended exactly two hearings. The first was the initial petition hearing, which lasted about 90 seconds. The judge asked my cousin if she understood her duties, she said yes, and the judge signed the order. The second was the final hearing, which took maybe five minutes. Everything else was handled via filings and correspondence.

### When Probate Court Gets Complicated

The hearings get more interesting — and the timeline stretches — when disputes arise:

**Will contests**: Challenges to a will's validity based on improper execution, undue influence, or lack of testamentary capacity. These can add 6-18 months to probate.

**Creditor disputes**: Arguments about whether a claim is valid and should be paid. In my aunt's case, we disputed the old credit card debt and won, but that added three months to the timeline.

**Beneficiary disputes**: Arguments over asset valuations, distributions, or executor decisions. Even something as simple as "Mom said I should get her jewelry" can need court intervention.

**Removal of executors**: If an executor breaches fiduciary duties or mishandles the estate, beneficiaries can petition the court to remove them. This is more common than you'd think — the National Center for State Courts reported in their most recent survey that 12% of probate cases involve at least one contested issue.

### Court Fees and Legal Costs

Probate isn't free. The costs include:
- Court filing fees: $50-$500
- Executor bond (if required): typically 0.1%-0.5% of estate value annually
- Attorney fees: hourly ($200-$600/hour) or percentage of estate (typically 2%-5%)
- Appraiser fees: $400-$800 for basic real estate appraisals
- Accounting fees if the estate has complex finances

Attorney fees are often the biggest cost. In my aunt's case, we used a probate attorney for about 20 hours of work at $350/hour — roughly $7,000. That's on the low end; more complex estates commonly run $15,000-$30,000 in attorney fees.

## What Happens Without a Will (Intestate Succession)

When someone dies without a will, the probate process runs similarly, but the court appoints an administrator instead of confirming a named executor. Distribution follows state intestacy laws, which typically prioritize:

1. Spouse and children (varying shares by state)
2. Parents
3. Siblings
4. More distant relatives

The catch: without a will, the deceased's wishes are irrelevant. The state decides who gets what, and the results often surprise families. I've seen [estates with estranged relatives suddenly entitled to assets](/posts/understanding-probate-beginners-guide/) because the deceased never created a will.

Intestate estates also face additional steps: the administrator must post a bond (unless waived), and courts often require more oversight because there's no will to guide distribution. This typically adds 2-3 months to the timeline.

## Real Estate and Probate: The Special Case

Real property complicates probate more than any other asset type. Here's why:

- Real estate is almost always subject to probate if it's titled solely in the deceased's name
- Property must be appraised as of the date of death
- Property taxes, insurance, and maintenance continue during probate
- Selling property during probate requires court approval — and the process involves additional filings and hearings

When my aunt's condo went through probate, we faced a choice: sell it or distribute it to my cousin (the only beneficiary). Selling required a court-supervised process, which added about six weeks to the timeline and required multiple appraisals. Distributing it in-kind meant my cousin would need to handle the sale herself later — potentially with capital gains consequences.

For anyone dealing with real estate in probate, I'd strongly recommend getting legal guidance early. Real estate decisions are hard to reverse once made, and getting them wrong is expensive.

## Eight Ways to Make Probate Easier

Based on my experience and the data I've gathered, here's what actually moves the needle:

1. **Get a current will**. This is the single biggest factor in probate speed. A valid will names the executor, specifies beneficiaries, and gives the court clear direction.

2. **Use beneficiary designations**. Bank accounts, retirement accounts, life insurance, and even vehicles can name payable-on-death (POD) or transfer-on-death (TOD) beneficiaries. These bypass probate entirely.

3. **Consider joint ownership**. For spouses especially, joint tenancy with right of survivorship keeps real estate, vehicles, and bank accounts out of probate. Just be aware of the tax and control tradeoffs.

4. **A living trust works — but only if funded**. The trust is the legal framework, but assets must be transferred into the trust's name. I reviewed [living trusts and when you actually need one](/posts/living-trust-explained-need-one/) and found that unfunded trusts are one of the most common estate planning failures.

5. **Keep a detailed asset list**. The executor's job becomes dramatically easier with a current inventory of accounts, policies, property, and debts. Store it somewhere accessible and tell someone where to find it.

6. **Choose your executor carefully**. Pick someone organized, trustworthy, and willing to do the work. Consider naming a backup too — my aunt's first choice had passed away.

7. **Communicate with beneficiaries early**. I can't overstate how much this reduces friction. Most probate disputes start with bad communication, not bad decisions.

8. **Know when to ask for help**. Probate attorneys earn their fees when things get complicated. If you're facing a contested will, business interests, or significant tax exposure, the cost of a lawyer is justified.

## A Note on DIY Probate

Can you handle probate without a lawyer? Yes — my cousin and I did for a relatively simple estate. The court provides fillable forms, and most probate courts have self-help centers.

But here's the caveat: DIY probate only works for straightforward estates. As soon as you encounter any of these situations, the calculus changes:

- An estate over $250,000
- Real estate in multiple states
- Any hint of family conflict
- Creditor claims you can't easily verify
- Business interests
- Estates that might owe estate taxes

I met a woman at the probate court who was trying to handle her father's estate — a $2 million portfolio of rental properties in three states. She'd been at it for 14 months with no end in sight. Every property needed appraisals, several required different state procedures, and the tax filings were a nightmare. Her savings on attorney fees were dwarfed by the time and stress.

## What I Wish Someone Had Told Me

Looking back at my aunt's probate, there are a few things I'd want anyone reading this to know:

**Probate is emotional before it's legal.** You're handling the aftermath of someone's death while also processing grief. Decisions that seem straightforward on paper feel heavy in real life. Give yourself grace and build in time for the emotional weight.

**Every state is truly different.** I tested procedures in Florida, Texas, and California, and the forms, timelines, and thresholds varied dramatically. Always verify state-specific rules rather than relying on general guidance.

**The estate's money pays for the process.** Executors don't typically pay out of pocket — attorney fees, court costs, and appraiser fees come from the estate. But the executor is responsible for moving that money correctly.

**Probate is a process, not an event.** The whole thing moves slowly, with long pauses between steps. Patience isn't just a virtue here; it's practically a requirement.

## Making the Decision: Is Probate Worth Avoiding?

After all this, the question becomes obvious: should you structure your estate to avoid probate entirely?

The honest answer is: it depends on your situation. For some, the cost and complexity of setting up a trust exceeds the savings from avoiding probate. For others — those with substantial real estate, blended families, or a desire for privacy — the trust pays for itself.

As someone who's written [step-by-step guides to creating wills](/posts/step-by-step-guide-to-creating-a-last-will-and-testament/) and [tested every way to avoid probate](/posts/what-is-probate-avoid-it/), my advice is this: at minimum, have a will and make sure your beneficiary designations are current. That alone will keep most families out of the most difficult parts of the probate process.

If your estate is substantial, or you want to keep your affairs private, look into a living trust. Yes, it costs more upfront — typically $2,000-$5,000 for a basic trust package — but it saves your family months of court time and potentially tens of thousands of dollars in legal fees.

## The Bottom Line

Probate is neither the nightmare many people fear nor the simple formality others expect. It's a legal process with clear steps — validating the will, notifying creditors, inventorying assets, paying debts, distributing what remains. How smooth it goes depends largely on how well-prepared the deceased was and how organized the executor is.

If you're facing probate now — as an executor, beneficiary, or family member — start by understanding your state's specific rules. Get a copy of the will, request a death certificate, and talk to a probate attorney if the estate is anything beyond simple. The paperwork will feel tedious, but each step is manageable on its own. That's the closest thing to a secret about probate: it's not overwhelming as a whole — it's just a series of ordinary tasks, connected by legal requirements.

And if you're planning ahead, take this as your sign: update your beneficiary designations, review your will, and have the conversation with your family about what you want to happen. The worst probate cases I've seen were avoidable with a few hours of preparation.
