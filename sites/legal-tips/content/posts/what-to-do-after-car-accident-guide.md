I've been rear-ended three times in eleven years. The first time I did almost everything wrong — I apologized, I let the other driver leave without a photo of his insurance card, and I signed a medical release form a claims adjuster waved in front of me three days later. The second and third times, I ran a script. This guide is that script, expanded.

This isn't a list of platitudes. It's the sequence I'd follow (and have followed) at the side of a road, plus the stuff that happens in the 72 hours and 10 days after — which is where most people quietly lose their claims. If your collision is genuinely minor with no injuries and you're already halfway through the process, my [field-tested checklist for minor accidents](/posts/after-minor-car-accident-steps/) covers the shorter version. For everyone else, read on.

## The 60-second triage: what actually matters first

Before anyone gets out of a car, four questions decide how the next hour goes:

1. **Is anyone hurt or in danger of being hit again?** If yes, this is a hazard scene, not a documentation exercise.
2. **Am I obstructing a live lane?** On a highway shoulder, you are three feet from a 70 mph collision.
3. **Is there fire, smoke, or fluid pouring out?** Get out and get away.
4. **Can the vehicles be moved legally?** Depends on your state — more on that below.

I noticed that most "what to do after a car accident" articles bury the actual physical danger. Statistically, that's backwards. According to the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS), pedestrian and roadside worker deaths on shoulders and breakdown lanes rose sharply through 2020–2023, with 2023 crash fatality data showing more than 40,000 total US traffic deaths for the fourth consecutive year. The most dangerous minutes of your day are the ones where you stand between a car and a travel lane.

So: hazard light on. Triangle or flare if you have one. Move to the far side of the guardrail if you can do so safely.

### When the vehicles must be moved

State law varies, but the general rule in the ~20 states with "steer it, clear it" or "move over" provisions is: if there are no injuries and the vehicles are drivable, you are **required** to move them out of travel lanes before exchanging information. In Florida (F.S. 316.061) and similar statutes, you can be cited for leaving a drivable vehicle blocking traffic.

In states without that rule — and in any case involving injury, fatality, or a suspected impaired driver — you leave the vehicles where they are until police arrive. Getting this wrong can invalidate your claim or create a legal headache.

## Step 1: Secure the scene and call 911 (or don't)

Call 911 if any of the following is true:

- Anyone reports pain, dizziness, numbness, or is bleeding — even if they say "I'm fine."
- A vehicle is on fire, leaking fuel, or is undrivable in a travel lane.
- The other driver appears impaired, is aggressive, or flees.
- There is a pedestrian, cyclist, or motorcycle involved.
- Public property (sign, guardrail, utility pole) is damaged.

Otherwise, in many jurisdictions you can call the non-emergency police line, or — in a handful of states and cities — file a self-report at a police station within a set window (often 24–72 hours). For reference, California requires an SR-1 report with the DMV within 10 days if anyone was injured or damage exceeds $1,000 (as of 2026; that threshold has been $1,000 since 2017).

> **My honest caveat:** police response times in dense cities can be over an hour for a no-injury fender bender. I once waited 95 minutes in Los Angeles for a report on a $2,400 incident. If you're going to wait, move to safety and do your documentation while you wait — don't stand at the scene doing nothing.

## Step 2: Document the scene like you're building evidence for a courtroom

You probably are. Even if your claim settles amicably, the photos you take in the next fifteen minutes will be the ones that decide who pays — and how much. Insurance adjusters I've spoken with consistently say the same thing: the party with better photos wins the negotiation, full stop.

### The photo sequence that actually works

Do this in order. Use your phone's wide lens for context, then the telephoto for detail.

| # | Shot | Why it matters |
|---|------|----------------|
| 1 | Wide shot of both vehicles in the roadway, including lane markings | Establishes position, right-of-way, and angle of impact |
| 2 | Four corners of each vehicle at eye level | Shows which panels are damaged and which are not |
| 3 | Close-ups of every dent, scratch, and crack | Prevents the other party claiming pre-existing damage |
| 4 | License plates on both vehicles | Obvious, but I've seen people skip it |
| 5 | The other driver's face (politely) | Discourages identity disputes later |
| 6 | Weather, road surface, and any traffic signals in the frame | Relevant to fault allocation |
| 7 | Damage to third-party property (fence, sign, hydrant) | Often missed, occasionally your liability |
| 8 | Odometer reading | Some insurers ask for mileage |

After photos, video a slow 360° walk-around. It captures things you didn't think to photograph — skid marks, debris position, brake lights.

### Exchange information (and only information)

Get, from every driver involved:

- Full legal name, address, phone
- Driver's license number and state
- Insurance carrier, policy number, and **the phone number on the card** (a surprising number of people hand over expired or wrong policies)
- License plate, make, model, year, color

Get from witnesses: name, phone, and a one-line summary of what they saw. Witnesses disappear. The two minutes you spend getting their number is worth more than any hour you'll spend calling them tomorrow. I've watched a witness ghost me twice. Get the number, get the story, note the time they saw it.

**Do not** discuss fault. Not "I'm so sorry," not "I didn't see you," not "it was my fault." You can be polite and human without admitting liability. "I'm glad you're okay — let's exchange information" is enough. Those words have cost real people real money.

### Note the officer and the report number

If police respond, get the responding officer's name, badge number, and the incident/case number. Reports can take 5–10 business days to become available; in some states you can request them online for a small fee. Keep the receipt — it's a documented loss.

## Step 3: The on-scene medical decision

Adrenaline masks pain for up to 24 hours. This is the single most consequential fact of a car accident, and almost nobody plans for it.

My rule after my second accident (a 25 mph rear-end at a red light, September 2022): **if I feel any pain, or if an hour has passed and I still feel "off," I go to urgent care the same day.** Not the ER unless there's a head strike, loss of consciousness, or severe pain — that's a $3,000+ visit and it delays triage.

Why same-day matters: insurance carriers treat a 72-hour gap between the accident and your first medical visit as evidence the injury is unrelated. It's a documented tactic. Personal injury attorneys I've interviewed call the medical gap the single most common defense against soft-tissue claims.

### Do not sign anything at the scene from the other driver's insurer

If a claims adjuster for the other driver shows up at the scene or calls you within hours with a "quick settlement," the correct answer is: "I'm not discussing settlement until I've been evaluated by a doctor." Then hang up.

That quick settlement is usually calculated off a formula the adjuster runs before knowing your diagnosis. An offer made at hour three is an offer made with zero information about your injuries. It is almost always a lowball.

## Step 4: The 72-hour window — reports, insurers, and the paper trail

Here's what I do in the first three days after the scene.

**Day 0 (same day):**
- Photos backed up to cloud storage immediately
- Call your own insurer to open a first-party claim (not to file against the other driver — that's separate)
- If you're injured, urgent care
- Note everything in a running document with timestamps

**Day 1:**
- Request the police report (online if possible)
- Get a repair estimate from a shop *you* choose, not one recommended by the other driver's insurer
- If your car is undrivable, arrange a rental under your policy's rental reimbursement (if you have it)

**Day 2–3:**
- Notify your employer if you've missed work (documented lost wages matter)
- Save every receipt: tow, rental, rideshare, parking, medical co-pays

That last item gets undercounted by nearly everyone. A $14 parking fee at the body shop is still a loss. Track it.

### Talking to the other driver's adjuster

The other party's adjuster will call you within 24–72 hours. This is a recorded statement in the making. Here's the script I use:

> "I'm happy to provide my contact information and clarify the vehicle details. I'm not prepared to give a recorded statement about fault or injuries at this time. Please send any requests in writing to [email]."

Adjusters are trained negotiators. They will ask leading questions ("So you had a green light the whole time? Are you *sure* you didn't glance at your phone?"). You do not owe them a recorded statement. In most states, you are **not** legally required to give one to the other driver's insurance company. You *are* required to cooperate with your own insurer under your policy's cooperation clause — but even that has limits, and the scope is what your contract says.

### The other driver's policy has a limit — get it in writing

Ask the other driver's adjuster, in writing, "What are the policy limits on the at-fault driver's policy?" They may decline to answer until a claim is filed, but the answer determines whether you should be looking at your own underinsured motorist (UIM) coverage. If their limits are $25,000 (state minimums in many states) and your medical bills exceed that, your UIM is where the rest comes from.

If you have to escalate to a lawsuit, the anatomy of that process is very different from an insurance claim. I wrote up the [12-step response plan I used when served a lawsuit](/posts/served-lawsuit-response-steps/) if things get that far — but most claims don't.

## Step 5: The 10-day clock — the deadlines that catch people out

Deadlines are the part of the process that quietly closes doors.

| Deadline type | Typical window | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Police report request | 1–10 days | Varies by jurisdiction; longer for full narrative reports |
| State DMV accident report (e.g., CA SR-1) | 10 days | Required if injury or damage over state threshold |
| Notify your own insurer | "Promptly" — usually within days | Contractual; can affect coverage |
| PIP / no-fault claim (in no-fault states) | 14–30 days | **State-specific; some are as short as 14 days** |
| Personal injury statute of limitations | 1–6 years | Varies by state; typically 2–3 years |
| Property damage statute of limitations | 1–6 years | Often shorter than injury |

That last one gets missed constantly. I spent time mapping out how long plaintiffs actually have to file and why the clock is different in every state — [the personal injury statute of limitations breakdown](/posts/personal-injury-statute-of-limitations/) lays out the ranges, but assume you need to act fast.

A specific caveat: in no-fault states like Florida, New York, and Michigan, you must file a PIP claim within a much shorter window than the injury statute of limitations. Florida's is 14 days from the accident (F.S. 627.736(5)(a)). Miss it, and you may have zero coverage for your own medical bills — even if you weren't at fault. This one has ended more claims than any other deadline.

You can be thorough.

## Common mistakes I've watched people make (including myself)

Let me be direct about what I got wrong, because the failures stick.

**Apologizing.** In my first accident, I said "I'm so sorry, I was looking at my GPS." That sentence became a footnote in the other party's claim record. It didn't determine fault, but it made my adjuster's job harder and probably cost me a couple of hundred dollars in negotiation.

**Assuming the other driver's insurance will "handle it."** They will — in their client's interest. Their goal is to minimize payout. Yours should be to create a fully documented record.

**Not tracking mileage and time off work.** I once lost ~$340 in wage documentation because I didn't get a letter from my employer confirming my hourly rate and missed shifts. Now it's a chain email the same week.

**Skipping the damage to my own body because I didn't "feel" it.** Whiplash and mild concussions can take hours or days to register. If a medical professional tells you to follow up in 48 hours, do it.

**Talking to a "claims specialist" without checking their identity.** Several carriers contract out initial calls to third-party agencies. Get the adjuster's name, employee number, and the carrier, in writing, before sharing any details.

If you're handling a collision without a lawyer — which is realistic for most small-to-medium claims — my [detailed playbook for handling a minor accident without a lawyer](/posts/handle-minor-car-accident-without-lawyer/) walks through the negotiation and the settlement release. That's where I'd caution you most: the settlement release is a contract, and reading it the way you'd read a lease is essential. The [lease-reading method I developed from reading 47 of them](/posts/how-to-read-lease-agreement-before-signing/) applies surprisingly well — check every clause for a waiver, an indemnification, or a release of future claims.

## When to actually hire a lawyer

I'm not anti-lawyer, but I am anti-hiring-one-because-you're-scared. Here's my honest threshold:

- **No lawyer:** soft-tissue injuries, damage under ~$10,000, clear fault, cooperative insurer.
- **Consider a lawyer:** disputed fault, injuries requiring ongoing treatment, policy limits that won't cover your bills, or a claim involving a commercial vehicle or rideshare (the latter almost always involves multiple insurers).
- **Definitely call a lawyer:** any permanent injury, brain injury, spinal cord injury, wrongful death, or if you're being pressured to sign a release before being cleared by a doctor.

A contingency fee is typically 33–40% of the settlement, plus costs. Run the math on the alternative. On a $15,000 claim with clear fault and a cooperative insurer, a lawyer will usually eat more than they add. On a $250,000 claim with a disputed fault and a $100,000 policy limit (where you're going after UIM), a lawyer is probably the only way to get near the top of that policy.

## The mental side (which nobody writes about)

Two days after my second accident, I couldn't get in a car without my hands shaking. This is normal. Adrenaline dumps do that. What's less normal is being embarrassed about it and not mentioning it to your doctor — which is exactly what I did, and which is exactly why my medical record showed nothing about anxiety when my adjuster asked about "emotional distress."

The record of your injury is the record your claim will be built on. If you tell an urgent care doc you're anxious, it's in the chart. If you don't, it's not. In most states (all but a handful), a documented emotional distress or anxiety component is compensable in a personal injury claim, but only if it's diagnosed by a professional, not by a claims adjuster.

If a claim does escalate — say, you're served because the other side disputes fault — the process changes shape completely. My [guide to responding to a lawsuit as a first-time defendant](/posts/what-to-do-if-youre-being-sued-a-first-time-defendants-guide/) is the one I wish I'd had before my first summons.

## The short version

If you're reading this on the shoulder with your hazard lights on, here's the sequence:

1. **Get out of the line of fire.** Move the cars if it's legal and safe; otherwise move yourself.
2. **Call 911 if there's injury, impairment, or danger** — otherwise non-emergency or self-report.
3. **Photograph everything before anyone drives away.** Wide, corners, close-ups, plates.
4. **Exchange information — and only information.** No fault, no apologies, no "I didn't see you."
5. **Note witnesses' names and phone numbers.** Do it now.
6. **Get medically evaluated the same day if anything hurts.**
7. **Call your own insurer within 24 hours** and open a claim.
8. **Request the police report and track your state's deadlines** — DMV report, PIP window, statute of limitations.

Everything after that is documentation and patience. The scene is the fifteen minutes you cannot redo.

I've run this sequence three times now. The last two claims closed without a lawsuit and with 90%+ of my documented costs recovered. The difference between accident one and accidents two and three wasn't luck. It was the checklist.
