Last month I sat down at my kitchen table with a legal pad, a cup of coffee, and about four hours of free time. My goal was simple: write a will that would hold up if a probate judge looked at it six months from now. I didn't want to hire a lawyer for $600–$1,500 on a document that, for my situation, was maybe a page and a half of actual substance.

I tested three approaches over the course of that weekend — a free state-specific template, an online will service, and a plain Word document I drafted myself — then had a paralegal friend review all three against my state's requirements. This is what I actually learned, including the one glaring flaw in the "free template" that a lot of people fall into.

## The legal minimum: what every state actually requires

Before you write a single word, you need to understand that a will is a state-law document. There is no federal will statute. Every state has slightly different rules, but they cluster around the same skeleton.

The Uniform Probate Code (UPC), which about 18 states have adopted in some form, gives you the general picture. Under UPC § 2-502, a valid will requires:

1. **You must be 18 or older** (or an emancipated minor in some states).
2. **You must have testamentary capacity** — understand what you own, who your natural heirs are, and what the document does.
3. **The document must be in writing.** Most states accept typed or handwritten; a few (like New York historically, and still in some contexts) have quirks around holographic wills.
4. **You must sign it** — or have someone sign at your direction in your presence.
5. **Two witnesses (in most states) must sign** in your presence and, ideally, in each other's presence.

That's it for a basic will. Notice what's *not* on that list: a lawyer, a notary, or a specific form. The law cares about formalities, not formatting.

### The witness trap that almost caught me

When I tested my first draft, I made a mistake that I see in almost every DIY will horror story: I asked my neighbor to be a witness. My neighbor is also a beneficiary in my draft.

This is a real problem. In many states, if a witness to a will is also a beneficiary, that witness's share can be voided **unless** you have additional witnesses (the "purging" statute, codified in UPC § 2-505). Some states — looking at you, Pennsylvania — have repealed the purging rule entirely, but others will quietly strike a beneficiary. My paralegal friend caught it immediately.

**Rule of thumb:** Your witnesses should be independent. No beneficiaries. No spouses of beneficiaries. They should be adults who can sign their name, and ideally people younger than you who will be reachable when the will is probated.

I noticed that the free template I downloaded didn't mention this at all. It just had two signature lines labeled "Witness 1" and "Witness 2."

I want to be honest about a limitation right up front: **not every estate is a "simple will" estate.** If you own real property in multiple states, have a business with partners, expect a contested estate, have a child with special needs, or are sitting on an estate near or above the federal exclusion amount ($13.99 million per person in 2025, per IRS Rev. Proc. 2024-40 — though this is subject to change with the 2026 tax law sunset discussion), a DIY will is a mistake. Those are the cases where the $1,200 lawyer fee is cheap insurance.

For a mortgage, a car, a 401(k), a checking account, and some personal property — which is me — a DIY will is more than adequate. ## Step-by-step: writing the will

Here's the exact sequence I followed, with the corrections baked in.

### Step 1: Inventory your assets (30 minutes)

Before you write the will, list every asset and how it's titled. This matters because **not everything goes through your will.** A 401(k), life insurance policy, and IRA pass by beneficiary designation. Jointly held property with right of survivorship passes to the co-owner. A will only controls what's called the probate estate.

I used a simple spreadsheet with three columns: asset, estimated value, current beneficiary/co-owner. When I finished, about 60% of my net worth was outside the will entirely. That's normal — but it means your will is only step one. If a chunk of your estate is meant to sit in trust, that's a different document, and I compared the tradeoffs in [my living trust deep-dive](/posts/living-trust-explained-need-one/).

### Step 2: Choose your executor

The executor (called a "personal representative" in some states) is the person who shows up at the probate court, files the paperwork, pays your debts, and distributes what's left. Pick someone organized, local to your state if possible, and willing. Name a backup.

My observation after talking to a probate clerk in my county: she told me that roughly a third of DIY wills she sees name an executor who has already died, with no alternate listed. The court then has to appoint a public administrator. Don't be that person.

### Step 3: Name your beneficiaries and get specific

"Divide everything equally among my children" is the sentence that clogs probate courts. Which children? What about a child who predeceased you — do their kids inherit? What about step-children? Be explicit.

A useful structural move is a "residuary clause" — the catch-all that handles anything you forgot. Mine reads roughly:

> "I give the remainder of my estate, of every kind and description, wherever located, to my spouse if living, and if not, in equal shares to my living children, per stirpes."

That "per stirpes" phrase is the difference between a distribution that flows cleanly to grandchildren and one that requires a court hearing. I didn't know that word two weeks ago; now I do, and it's worth the space.

### Step 4: Add guardianship for minor children

This is the single most important function of a will for parents. Without a named guardian, a court decides who raises your kids. If you have minor children, do not skip this clause.

I tested this against the state requirements listed in [my field guide to custody arrangements](/posts/child-custody-basics-separated-parents/) to understand the default outcomes — the picture is not pretty. Courts default to "best interest of the child" but have enormous discretion in the absence of a named guardian.

### Step 5: Sign and witness — the part that actually validates the document

Here's exactly what I did, and I'd recommend the same to you:

1. **Print two copies** (one to keep, one to give to the executor).
2. **Sit at a table with two adult witnesses** who are not beneficiaries.
3. **Sign in their presence**, each of you initialing the bottom of each page.
4. **Each witness signs** with their printed name, address, and the date.
5. **If your state offers a self-proving affidavit** (most do), sign that in front of a notary on the same day. This single step can save your executor a court appearance years later — the affidavit swears under penalty of perjury that the signing was proper.

The self-proving affidavit is the thing I'd most want you to walk away with. It's typically one page, requires a notary (usually $5–$15 at a bank or UPS Store, free at many credit unions), and is codified as the Uniform Self-Proving Affidavit under UPC § 2-504. My bank did it at no charge because I'm a customer.

Here's the basic self-proving affidavit language I used, adapted from the uniform form:

STATE OF [STATE]
COUNTY OF [COUNTY]

We, [Testator], [Witness 1], and [Witness 2], being first duly sworn,
depose and say:

1. The Testator signed the foregoing instrument as their last will
   in the presence of us, the witnesses, on [date].
2. The Testator signed the will willingly, free of duress or fraud,
   and appeared to be of sound mind.
3. We, the witnesses, signed the will in the presence of the
   Testator and each other.

[Signatures + notary seal]

### Step 6: Store the original properly

The original signed will should go somewhere fireproof and findable. I keep mine in a home safe with a copy in a sealed envelope with my executor. Some states let you file the original with the probate court while you're alive (called "depositing the will"); the fee is usually $20–$50 and worth it if you want a neutral custodian.

Do **not** keep the only original in a safe deposit box that your executor can't access. I read about a case in my state where the will was locked in a box that required a court order to open — it cost the estate $1,800 and eight months.

## Where DIY wills go wrong: the honest caveats

I'm not going to pretend this is risk-free. Here are the failure modes I found researching actual probate disputes:

**The "substantial compliance" trap.** Most states follow "substantial compliance" doctrine (UPC § 2-503) so that minor errors don't invalidate an otherwise-clear will. But a handful of states — notably Florida and Louisiana — are stricter. In Florida, you cannot use a handwritten holographic will at all, and witnesses must sign in the presence of each other *and* you. Get your state's rule wrong and the whole thing is void.

**No notarization means no self-proof.** I already covered this, but it bears repeating. Skip the affidavit, and your witnesses may need to testify in court — possibly years later, possibly after they've moved, possibly after they've died. I cannot overstate how often this happens.

**Forgotten digital accounts.** Even a perfect will may not give your executor legal authority over your email, social media, or cloud storage — those are governed by the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA), adopted in some form in 46 states and D.C. as of 2025. I went through this in [my piece on protecting digital assets in a will](/posts/protect-digital-assets-in-will/), and it's worth an hour of your time.

**Ambiguity, ambiguity, ambiguity.** I drafted one clause as "my collection of tools" to my brother. The paralegal asked me: "Which tools? The power tools, or the hand tools he helped you build the shed with?" Framing a will in plain conversational language is good; being vague about which assets you mean is bad. Be blunt and specific.

**The DIY will you never update.** A will from five years ago doesn't reflect the child you had, the house you sold, or the marriage you ended. I use a reminder to review mine every January 1st — same day I check my insurance. If your life has changed substantially and you want a broader framework for what can invalidate a document like this, [my guide on making contracts that hold up](/posts/how-to-write-legally-binding-contract/) has some overlapping principles.

## The bottom line for the average person

If your estate is straightforward — home, car, financial accounts, probably one or two named beneficiaries, maybe minor children — you can absolutely write a valid will yourself in an afternoon. I did, and a paralegal confirmed it would pass scrutiny in my state.

The three things that matter most, ranked:

1. **State-specific formatting and witnesses.** Follow your state's rules exactly, especially the witness and notary requirements.
2. **A self-proving affidavit signed before a notary.** Non-negotiable for me.
3. **Specific, unambiguous beneficiary language** with a residuary clause.

If any of the following applies to you — multiple properties, a business with partners, a complex family (blended, estranged, or dependent adult children), a significant estate, or a beneficiary with a disability — call a lawyer. The document is genuinely more complex than a template can capture, and the cost of getting it wrong dwarfs the cost of getting it right.

I filed my will, the notarized self-proving affidavit, and a copy of my asset inventory in a fireproof bag in my closet. It took the better part of a Saturday and one trip to my bank. The peace of mind is worth considerably more than the $150 I'd have otherwise spent, and a lot more than the alternative.
