I've signed nine NDAs in the past three years. I've drafted four of my own. And I've watched exactly one of them do anything useful in a real dispute. That ratio is the whole reason I'm writing this.

The internet will tell you that an NDA is the first document every founder, freelancer, and employee should whip out before any meaningful conversation. My experience says that's backwards. An NDA is a narrow tool for a narrow situation, and using it in the wrong context doesn't just waste paper — it can actively hurt you by signaling distrust before a relationship has earned any.

This is the framework I landed on after testing NDA language against real scenarios in 2024 and 2025. It's not legal advice — I'm a writer, not an attorney — but it's the practical filter I wish someone had handed me years ago.

## What an NDA actually is (and what it isn't)

An NDA, or non-disclosure agreement, is a contract where one party promises not to share certain information with outside parties, and usually promises not to use that information for their own benefit. That's it. That's the core.

Two flavors dominate the landscape:

- **Unilateral (one-way) NDA**: one party discloses, the other receives. Typical for a freelancer getting briefed by a client, or an employee onboarding at a company.
- **Mutual (bilateral) NDA**: both parties disclose and both are bound. Standard for partnership discussions, M&A talks, and co-founder conversations where both sides are handing over sensitive material.

There's also a third category people forget: the **multilateral NDA**, used when three or more parties are in the same room and everyone needs to be bound simultaneously. I've only seen it in joint venture discussions and once in a real estate deal with two competing buyers present.

What an NDA is *not*:

1. A non-compete. It doesn't stop you from working for a competitor. It stops you from talking about specific information.
2. A non-solicit. It doesn't prevent you from hiring someone's employee.
3. A magical shield. If someone steals your idea and the NDA covers "ideas," good luck proving it in court unless the idea was concrete and documented.
4. A substitute for a real contract. If you're doing paid work, you need a services agreement with payment terms — the NDA is a supporting document, not the main event.

If you want to see how this plays into the broader contract picture, I broke down the core mechanics in my guide to [reading a contract before signing](/posts/read-contract-before-signing/) — the same clause-detection instincts apply here.

## Do I need an NDA? The four-question test

I use this filter on every situation where someone suggests an NDA. If you can't pass all four, the NDA is probably theater.

### 1. Is the information actually confidential?

Ask: **if this leaked tomorrow, would it cause measurable harm?**

Things that pass: source code, financial projections, customer lists with contact details, a specific manufacturing process, unannounced product specs, pricing models, patient or client data.

Things that fail: your "business idea" (ideas aren't protectable — execution is), general market analysis, anything you could find via Google in ten minutes, anything already public, anything you've discussed in a public setting.

I noticed that roughly 70% of the NDAs I've been handed cover things like "the fact that we're talking" and "general business information." That's meaningless language. A court can't enforce a promise not to discuss something you can't define.

### 2. Can I identify the specific harm from disclosure?

This is the enforceability test. Courts won't enforce a vague NDA. They *will* enforce an NDA that says "you will not disclose the formula disclosed in Appendix A, and here's why it's commercially valuable."

This is why I now push back on any NDA handed to me that doesn't have a schedule or appendix naming the confidential material. Generic NDAs are almost unenforceable and definitely unhelpful.

### 3. Is there a relationship worth protecting?

Here's where most people go wrong. You don't need an NDA to talk to a potential client about your rates. You don't need one before a first interview. You don't need one for a casual coffee with a founder.

You need one when:

- You're about to share material that would damage you if it leaked
- You're doing due diligence on a business acquisition
- You're onboarding an employee or contractor who will access secrets
- You're in a joint venture where both sides bring proprietary assets
- You're pitching a specific technical solution that isn't patentable yet

Contrast that with how people handle employment situations. When I wrote about [employment contracts and the clauses that reshape careers](/posts/understanding-employment-contracts-key-clauses/), the pattern was clear: the NDA in a typical employment agreement is boilerplate. The real teeth are in the IP assignment and non-solicit clauses that ride alongside it.

### 4. What's the cost of asking?

Asking someone to sign an NDA has a social cost. In venture pitch settings, asking a VC to sign an NDA is essentially a way of telling them you don't understand how venture works. Established funds don't sign them for cold pitches. They'll sign them for actual due diligence, but by then you're past the pitch stage.

Freelancers have more room. I've asked clients to sign short mutual NDAs before seeing proprietary product roadmaps, and nobody blinked. The difference is context.

## What a workable NDA actually contains

Here's the structure I've landed on after reviewing roughly 20 NDAs in the wild. This isn't legal advice — a real attorney should review anything that matters — but it's the shape I look for.

| Clause | What It Should Do | Red Flag Version |
|---|---|---|
| Definition of confidential info | Narrows to specific categories, excludes public info | "All information of any kind" |
| Exclusions | Public info, prior knowledge, independently developed, legally compelled | Missing entirely |
| Obligations of receiving party | Reasonable care, same standard as own secrets, limited internal access | Absolute no-disclosure with no exceptions |
| Term | 2–5 years for most business info, indefinite for trade secrets | Perpetual for everything |
| Return/destruction | Delete or return on request, with certification | Silent or vague |
| Remedies | Injunctive relief plus damages, reasonable attorneys' fees | Massive liquidated damages |
| Governing law & venue | Your state or a neutral one | Delaware for a small freelancer deal |
| Non-solicit (if present) | Narrow, time-limited to the actual engagement | 5-year employee no-hire provisions |

A defensive check I run every time: how long is the term, and does the definition of confidential information survive the term? If I sign a 2-year NDA but "trade secrets" are defined so broadly that everything I learned is covered forever, I've signed a lifetime commitment with a 2-year label.

When I tested this against NDAs from three different tech companies in 2025, two of them had perpetual trade secret clauses that effectively extended beyond the stated term. That's legal in most states. It's also the clause nobody reads.

## Mutual vs unilateral: a decision table

| Scenario | Recommended Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Freelancer preview of client material | Mutual | You may share your own process |
| Employee onboarding | Unilateral (employer receives) | You're receiving company info, not disclosing yours |
| Co-founder discussions | Mutual | Both sides disclose plans and IP |
| M&A due diligence | Mutual | Both companies exchange financials |
| Investor pitch (cold) | Neither | VCs won't sign; use a pitch deck with a copyright notice |
| Investor pitch (term sheet stage) | Mutual | Real diligence requires real protection |
| Vendor evaluation | Unilateral (you receive) | You're seeing their proprietary tools |
| Beta testing your product | Unilateral (tester receives) | Tester agrees not to leak features |

I've noticed that mutual NDAs are negotiated faster in practice, because both parties have symmetric exposure. Unilateral NDAs trigger more back-and-forth because the receiving party wants to know the boundaries of what they're promising.

## The freelancer and small business situation

Most people searching "do I need an NDA" are freelancers or small business owners. Here's the honest take.

**You probably need one of these three documents instead, in this order:**

1. A **services agreement** with clear IP assignment, payment terms, and deliverable definitions. This is 80% of your protection.
2. A **mutual NDA** for the rare case where you're sharing non-public strategic information before a contract exists.
3. An **IP assignment clause** embedded in your services agreement, which does far more work than an NDA ever will.

If you're just starting out and trying to figure out what paperwork actually matters, the process I documented in [legally starting a small business](/posts/simple-guide-starting-small-business-legally/) is a better first stop than an NDA template.

A specific observation: I've seen freelancers lose more money to clients who didn't pay than to clients who stole ideas. The breach that hurts is the invoice that never gets paid. An NDA doesn't help you there. A good services agreement with a deposit and late-fee clause does.

## Writing NDA language that holds up

I've drafted a handful of these now. Here's a template clause structure that a lawyer told me was "reasonably tight" (which, from a lawyer, is a compliment).

CONFIDENTIAL INFORMATION means any non-public information
disclosed by Discloser to Recipient that is (a) marked
"Confidential" at the time of disclosure, or (b) if disclosed
orally, identified as confidential within 10 business days
and reduced to writing, AND that relates to:
  (1) source code or technical architecture,
  (2) customer or supplier lists,
  (3) pricing, margins, or financial projections,
  (4) unreleased product specifications.

CONFIDENTIAL INFORMATION does not include information that:
  (a) is or becomes publicly available through no fault of Recipient,
  (b) was known to Recipient prior to disclosure without duty of confidence,
  (c) is independently developed by Recipient without use of Confidential Information,
  (d) is rightfully received from a third party without restriction,
  (e) is required to be disclosed by law, provided Recipient gives prompt notice.

TERM. The obligations in this Agreement survive for three (3) years
from the date of disclosure, except for trade secrets, which remain
protected for so long as they qualify as trade secrets under
applicable law.

The specificity is the point. Vague NDAs get tossed. Specific NDAs get enforced — I covered the same dynamic when I wrote about [protecting your intellectual property online](/posts/legal-ways-protect-your-intellectual-property-online/), where the pattern was identical: defined assets get protected, vague claims don't.

One caveat: if you're drafting for a jurisdiction outside the US, the "trade secret" concept varies. In the EU, the Trade Secrets Directive sets different thresholds. Don't assume US boilerplate travels.

## When an NDA hurts you

Here's the honest limitation nobody selling NDA templates will tell you: **NDAs can bind you more than they protect you.**

Four ways this happens:

1. **Broad definition of confidential information.** If you signed something in 2023 that covers "all information shared in any form," and you later start a competing business, you're exposed to a claim that you used info you can't even remember receiving.
2. **Overlapping non-solicit.** Many NDAs bundle a non-solicit clause. That clause can stop you from hiring your own former colleagues for years, even if you left the company on great terms.
3. **Perpetual trade secret carve-outs.** As I mentioned, these effectively extend the NDA forever.
4. **Assignment of inventions.** Some NDAs sneak in an IP assignment that gives the other party rights to things you build on your own time. This is far more common than people realize.

I saw this exact trap play out in the [legal implications of NDAs when things go wrong](/posts/legal-implications-non-disclosure-agreement/) — the person who got sued had signed a mutual NDA that quietly assigned everything created "during the term of the relationship." The term was 5 years. They'd built a whole side business inside that window.

The lesson: **read the whole document**. Sometimes the dangerous clause is three pages away from the confidentiality language you were focused on.

## NDA vs the alternatives

People reach for NDAs when other tools would serve better.

| Goal | NDA | Non-Compete | Non-Solicit | Services Agreement | Copyright / Trademark |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stop someone sharing your info | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ | Sometimes | ❌ |
| Stop someone competing with you | ❌ | ✅ | Partial | ❌ | ❌ |
| Stop someone hiring your team | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ | ❌ | ❌ |
| Secure payment for work | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ | ❌ |
| Protect a name or logo | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ |
| Protect source code as an asset | Partial | ❌ | ❌ | ✅ (IP assignment) | ✅ |

The trademark and copyright distinction matters here. I went deeper in my [copyright vs trademark vs patent comparison](/posts/copyright-trademark-patent-comparison/), but the short version: NDAs protect information, IP registrations protect *assets*. You want both for anything valuable.

## A practical checklist before you sign or send one

Every time I'm about to sign or send an NDA, I run this list:

- [ ] Is the confidential info defined by category, not by the word "everything"?
- [ ] Are the standard exclusions present (public info, prior knowledge, independent development, legal compulsion)?
- [ ] How long is the term, and does anything survive the term?
- [ ] Is this unilateral or mutual, and which one do I actually need?
- [ ] Does it contain a hidden non-solicit or IP assignment?
- [ ] What's the governing law, and is it somewhere I could realistically defend myself?
- [ ] Are there liquidated damages or penalty clauses?
- [ ] Is there a return-or-destroy obligation, and how onerous is compliance?
- [ ] If it's an employment-related NDA, does it conflict with my state's rules? (California, for example, is famously hostile to non-competes and increasingly skeptical of broad NDAs.)
- [ ] Who actually needs to sign — just me, or me and my company?

The last item trips people up. If you're signing on behalf of a company, sign as an officer of the company, not personally. Otherwise you've personally guaranteed an obligation that should have been corporate.

## Pricing and tooling reality check

If you're going to pay for help, here's the landscape I've tested as of late 2025:

- **LegalZoom NDA templates**: free to browse, roughly $39 for a downloadable basic version. Covers a single mutual template. Fine for freelancers, thin for anything involving real IP.
- **Rocket Lawyer**: included in their $39.99/month membership plan; templates for both mutual and unilateral NDAs, with a guided builder.
- **LawDepot**: free-to-generate with a paywall at download; typically $7.99–$29.99 depending on the plan.
- **Attorney-drafted custom NDA**: I got quotes in 2025 ranging from $250 (small firm, standard template tweak) to $1,200 (boutique firm, fully customized with IP assignment and non-solicit separation).
- **Free templates from universities and state bar associations**: surprisingly usable, but always check the date — several I found in 2025 were drafted pre-2010 and hadn't been updated for modern remote work or AI-assisted disclosure.

The honest take: if the deal value is under $10,000 and the risk is limited, a $39 template is probably fine. Above that, or if there's real IP in play, a $250–$500 attorney review is the cheapest insurance you'll buy.

## FAQ: the questions I actually get asked

**Do I need an NDA to talk to a developer about my app idea?**
Probably not before the first call. Do sign a mutual NDA before sharing architecture diagrams, source code, or a product roadmap with specific feature specs. The generic "I have an idea for an app" conversation doesn't need one — and asking for one will often cost you the meeting.

**Can I write my own NDA?**
Yes, for low-stakes situations. I walked through the whole process in [how to draft an NDA yourself](/posts/how-to-draft-non-disclosure-agreement-yourself/). But if you're protecting anything that would materially hurt you if leaked, get a lawyer to review. A $300 review is cheaper than a $30,000 dispute.

**Are NDAs enforceable if there's no signature?**
Sometimes. If both parties behaved as if the NDA existed and the receiving party accepted the information under its terms, a court might find an implied contract. But "sometimes" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Get it signed.

**Can an NDA be too broad to enforce?**
Yes. Courts regularly strike down NDAs that are effectively non-competes in disguise, or that define confidential info so broadly the recipient can't know what they've promised. Specificity is your friend.

**What happens if I breach an NDA?**
Civil liability, typically. Money damages, possible injunction, and attorneys' fees if the NDA provides for them. In rare cases involving trade secrets, there can be criminal exposure under the Economic Espionage Act of 1996, but that's reserved for extreme conduct.

**Do NDAs work internationally?**
They work better if the parties agree on governing law and venue up front. Cross-border enforcement is messy. If you're dealing with a party in another country, expect to spend more and get less certainty.

## The bottom line

Most NDAs get signed in situations where they'd never be enforced, protect information that isn't actually sensitive, and add friction to relationships that are still forming.

The NDA earns its keep in a narrow band: when you have defined, valuable, non-public information; when the other party is going to actually see it; and when there's a real relationship worth protecting. In that band, a well-drafted NDA is worth every page. Outside that band, it's clutter.

If you're building a habit of protecting yourself legally, the sequence I'd recommend is: get a solid services agreement or employment contract first, register your trademarks and copyrights second, use an NDA third. The order matters more than the individual document. When I watched a lawsuit unfold over a broken [small business partnership](/posts/how-to-create-a-simple-business-partnership-agreement/), the missing NDA wasn't the problem — the missing partnership agreement was. The NDA would have been a bandage on a wound that needed stitches.

Draft carefully. Read everything. And don't ask a VC to sign an NDA before the first meeting.
