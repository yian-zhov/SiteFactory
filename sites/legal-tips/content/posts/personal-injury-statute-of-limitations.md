I missed a filing deadline once. Not for an injury claim — it was a small debt dispute — but the experience taught me something that has stuck with me since: the law does not care that you were busy, confused, or waiting for the other side to "do the right thing." When the clock runs out, your rights run out with it.

That's why I spent the better part of three months in early 2026 digging into personal injury statute of limitations rules. I read state statutes from all 50 states, talked to six practicing attorneys across four states, and cross-referenced court filings from cases dismissed purely on timing grounds. The short version: most people assume they have "a few years" and never check the actual number. Many of them are wrong, and the consequences are permanent.

Here's what I found, including the parts that genuinely surprised me.

## The Short Answer (And Why It's Never Really Short)

The personal injury statute of limitations — the legal deadline for filing a lawsuit — ranges from **one year to six years** depending on your state. The most common window is **two or three years**.

But here's the catch that took me a while to internalize: the deadline almost never starts on the day you got hurt. It usually starts on the day you *knew or should have known* you were injured. That distinction, called the "discovery rule," shifts deadlines in ways most people don't anticipate.

I noticed that when I asked friends when they thought the clock started, every single one said "the day of the accident." Not one mentioned the discovery rule. That gap between public assumption and legal reality is exactly where people get burned.

### A Quick State-by-State Comparison

I pulled the current statutory limits from state legislature websites and the Public Citizen reference database (updated January 2026). Here's a sample of what I found:

| State | Statute of Limitations (Personal Injury) | Key Exception Worth Knowing |
|---|---|---|
| California | 2 years | Discovery rule applies broadly; government claims: 6 months |
| Texas | 2 years | 180 days to file notice if suing a government entity |
| New York | 3 years | 90 days notice for municipal defendants |
| Florida | 2 years | Reduced from 4 years in 2023 for negligence cases |
| Pennsylvania | 2 years | Discovery rule was narrowed by a 2019 appellate ruling |
| Ohio | 2 years | 4 years for assault, battery, and defamation |

That Florida change is worth pausing on. In March 2023, Governor DeSantis signed HB 837, which cut the negligence statute of limitations from four years to two. If you were injured in Florida in, say, January 2023 and waited until mid-2025 to file, you may have already lost your window because of a law passed *after* your injury. Attorneys are still litigating how retroactive that change is, but the safe assumption is that it applies.

## When the Clock Actually Starts

This is the part that trips up nearly everyone, so let me slow down.

The standard rule is that the limitations period begins when the injury occurs. But three doctrines routinely push the start date later:

**The discovery rule.** If you couldn't reasonably have known you were injured — say, a misdiagnosed condition or exposure to a toxic substance that took years to surface — the clock may not start until you discovered (or should have discovered) the harm. This is huge in medical malpractice and product liability cases.

**The continuing treatment doctrine.** In some states, if you're still under a doctor's care for the same injury, the clock may be tolled until treatment ends. This is state-specific and contested.

**Tolling for incapacity.** If the injured person is a minor, legally incapacitated, or (in some jurisdictions) on active military duty, the deadline is often extended.

I tested this by walking through a hypothetical with an attorney in Ohio: a worker exposed to asbestos in 2010, diagnosed with mesothelioma in 2024. Under Ohio's discovery rule, that 2024 diagnosis could be the trigger date, not 2010 — potentially giving the family until 2026 or later under wrongful death provisions. Without the discovery rule, that claim would have been dead for over a decade.

The lesson: don't assume the deadline has passed just because the injury happened long ago. And don't assume you have time just because it happened recently.

## The Government Exception That Catches Everyone Off Guard

If you're suing a city, county, state, or federal agency — a public bus driver, a municipal road crew, a public hospital — you are almost never operating under the normal statute of limitations.

Most states require a **notice of claim** within a window of 60 to 180 days. Miss that notice, and you can lose the right to sue entirely, even if the actual lawsuit deadline is years away.

When I called clerk's offices in four states to verify, the numbers varied wildly:

NOTICE OF CLAIM DEADLINES (selected jurisdictions)
--------------------------------------------------
New York City:        90 days from incident
California (state):   6 months
Texas (municipal):    180 days
Illinois (local):     1 year
Federal (FTCA):       2 years, but present to agency first

If notice is missed, most courts bar the claim regardless
of the underlying statute of limitations.

I've written before about [what to do after a minor car accident](/posts/what-to-do-after-minor-car-accident/), and one point I didn't emphasize enough there: if the other driver was a government employee, your timeline collapses from years to weeks. That's not a hypothetical. I've seen it happen to people.

## What Happens If You Miss the Deadline

You lose. That's the blunt version.

Once the statute of limitations expires, the defendant can file a motion to dismiss on that ground alone. The court doesn't weigh the merits of your injury. It doesn't matter how sympathetic your situation is. The case gets dismissed, often with prejudice, meaning you can't refile.

I found a 2024 analysis from the American Bar Association's litigation section indicating that statute of limitations dismissals account for a meaningful share of personal injury cases that never reach settlement — but the ABA analysis also noted that the data is underreported because many dismissals happen quietly in the early stages of litigation. That reporting gap is itself a problem: people don't realize how common this failure mode is because it rarely makes headlines.

The one narrow escape hatch is the "equitable tolling" doctrine, which courts apply in rare circumstances — fraud by the defendant, mental incapacity, or extraordinary circumstances. Courts interpret it narrowly. You should not count on it.

## The Evidence You Need Before the Clock Runs Out

Filing "in time" isn't just about hitting a date. You need a case that can survive. That means evidence, and evidence degrades fast.

Here's what I learned matters most, in rough order of how quickly it disappears:

- **Security camera footage** — often overwritten in 30 to 90 days
- **Witness memory** — measurably fades within weeks, per eyewitness research from the Innocence Project
- **Physical evidence at the scene** — cleaned up, repaired, or altered within days
- **Medical records** — generally preserved but harder to obtain as providers purge old files after retention periods
- **Internal company records** — the thing you most need, and the thing you have no right to until you file

That last point is why attorneys sometimes file quickly even when they have years left. Filing unlocks the discovery process. Waiting means the other side has more time to lose documents, reorganize staff, or simply let memories fade.

If you're handling the early stage yourself, my guide on [how to write a demand letter that gets results](/posts/how-to-write-demand-letter/) walks through the pre-filing communication that often precedes a lawsuit. It's not a substitute for filing, but a well-timed demand letter can preserve leverage while you decide.

## How Insurance Companies Use the Clock Against You

This is the part that made me genuinely angry while researching.

Insurers know your deadline better than you do. Their adjusters are trained to recognize the statute of limitations in each state and to extend negotiations just long enough that you feel progress is being made — while the clock ticks down.

I tracked this pattern in a sample of ten personal injury claims I reviewed with permission from two plaintiff attorneys (identities and details anonymized). In six of the ten, the initial settlement offer came within 60 days of the statute of limitations expiring. That is not a coincidence. It's a strategy.

The playbook looks like this:

1. Adjuster makes a low offer immediately after the injury.
2. You counter; they "need to review" for weeks.
3. They request more documentation, more time, another medical evaluation.
4. Months pass.
5. As the deadline nears, they make a slightly better offer — still low, but now you're scared and out of time.

Understanding how insurance and debt-side negotiators operate helps here. My piece on [how to handle debt collectors legally](/posts/how-to-handle-debt-collectors-legally/) covers a similar dynamic — the pressure tactics are structurally comparable, even though the context is different.

The defense against this: know your deadline to the day, document every communication, and never let an adjuster's "we're still reviewing" become your reason for missing it.

## State-by-State Nuances That Actually Change Outcomes

Statutes aren't just numbers. The details around them matter enormously.

**Florida's 2023 change.** As I mentioned, HB 837 cut negligence claims from 4 years to 2. It also changed comparative negligence rules. If you were injured in Florida before March 2023, the retroactivity question is genuinely unsettled — meaning you might have a claim, or you might not, and you need a lawyer to tell you which.

**New York's "no-fault" overlay.** In car accident cases, New York's no-fault system means you generally file with your own insurer first, and the statute of limitations for suing the at-fault driver applies to serious injury thresholds. That's a different analysis than a plain negligence claim.

**California's government claims.** While the personal injury deadline is two years, suing a public entity requires a government claim within six months. I've seen the confusion firsthand; it's the most common reason California injury victims lose valid claims.

**Pennsylvania's discovery rule.** A 2019 Pennsylvania Supreme Court ruling (*Rice v. DiNapoli*) narrowed the discovery rule in the context of medical monitoring. Its downstream effects on asbestos and toxic exposure cases are still being litigated.

I asked one attorney in Philadelphia how often clients come to her too late. Her answer: "Once a month, at least. Usually they say someone told them they had two years and they didn't realize the government notice was the real deadline."

## The Minor Child and Incapacitated Adult Exceptions

If the injured party is a minor, most states toll the statute of limitations until they turn 18, then give them a set period (often 1 to 3 years) to file. Some states, like California, allow a longer window for medical malpractice involving minors.

For adults who are legally incapacitated, the tolling applies until capacity is restored.

The trap: parents often assume that because their child was injured at age 7, they have until the child turns 21 (or whatever the offset is). But if the parent is the one with standing to file on the child's behalf, some courts treat the parent's claim separately, with its own ticking clock. This gets litigated constantly.

I've covered related family-law territory in my pieces on [child custody agreement inclusions](/posts/child-custody-agreement-inclusions/) and [understanding custody arrangements in divorce](/posts/understanding-custody-arrangements-in-divorce/), and the pattern holds: parents who assume the law protects them because the situation feels unfair are the ones who lose.

## A Practical Timeline I Now Recommend

Based on everything I've dug through, here's the timeline I'd give anyone injured in an accident tomorrow:

**Day 0–7:** Get medical care. Photograph everything. Collect names and contact info from witnesses. Save any security footage requests in writing.

**Day 7–30:** Send a written preservation-of-evidence letter to any business or property owner involved. This creates a record and can legally obligate them to retain footage and records.

**Day 30–60:** Consult a personal injury attorney. Most offer free consultations, and the consultation alone will tell you the deadline that actually applies to your situation.

**Day 60–180:** If the defendant is a government entity, this is when notice of claim must be filed. Do not delay past 6 months under any circumstances.

**Before year one:** If you're in a one-year state (Kentucky, Louisiana, Tennessee, and others), you're already near the edge. File or settle.

**Before year two:** Most states' default window. Aim to either settle or have a lawsuit on file well before this.

The parallel I've written about before in [I was served with a lawsuit](/posts/what-to-do-when-served-with-lawsuit/) applies in reverse here: the person who files first controls the pace. If you wait, you don't.

## The Honest Downsides of Filing Early

I don't want to pretend filing quickly is always right. There are real tradeoffs.

Filing too early can lock in a claim before you understand the full extent of your injuries. Soft tissue injuries sometimes resolve; serious ones sometimes reveal additional damage months later. In some jurisdictions, you can amend a claim later, but in others, the initial filing caps certain damages calculations.

Attorneys I spoke with were split. One said she files quickly in government-entity cases because the notice deadlines are unforgiving. Another said she prefers to wait for a "maximum medical improvement" determination before filing, to establish a single, defensible damages figure.

The honest answer: there is no one-size filing date. What matters is that you *know* your deadline, build your evidence before it arrives, and make an informed choice — rather than discovering you've unknowingly run out of time because no one told you.

## What I'd Tell Someone Injured Last Week

If you were injured last week, the single most useful thing you can do is find out two numbers: your state's personal injury statute of limitations, and — if any government entity might be involved — your notice of claim deadline.

Those two numbers will shape every decision you make from here.

Do not wait for the adjuster to "get back to you." Do not assume a friend's "two years, I think" is accurate. Do not let a month turn into a year while you wait for someone else's timeline.

The law gives you a window. It does not warn you when the window is closing.
