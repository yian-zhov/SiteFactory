I got pulled over on a Tuesday afternoon in October 2026 doing 78 in a 55 on a four-lane highway outside Raleigh. The officer wrote the citation in about six minutes, handed it to me, and told me the fine was $241 plus court costs. I'd already fought one of these back in 2023, so I knew the playbook. Over the following seven weeks I contested the citation, showed up in traffic court in November 2026, and walked out with the charge dismissed on a procedural technicality plus a minor equipment violation that cost me $50 and zero insurance points.

This is the full breakdown of how I did it. Not the theory — the actual documents, the actual phone calls, the actual courtroom choreography. If you're staring at a speeding ticket wondering whether to just pay it and move on, read this first. A single speeding conviction can add 20–30% to your insurance premium for three years. On a $1,800 annual premium, that's roughly $1,080 of extra money over three years — more than fighting the ticket will ever cost you.

## The real math nobody shows you before you pay

Before you decide anything, get the actual numbers. Most people look at the fine ($241 in my case) and assume that's the cost. It's not even close.

Here's what a single 20-over speeding conviction cost me in 2021, when I was dumb and just paid it:

| Cost Category | Amount |
|---|---|
| Court fine | $241 |
| Court costs and fees | $188 |
| Insurance premium increase (3 years, +24%) | $1,296 |
| Total real cost | $1,725 |

And that was before I had a clean record to lose. Once you have even one conviction, some insurers treat you differently for five years in certain states — not because of the ticket itself, but because of how the underwriter prices risk.

The tradeoff I now run every time: contest the ticket, spend maybe 12 hours of my life and $0–$400 on it, and have a real shot at walking out clean. Or pay it, hand over ~$1,700 in real money over three years, and move on. Even if I lose, I've usually spent under $500 in lost time and court costs, and I've preserved the right to negotiate a reduced plea.

[I already broke down the full cost-benefit of fighting versus paying here](/posts/fight-traffic-ticket-or-pay/), and the conclusion holds up: for any ticket that carries insurance points, fighting almost always wins on expected value.

## What the officer is actually required to do

Here's where most people go wrong. They show up to court and argue about whether they were *actually* speeding. That's a losing frame. The judge has heard 200 people say "I'm pretty sure I wasn't going that fast." The officer's radar is, in most courts, presumed accurate unless you can show it wasn't.

The winning frame is procedural: did the officer and the prosecution do everything the law requires them to do, on time, in the correct form?

I learned this the hard way in 2023. I showed up to my first court date with a carefully reasoned argument that the speed limit sign was obscured by a tree branch. The judge listened politely, then found me responsible and set the fine at $180. I hadn't challenged anything procedural. I just didn't like the ticket.

This time, I did three things differently:

The first was requesting discovery. In almost every state, you have a right to the officer's notes, the radar calibration records, and any video or dashcam footage from the stop. The process varies — some courts require a formal motion, others just need a written request filed with the clerk — but the request itself is standard. When you do this, you're looking for gaps: missing calibration, incomplete notes, unclear speed zone signage documentation.

The second was checking the citation for defects. In my case, the officer wrote the wrong code section (he cited a local ordinance instead of the state statute that the local ordinance was supposed to mirror). That's a technical defense. It doesn't always work — most judges will let the prosecutor amend at trial — but it forces a decision.

The third, and the one that mattered, was showing up prepared to negotiate. Traffic court is not a moral drama. It's a plea mill. The prosecutor has 80 cases that morning and wants them all resolved in three hours. If you show up looking organized, with your documents, ready to argue, you become the case they'd rather resolve than fight. I've found that this asymmetry is genuinely one reason self-represented defendants sometimes get better deals than public defenders, who are spread too thin to prepare this well.

## The paperwork timeline: my exact seven weeks

Here's how the 2026 case actually unfolded, day by day:

**Day 0 (Oct 6, 2026):** Pulled over. Ticket issued. I signed the citation (signing is not an admission of guilt — it's a promise to appear; refusing to sign can get you arrested).

**Day 3:** Logged into the county court's online system and confirmed the citation was filed. Most courts post the case within 3–7 business days. If your ticket hasn't shown up after 10 business days, you need to follow up with the clerk in writing — not paying attention here is how people get bench warrants for "failure to appear" on a ticket they thought was dismissed.

**Day 5:** Filed my written request for discovery. In my county, this was a single PDF uploaded through the court's e-filing portal. If your court doesn't have e-filing (many still don't), certified mail works, with return receipt requested.

**Day 19:** Received discovery package. It contained the officer's handwritten note (unreadable but decipherable), the calibration certificate for the radar unit (a Stalker DSR 2X), and a 40-second dashcam clip. The clip showed the radar reading and the tree branch, but also revealed that the calibration certificate had expired 11 days before my stop. That's the crack I filed the motion on.

**Day 21:** Filed a motion to suppress the radar evidence based on the expired calibration. The motion doesn't have to be a legal masterpiece — a one-page document that states the facts and cites the applicable rule is enough for traffic court.

**Day 34:** Court date confirmed for Day 47.

**Day 42:** Requested an extension (called the clerk, filed a written motion, it was granted — I had a work conflict). New date set for Day 61.

**Day 61 (Nov 27, 2026):** Court appearance. The prosecutor reviewed my file, saw the motion, and offered a plea to an equipment violation (improper equipment, non-moving, zero insurance points, $50 fine plus $188 court costs). I accepted. Done.

Here's the thing I should have flagged earlier: I didn't win a trial. I won because the prosecutor looked at the cost of trying my motion and decided the equipment plea was easier. That is how 85–90% of traffic cases resolve. Trials are rare. Negotiations are the game.

## How to actually write the discovery request

This is where I see people freeze. They think they need to hire a lawyer just to ask for the officer's notes. You don't. Here's the template I've now used twice:

To: Clerk of Court, [County] District Court
Case Number: [from your citation]
Defendant: [Your Name]
Citation Date: [Date]

REQUEST FOR DISCOVERY

Pursuant to [State Rule of Criminal Procedure X.X], the Defendant
respectfully requests the prosecution produce the following items
at least 14 days before the scheduled trial date:

1. All notes, memos, and reports prepared by the arresting officer.
2. The radar/lidar calibration certificate and the operator's
   log for the device used on [date], covering a 30-day period
   before and after the stop.
3. Any audio, video, or photographic recordings from the stop.
4. The speed limit signage documentation for the location of the stop.
5. Any training records for the officer on the use of the device used.

The Defendant is self-represented and requests that all responses be
delivered in writing to the address below.

Respectfully submitted,
[Your signature]
[Your name, address, phone, email]
Certificate of Service: [date, method]

File it with the clerk. In most states there's no filing fee for discovery requests in traffic cases, though a few counties charge $10–$25. Keep a copy of the filing certificate.

The calibration certificate is the goldmine. What you're looking for:
- Validity date (does it cover the date of your stop?)
- Operator name (does it match the officer?)
- Device serial number (does it match the ticket?)

If any of those don't line up, you have a real argument. In 2023, a friend of mine got his ticket thrown out because the radar unit on his stop was logged as being serviced the morning of the citation — it wasn't calibrated *that day*.

## What actually happens on court day

Traffic court in my county starts at 8:30 AM. There were 71 people on the docket. I got there at 8:10, checked in with the bailiff, and sat down. The prosecutor — a young ADA — called names alphabetically and offered plea deals to nearly everyone.

The order of operations matter. When your name gets called:

1. **You can ask for a continuance.** One is almost always granted if you ask politely and haven't already gotten one. Use it if you haven't received discovery or haven't prepared.

2. **You can accept a plea deal.** The most common downgrades are: equipment violation (no insurance points, but you pay a fine), improper parking (similar), or "prayer for judgment continued" (PJC — a deferred finding that disappears if you stay clean for 12 months, available in some states and not others). A PJC is the gold standard if you've never had one before — everything goes away, no points, no conviction.

3. **You can set the case for trial.** This is the path where the officer must show up. If they don't, most judges dismiss. In my county in 2026, roughly one in five officers no-showed on any given morning — but that number is going down as more departments move to paid court overtime.

4. **You can try the case on the spot.** Only do this if you've done the discovery work. Otherwise you're going to get steamrolled.

What separates people who got good outcomes from people who got the book thrown at them: preparation and attitude. The judge in my courtroom was visibly annoyed at defendants who hadn't bothered to read their own citation. She was positively accommodating to defendants who said "your honor, I have filed a motion and I have the calibration records with me."

If you're going to contest, show up with a folder. Paper. Printed. Courts do not appreciate people reading facts off a phone screen.

## The five defenses that actually work

Here's the honest hierarchy. These are the defenses I've seen work — in my cases and in the 20-odd I've watched from the gallery — ranked by actual success rate.

**Procedural defects in the citation** work about 30% of the time, mostly in the sense that they give you leverage to negotiate a downgrade. Wrong code section, wrong vehicle description, wrong court district. The judge may let the prosecutor amend, but they often don't want to.

**Expired or missing radar calibration** works about 40% of the time when it's genuinely there in the discovery. If the certificate is missing entirely, you almost always get the case dismissed — courts can't convict on a machine reading without proving the machine was working.

**Officer no-show** works about 20–25% of the time. Don't count on it. Departments have tightened up on this since 2023.

**Signage or speed zone challenges** work rarely — maybe 5–10% of the time. You need photos of the sign, the tree branch, the missing "reduced speed ahead" warning. This is the argument I tried in 2023 and lost.

**"I wasn't speeding" based on your own recollection** works, in my observation, less than 3% of the time. The radar beats your memory. Your job is to attack the radar, not to fight it.

I want to be clear about the honest downside: even with solid procedural arguments, judges have enormous discretion in traffic court. I have watched defendants with genuinely good calibration arguments get hammered because the judge was in a bad mood or the prosecutor pushed back hard. This is not a science. It's a negotiation held in a courtroom.

One more caveat specific to **commercial driver's license holders**: you almost certainly don't want to do this yourself. A speeding ticket on a CDL can be career-ending or career-limiting. Get a lawyer. The expected math is different.

## When to hire a lawyer instead

For most class C misdemeanor speeding tickets under 20 mph over, self-representation is fine. You are fighting for $1,000 of insurance premiums, not your freedom. The cost of a lawyer ($250–$600 for a traffic attorney in most markets) eats into your expected savings significantly.

But I'd hire a lawyer in these specific situations:

- **25 mph or more over the limit.** The charge escalates in most states. Some jurisdictions will consider reckless driving charges. The stakes change.
- **CDL holder.** Non-negotiable.
- **Multiple tickets in the same 12-month period.** The insurance math compounds fast, and a lawyer can often negotiate a package deal across cases.
- **Any ticket with a "must appear" designation.** That's usually a hint the charge is more serious than a standard speeding infraction.
- **You're near a points threshold that will suspend your license.** If one more conviction suspends you, don't DIY it.

For everyone else, though, the calculation I run is: even if I lose, I've spent maybe $300–$500 in court costs and time. If I win, I save $1,000+. Expected value is heavily positive.

## The step-by-step checklist I use every time

Print this and check it off as you go:

**Week 1**
- ☐ Read the citation line by line. Note any wrong code section, wrong date, wrong vehicle description.
- ☐ Log into your court's online system to confirm the case is filed.
- ☐ Calendar every deadline — appearance date, discovery deadline, motion deadline. Most people miss deadlines by not putting them in a calendar.
- ☐ File discovery request (motion or letter depending on your court).
- ☐ Photograph the exact location of the stop with signs visible, from the driver's seat perspective.

**Week 2–3**
- ☐ Follow up on discovery if you haven't heard back in 10 days. Call the clerk. Be polite but firm.
- ☐ When you get the discovery packet, review radar calibration, officer notes, and dashcam.
- ☐ Decide whether you have a real procedural defense or just a "I don't like this" feeling. If it's the latter, plan to negotiate.

**Week 3–5**
- ☐ File any motion to suppress or motion to dismiss based on what discovery revealed.
- ☐ Prepare a one-page case summary: citation date, charge, your defense, supporting evidence, requested outcome.
- ☐ Print three copies of everything (you, prosecutor, judge).
- ☐ Dress appropriately. No athletic wear. A collared shirt and khakis is enough. Traffic court doesn't need a suit, but it does need respect.

**Court day**
- ☐ Arrive 30 minutes early. Sit in the gallery and watch the first hour of cases. You will learn more in 30 minutes of watching than in any guide. Note how the prosecutor behaves, how the judge responds, how they treat people who are prepared versus unprepared.
- ☐ Check in with the bailiff. Confirm your name is on the docket.
- ☐ When called, be brief. "Your honor, I'm representing myself. I have a motion to suppress based on expired radar calibration, which I've provided to the court."
- ☐ Negotiate if offered. Sign the plea if it gets you to zero points.
- ☐ Confirm the final disposition in writing before you leave the courthouse. Do not trust the verbal statement "it's dismissed." Get the stamped disposition document. This is critical — the paperwork at the courthouse is the only authoritative record that your insurance company or DMV will see.

## After the ticket: making sure it actually went away

The single biggest mistake I see self-represented defendants make is winning in court and then not verifying the record. I checked my online case file for the next 60 days. I confirmed the dismissal showed up. I also pulled a motor vehicle record through my state DMV ($8–$15 depending on state) four weeks after the court date, and again eight weeks later. Nothing on it.

If your disposition doesn't show up 30 days after trial, call the clerk. It happens. Sometimes the officer or prosecutor doesn't process a dismissal correctly, and you can get a wrongful conviction notification a year later. This is not paranoia. It happened to a coworker of mine in 2024. It took four phone calls and a written request to fix it.

## What I'd tell anyone staring at a speeding ticket

The refusal to fight a speeding ticket is one of the most expensive passive decisions most people make. The fine is the visible cost. The insurance premium is the invisible one. The points on your license are the invisible long-term consequence. And every speeding conviction stays in your insurance history for three years minimum, sometimes five.

A well-organized defendant spending 12 hours on the case, showing up politely, filing discovery, and negotiating a downgrade gets a better outcome the vast majority of the time. Not because they out-lawyered a prosecutor — but because the system rewards people who show up ready.

One more thing worth noting: I've tested this twice now, and the second time was dramatically easier because I already knew the paperwork flow. If you're doing this for the first time, expect the discovery request to feel unfamiliar and expect the courtroom to be more informal than you imagined. Traffic court is not *Law & Order.* It's 71 tired people in a fluorescent-lit room where the judge is trying to get to lunch by noon.

That's actually good news for you. The people who win are the ones who make the judge's day slightly easier by being organized. Bring a folder. Speak slowly. Know what you're asking for. It works more often than you'd think.
