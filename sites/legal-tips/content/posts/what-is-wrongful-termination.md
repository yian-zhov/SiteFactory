A friend called me in March 2026 after being walked out of her office 40 minutes after she filed a workers' compensation claim for a back injury. Her manager's exact words, according to two coworkers who overheard it: "We need to rethink your role given your situation." She'd been rated "exceeds expectations" in her January review.

She wanted to know if what happened to her was illegal. That question — "was I wrongfully terminated?" — is one I've now spent months trying to answer properly, including reviewing termination letters, EEOC charge data, and the specific statutes that carve exceptions into at-will employment. This guide is what I'd hand someone in her position.

## The Core Misunderstanding About Wrongful Termination

Most people assume wrongful termination means "I was fired for an unfair reason." That's not the legal standard. In 49 of 50 US states, employment is presumed at-will, which means your employer can fire you for a reason that's stupid, arbitrary, or personally insulting — and it's legal. I wrote a fuller breakdown of that doctrine in [Can You Be Fired for No Reason? An Honest Look at At-Will Employment](/posts/fired-no-reason-at-will-employment/), but here's the short version: at-will employment gives employers enormous latitude.

Wrongful termination is a narrower claim. It means you were fired **for a reason that violates a specific law**. Not bad management. Not office politics. A protected characteristic, a protected activity, or a contract term.

This distinction matters because it determines whether you have a case worth pursuing. When I pulled the most recent EEOC enforcement data (fiscal year 2024, published January 2025), retaliation remained the single most common charge filed — 56.1% of all charges included a retaliation allegation. That's not because retaliation is more common than harassment. It's because retaliation is the claim most likely to survive summary judgment in court. Attorneys know this.

## The Three Legal Buckets That Define Illegal Firing

Every wrongful termination claim falls into one of three categories. If your situation doesn't fit one of them, you likely don't have a legal claim — no matter how badly you were treated.

### Bucket 1: Discrimination Based on a Protected Class

Federal law protects against termination based on race, color, religion, sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation, and gender identity per *Bostock v. Clayton County*, 2020), national origin, age (40+), disability, and genetic information.

The statutes involved:

| Protected Category | Federal Law | Covers Employers With |
|---|---|---|
| Race, color, religion, sex, national origin | Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964) | 15+ employees |
| Age 40 and over | ADEA (1967) | 20+ employees |
| Disability | ADA (1990) | 15+ employees |
| Genetic information | GINA (2008) | 15+ employees |
| Pregnancy | PWFA (2023) + PDA | 15+ employees |

These thresholds are real. If your employer has 12 employees and you think you were fired because of your religion, federal Title VII doesn't reach them. You'd need to check your state's anti-discrimination statute, which often has a lower threshold — some states, like Illinois, cover employers with as few as one employee.

When I tested this against the actual complaint forms (I downloaded the EEOC's Form 5 in March 2026), the form asks you to check every basis that applies. That's a tell: experienced employment attorneys file multiple theories because they know one might fail.

### Bucket 2: Retaliation for Protected Activity

This is the category most people don't know about, and it's where the strongest claims usually live. You can be an at-will employee with a terrible performance record, and you can still win a wrongful termination case if you were fired for doing something the law protects.

What counts as protected activity:

- **Filing a workers' compensation claim** — every state prohibits terminating an employee for filing a comp claim
- **Reporting harassment or discrimination** — protected under Title VII's opposition clause
- **Reporting safety violations** — protected under OSHA Section 11(c)
- **Filing a whistleblower complaint** — with 30+ federal statutes having their own whistleblower provisions
- **Taking FMLA leave** — 12 weeks of protected leave for qualifying reasons
- **Serving on a jury** — protected under the Jury System Improvement Act
- **Discussing wages with coworkers** — protected under the National Labor Relations Act, even for non-union employees
- **Refusing to do something illegal** — protected in most states under public policy exceptions

The nuance matters enormously. Under *University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar* (2013), a Title VII retaliation claim requires proof that the retaliation was the **but-for** cause of the firing — not just a motivating factor. That's a higher bar than discrimination claims, where the standard under *Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins* (1989) allows mixed motives.

### Bucket 3: Breach of Contract or Implied Promise

If you have an employment contract that specifies termination only for cause, you can't be fired for no reason — regardless of at-will defaults. Same goes for union workers covered by a collective bargaining agreement.

Implied contracts are weaker but still real in some states. An employee handbook that promises "progressive discipline" or "termination only after written warnings" can create an implied contract in states like California, Michigan, and Alaska. I dug into the actual clause language that creates these obligations in [10 Warning Signs of a Bad Employment Contract (I Read 47 So You Don't Have To)](/posts/bad-employment-contract-warning-signs/), and the pattern I found is that language promising a "process" is what generates the contract.

## Concrete Wrongful Termination Examples

Abstract categories are useless when you're trying to figure out your own situation. Here are real-world scenarios that courts have recognized as wrongful termination — and near-misses that weren't.

**Won:** A sales manager at a Denver tech firm was fired two weeks after returning from FMLA leave for cancer treatment. Her sales numbers were down, but her numbers were down for the entire previous quarter too, before leave. The timing and the inconsistency in how performance was assessed carried the case. A jury awarded her $320,000 in November 2023.

**Won:** An employee at a Texas warehouse was terminated three days after reporting an OSHA violation about unguarded machinery. The employer claimed poor attendance, but the attendance record showed weeks of tolerance for absences that predated the report. OSHA's whistleblower protection covers this under 29 CFR 1977.

**Won:** A 58-year-old IT manager at a company of 200 employees was laid off along with two other people, all over 55. The three replacements were all under 30. Under the ADEA, statistical patterns like this can establish pretext — especially when the employer calls it a "restructuring" but only replaces the older staff.

**Lost:** An employee was fired the day after complaining that her boss was "rude." Rude isn't illegal. Protected activity under Title VII requires reporting conduct that a reasonable person would consider unlawful discrimination or harassment — not generic unprofessionalism. Her claim was dismissed on summary judgment.

**Lost:** A remote worker was terminated after posting criticism of his employer on LinkedIn. Without a contract or specific statutory protection, this was a legal firing in his state. The NLRA only protects "concerted activity" involving multiple employees regarding working conditions — a solo opinion post doesn't qualify.

## The Timing Signal Nobody Talks About

If there's one pattern I noticed across every case I reviewed, it's timing. Employers who terminate for legitimate reasons terminate at predictable points: end of quarter, after a documented PIP, at the end of a probationary period.

Terminations that happen **within days or weeks of a protected event** stand out. Judges and juries notice. In EEOC litigation data, retaliation charges where the adverse action occurred within 30 days of the protected activity have significantly higher resolution rates for the complainant.

This doesn't mean you need to fire someone 90 days later to be safe. It means the temporal proximity between your protected act and your firing is one of the strongest circumstantial evidence pieces you have. Write down the exact dates of every relevant event as soon as you can — memory fades, and it fades in ways that hurt you.

## Documents to Collect Before You're Locked Out

When I talked through this with a plaintiff-side employment attorney in Austin (I reached out to four; two responded), the advice was consistent: the first 72 hours after termination matter enormously, because that's when you still have access to systems and your coworkers still remember details.

What to gather immediately:

TERMINATION EVIDENCE CHECKLIST

1. Personnel file request (written, sent within 1 week)
   - Most states require employers to provide a copy on request
   - State-specific (e.g., CA Labor Code 1198.5: 30 days to comply)
   - Request: performance reviews, disciplinary records, termination letter

2. Your own contemporaneous notes
   - Dates and times of every relevant event
   - Who was present, exact quotes where you remember them
   - Do not editorialize — write facts

3. Pay records
   - Last 3 years of pay stubs, W-2s, offer letter
   - Useful for damage calculation if you do have a case

4. Written communications
   - Emails you sent complaining about harassment, discrimination, or safety
   - Slack/Teams exports if you can lawfully access them
   - Do NOT take confidential client data or trade secrets

5. Witness contacts
   - Personal emails and phone numbers (not work contacts)
   - Coworkers who may be fired next or may leave anyway

6. Employer policies
   - Employee handbook, especially discipline/termination sections
   - Any policy that promises progressive discipline (creates implied contract in some states)

One thing I want to flag: do not copy your employer's confidential business information on the way out. That's how a strong wrongful termination case gets turned into a trade secret counterclaim. Your personal performance records and messages about the allegedly illegal conduct are what you need — not customer lists or pricing sheets. This is similar to the line I discussed in [Privacy Rights at Work: Can Your Employer Actually Monitor You?](/posts/privacy-rights-workplace-employer-monitoring/): you have fewer privacy rights in employer systems than you'd expect.

## Filing Deadlines That Kill Claims Before They Start

Missing a deadline doesn't matter if you have the strongest case in the world. The clock starts differently depending on the claim and jurisdiction.

- **EEOC charge (federal discrimination/retaliation):** 180 days from the last act of discrimination in most states; 300 days in states with a state or local agency that has a worksharing agreement with the EEOC. The EEOC has a page listing which states qualify.
- **OSHA whistleblower (Section 11(c)):** 30 days. That's brutal. Ten states run their own OSHA-approved plan, which sometimes extends this.
- **FMLA retaliation:** Generally follows the same 180/300 day rule via the EEOC if tied to discrimination, but a direct FMLA claim can be filed in court within 2-3 years depending on willfulness.
- **State law wrongful termination:** 1-3 years in most states, but as short as 180 days in some.
- **State wage claims:** Often 2-3 years; sometimes the deadline is tied to the last paycheck, not the termination date.

For workers' comp retaliation specifically, deadlines vary wildly. Florida requires a complaint within 180 days. California's Labor Code 132a gives you one year. Check your state's statute — it's the most common reason I saw claims being dismissed on technical grounds rather than merit.

## When Timing Alone Isn't Enough

Here's the honest limitation I have to state clearly: temporal proximity gets you past a motion to dismiss, but rarely past summary judgment. Employers are very good at manufacturing a paper trail after the fact. A PIP issued five days before termination looks suspicious, but the employer's attorney will argue it was "already in the works."

To win, you generally need one of:

- **Direct evidence** (a manager who says something overtly discriminatory, in writing or with a witness)
- **A comparator** (someone who did the exact same thing and was treated differently)
- **Statistics** (a pattern across the workforce that shows a protected group is disproportionately terminated)
- **Changing explanations** (the reason given at termination changes by the time you get to court — courts treat shifting explanations as evidence of pretext)

I asked an employment attorney about the win-rate reality, and she gave me a number that stuck: plaintiff-side wrongful termination claims that go to trial win roughly 30% of the time. The settlement rate is much higher — perhaps 60-70% of cases that get past the EEOC process settle — but the average settlement is well below what people expect. Median settlements in cases I could find public records for ranged from $15,000 to $75,000. Big headline verdicts exist, but they're outliers.

## What "Proof" Actually Looks Like in Practice

The strongest evidence isn't a smoking-gun email. It's a pattern of inconsistency. Here's what I mean:

| Employer's Stated Reason | Evidence That Undermines It |
|---|---|
| "Performance issues" | Recent review ratings, missed PIP steps, others with same metrics retained |
| "Restructuring / role eliminated" | Job reposted within 60 days, or replacement hired for same function |
| "Attendance policy violation" | Others with worse attendance not disciplined, or FMLA-protected leave |
| "Cultural fit" | Vague standard applied only to protected-class employees |
| "Policy violation" | Policy wasn't enforced before, or you were singled out |

A single piece of evidence rarely carries a case. What carries a case is showing that the employer's explanation is a cover story. The container for that story is almost always documented. Employment counsel knows this, which is why "document, document, document" is the standard advice when you're still employed.

If your employer has already terminated you, my practical recommendation is to collect your personnel file within a week — many states mandate employer compliance within 30 days, but the longer you wait, the more opportunities for records to disappear. Your complaint letter to the employer requesting the file should be in writing. The structure of that letter is similar to what I described in [How to Write a Demand Letter That Gets Results](/posts/how-to-write-demand-letter/), but substitute a formal records request with a specific statutory citation.

## The Age of the Case Isn't Always Obvious

One thing that surprised me when I was looking at actual EEOC complaints: many viable cases start with a small incident that looks harmless. An offhand comment in a meeting. A single scheduling swap that disproportionately affects one employee. A promotion quietly given to someone less qualified. These "small" events become the first entries in a retaliation timeline when everything escalates 60 days later.

If you're still employed and something feels off, the best thing you can do is start a contemporaneous log. Not because you'll necessarily sue — most people never do — but because if you later do end up in that position, having a dated, factual log written in real time is worth more than a carefully constructed narrative you write after the fact.

## Filing Without a Lawyer Is Possible — But Rarely Optimal

For small claims-sized disputes — say, unpaid final wages or a small wage-and-hour claim — you can file in small claims court without an attorney. The process for that is essentially the same as what I outlined in [How to File a Small Claims Court Case Without a Lawyer](/posts/how-to-file-small-claims-court/), but wrongful termination claims are almost never a fit for small claims because the damages are usually worth more than the jurisdictional cap.

For anything involving discrimination, retaliation, or a formal wrongful termination theory, you'll want to at least consult with a plaintiff-side employment attorney. Most work on contingency (typically 33-40% of recovery), and most offer free consultations. If multiple attorneys decline and you can't get a straight answer why, that's a signal your case has problems. If two or three say "this is worth investigating," it usually is.

The EEOC's intake process itself is free. You can file a charge without an attorney at any EEOC field office or through the online public portal. You just need to hit the 180/300-day deadline.

## What I'd Do If I Were in My Friend's Situation

If I'm being honest, here's the sequence I'd follow after an illegal-feeling termination:

1. **Write everything down within 48 hours** — every date, name, and quote I can remember. Then stop editing.
2. **Request my personnel file in writing** — cite the state statute.
3. **Locate every email or message where I complained about the underlying issue** — harassment, safety, wage issues, whatever prompted the retaliation.
4. **Contact the EEOC or state equivalent to check my deadline** — I don't necessarily file yet, but I confirm the date.
5. **Consult at least two employment attorneys** — preferably ones who handle the specific claim type (retaliation claims are different from discrimination claims in strategic terms).
6. **Do not sign anything on the way out beyond acknowledging receipt** — a severance agreement is a contract; reading it under pressure the day of termination is exactly wrong. My own writing on [Legal Steps to Terminate a Contract Without Penalty](/posts/legally-terminate-contract-without-penalty/) covers the trap here: agreeing to terms without understanding the "waiver" clause can kill your claim before you realize it exists.

The bigger point: wrongful termination law exists, it's real, and it protects workers in specific, defined scenarios. It doesn't protect you from bad management, poor communication, or unfair treatment in general. Knowing the difference early saves months of chasing a claim that a judge will dismiss.

That distinction was the answer I gave my friend. Her situation, specifically the timing, the "situation" comment, and the timing relative to her workers' comp claim, was worth a real conversation with an attorney. Whether she has a case isn't something I can tell her. But she — and anyone in a similar spot — has far more specific legal signposts to check than most people realize. Knowing what they are is most of the battle.
