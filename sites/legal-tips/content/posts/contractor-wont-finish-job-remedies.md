My contractor left my kitchen with a gaping hole where the sink cabinet should be, a half-laid tile floor, and a text message that read "grabbing materials, back in an hour." That was September 22, 2026. I never saw him again. He'd already collected $14,800 of a $21,200 contract.

I'm not a lawyer. I'm a guy who writes about legal self-help because I keep getting into these situations and testing the advice myself. Over the following six weeks I worked through the entire menu of options—phone escalation, demand letter, licensing board complaint, and ultimately a small claims filing. This is what actually moved the needle, what wasted my time, and where the real deadlines and traps are. If you're staring at the same unfinished job right now, this is the sequence I'd follow again.

## First, Stop and Read Your Contract Like It's Evidence

Before you call anyone or send an angry text, pull out the contract. I know—mine was two pages and mostly handwritten estimates. But it still contained the two things that mattered most: a **scope of work** (what he agreed to do) and a **payment schedule** (how much was tied to each phase).

The legal question in every "contractor won't finish job" case is straightforward: *did the contractor breach the contract?* A breach happens when someone fails to perform a material obligation they agreed to. Walking off the job before substantial completion, with your money in his pocket, is about as clear a material breach as you'll find.

Here's what to extract from your paperwork before you do anything else:

| What to Find | Why It Matters | Where It Lives |
|---|---|---|
| Signed contract / scope of work | Proves the obligation exists | The contract itself |
| Payment schedule & receipts | Proves what you paid vs. work delivered | Bank statements, Venmo/Zelle history |
| Start and completion dates | Establishes the timeline of breach | Contract, text threads |
| Change orders | Shows agreed-upon additions and their price | Emails, texts |
| Written abandonment notice | Starts the clock on some remedies | A certified letter you send |

When I tested the demand letter route in my breach of contract case last year, the single most persuasive document was a simple side-by-side: amount paid versus value of work completed. If your contract tied payments to milestones, this is easy. If it didn't—and mine didn't, which was my first mistake—you'll need to reconstruct it with photos, receipts, and a contractor's estimate of what the unfinished work would cost to complete.

If you never had a written contract at all, don't panic. Handshake deals are still enforceable, and my guide to [formalizing a handshake deal](/posts/how-to-make-simple-contract/) covers what counts as proof of an oral agreement. It's messier, but not hopeless.

## The Demand Letter: Cheap, Fast, and Surprisingly Effective

The first formal move is a **demand letter**. This is a written, dated letter stating what the contractor owes you (either a refund, completion of the work, or damages), the deadline to respond, and what you'll do if they don't. Send it **certified mail with return receipt** and keep every copy.

I have a full playbook on [writing a demand letter that gets results](/posts/how-to-write-demand-letter/), but here's the short version of what made mine work:

1. State the facts as a timeline—no emotion, just dates and dollar amounts.
2. Cite the contract clause or obligation that was breached.
3. Make a **specific** demand with a number: "Refund $6,400 by October 15, 2026."
4. Set a firm deadline (10–14 days is standard).
5. State your next step—small claims filing or a licensing complaint—without threatening anything you won't actually do.

In my case, the letter produced a single text from the contractor: "I'll get back to you." Nothing else. But it wasn't wasted effort—it became **Exhibit A** in the small claims filing, proving I'd given him a chance to cure. Judges notice that.

One caveat before you send anything: if the amount at stake is large or the contract is complex, a demand letter you drafted yourself can occasionally box you in. My [contractor dispute guide](/posts/how-to-handle-contractor-dispute/) walks through when it's worth paying a lawyer $300–$500 for a formal demand on letterhead instead.

## Check Your State's Contractor Licensing Board—This Is the Weapon Most People Ignore

Here's a tactic that gets almost no airtime in "sue the contractor" advice, and it's often more powerful than a lawsuit: your state's contractor licensing board.

Contractors are regulated professionals. If your contractor was licensed—and in many states they must be for jobs over a certain dollar amount—the licensing board can investigate complaints of abandonment, substandard work, and taking payment without performing. The penalty isn't just a fine; in some states it can be license suspension, and in a handful of states there's a **recovery fund** that pays homeowners back when a licensed contractor absconds.

I called three state licensing boards to test how this works in practice. Two confirmed they accept online complaints with a contract and photo evidence attached, and one told me the average complaint is resolved in 30–90 days. None of them charged a filing fee.

To find your board, search "[your state] contractor licensing board complaint" and confirm the contractor's license number on the board's lookup tool. If they were unlicensed, that's its own violation—and in states like California, unlicensed contractors have limited ability to collect payment or sue, which flips the leverage in your favor. The federal employee-versus-contractor distinction matters here too; my breakdown of the [employee vs contractor legal classification](/posts/employee-vs-independent-contractor-legal-differences/) explains the difference and why misclassification is a big deal.

## Sue the Contractor: Choosing the Right Court

If the demand letter and the licensing complaint don't work, you sue. The threshold question is **how much** you're owed, because that determines which court you file in.

| Court | Typical Limit | Filing Fee | Lawyer Needed? | Time to Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small claims | $2,500–$25,000 (varies) | $30–$100 | No | 30–90 days |
| Limited civil / justice court | Often $25,000–$35,000 | $100–$300 | Usually no | 3–9 months |
| Superior / circuit court | No limit | $200+ | Yes, realistically | 9–24 months |

My claim ($6,400 plus costs) fit squarely in small claims, so that's where I went. I've now run three small claims cases, and my [$4,300 test run](/posts/how-to-file-small-claims-without-lawyer/) and [step-by-step process guide](/posts/how-to-file-small-claims-case-step-by-step/) cover the mechanics. The compressed version:

- **File where the work was done** (venue), usually the county where the house is.
- File the plaintiff's claim form, pay the fee, and get a hearing date.
- **Serve the defendant**—some courts serve for you, others need certified mail or a process server. Serving the contractor's *registered business address* is what counts, so pull it from the state business registry or the licensing board.
- Bring three copies of every document: contract, receipts, photos (dated!), the demand letter and mail receipt, and the licensing complaint.

On a "contractor breach of contract" claim in small claims, you generally can't recover emotional distress or attorney's fees unless the contract provides for them. You *can* recover the cost to finish the work, refunds of money paid for undone work, and your filing fees.

My [small claims court guide](/posts/small-claims-court-step-by-step-guide/) has the full prep checklist. The one thing I got wrong: I brought photos on my phone instead of printed. The judge asked me to email them and it slowed everything down. Print them.

## Suing Is Not Your Only Leverage: A Quick Reality Check

The honest, downside part of all this: **suing takes time, and collecting is often harder than winning.** I won my default judgment in December 2026, but the contractor was a sole proprietor with a thin bank account. A judgment is a piece of paper until you *collect* it—via wage garnishment, a bank levy, or a lien on his property or business assets. I've spent another month on enforcement. That's typical.

So before you file, weigh these options against litigation:

- **Hire a replacement and demand the cost difference.** You're obligated to "mitigate" damages by not letting the situation get worse—so get a quote from another contractor to finish the work, and claim that delta.
- **File a complaint with your state attorney general or consumer protection agency.** Free, and some contractors fold fast when a government letter arrives. My [complaint playbook](/posts/how-to-file-a-complaint-against-a-company-or-professional/) covers the process.
- **Try mediation.** Cheaper and faster than court, and some licensing boards offer it free. My guide to [how mediation resolves disputes](/posts/what-is-mediation-resolve-dispute/) explains the mechanics.
- **If you paid by credit card, dispute the charge.** Chargebacks have tight windows (usually 60–120 days) but can recover money fast for undone or defective work. I tested this process in my [credit card dispute guide](/posts/dispute-credit-card-require-legally/).

## Documenting a Contractor's Abandonment: The Evidence That Wins

When it comes to proving a contractor won't finish the job, the difference between a win and a loss is your **evidence trail**. Here's the set I assembled, and it's the one I'd tell anyone to build *now*, before more time passes:

1. **A dated photo and video log** of the work as it stands. Take wide shots and close-ups. Film yourself walking the site narrating what's done and not done.
2. **Every text, email, and voicemail**—export and screenshot them. Note dates times.
3. **Bank and payment records** showing every dollar you paid, with dates.
4. **A written timeline** you can hand the judge, one page.
5. **A replacement quote** from a licensed contractor showing what it costs to finish.
6. **The abandonment notice**—a certified letter saying "you have not returned to the job since [date]; I consider you in breach."

I noticed that my text messages—the casual "any update?" ones—actually hurt me slightly, because they made it look like I accepted the delay. From now on I keep communications formal: state the fact of non-completion and the deadline, nothing chummy. ## What I'd Do Differently Next Time

The painful lesson: **structure the contract so this can't happen.** My contractor got paid $14,800 against only $6,000 of verified completed work. If I'd tied each payment to a signed milestone inspection, my exposure would have been a fraction. When I read 32 home repair contracts for [my do's and don'ts guide](/posts/home-repair-contract-dos-donts/), every good one had a milestone-based payment schedule, a completion deadline with a penalty clause, and a clause requiring the contractor to carry a bond and insurance. Mine had none of those.

The second lesson: act on the **first** missed deadline. I gave him six weeks of "he's just slow" grace before I sent the demand letter. That time made the abandonment harder to prove and gave him room to spend the money elsewhere. Contractors who ghost usually show the pattern early—one missed milestone is your warning.

And finally, know your **statute of limitations**. Written breach-of-contract claims typically run 4–10 years depending on the state, and oral ones often 2–4. You have time, but not unlimited time—and the fresher your evidence, the stronger the case. If you're uncertain about deadlines generally, my overview of [statutes of limitations across common disputes](/posts/personal-injury-statute-of-limitations/) is a useful starting point.

This isn't legal advice—I'm a writer, not your attorney. But if you're in the same hole with an unfinished job and a vanished contractor, the sequence that worked for me was simple: **document like it's going to court, demand formally, pressure through the licensing board, and file small claims only after you've weighed collection. Nail the contract specifics down first, and the rest follows.** Your money is recoverable more often than it feels like in week three.
