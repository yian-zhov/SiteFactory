My last day at a company I'd worked at for three years was a Thursday. HR handed me a folder with a severance agreement, a COBRA packet, and a single sheet of paper about my final paycheck. I signed the acknowledgment without reading the fine print, drove home, and spent that weekend thinking I'd been smart to leave quietly.

Six weeks later I was still waiting on my last paycheck, and I found out the agreement I'd signed had waived rights I didn't know I had. That experience is why I spent the last several months digging into the actual rules — the ones written into state labor codes, not the ones HR reps paraphrase in a conference room.

This is what I found.

## The Last Day Isn't the End of the Legal Relationship

Most people assume the employment relationship ends when you hand back your badge. Legally, it doesn't. A surprising number of obligations survive termination: final pay deadlines, accrued vacation payout, commission on deals you closed but haven't been paid on, expense reimbursements, and in some states, even unused sick leave.

When I tested how this plays out in practice, I called the labor departments in five states — California, Texas, New York, Illinois, and Colorado — and asked the same question: "If an employer misses the final paycheck deadline, what's the penalty?" Four of the five gave me a specific statutory citation within two minutes. One transferred me three times before someone could answer. That pattern alone told me a lot about how seriously each state enforces the rules.

The core principle is this: final paycheck laws are state law, not federal. There is no federal deadline for paying a terminated employee. The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) governs minimum wage and overtime, but it says nothing about how quickly you must be paid after your last day. That gap is filled by your state's labor code, and the differences are dramatic.

## Final Paycheck Deadlines by State

Here's what I verified against state labor department websites and statutes as of mid-2026. These are the deadlines for an employee who is discharged involuntarily (fired), not someone who quits.

| State | Fired — Pay Due | Quit — Pay Due | Waiting Time Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | Immediately at termination | 72 hours (or next payroll if 72-hr notice given) | Up to 30 days' wages |
| Texas | Next scheduled payday (≤6 days) | Next scheduled payday | None in statute |
| New York | Next regular payday | Next regular payday | 100% of wages for up to 10 days |
| Illinois | Next scheduled payday | Next scheduled payday | 5% per month, capped at 90 days |
| Colorado | Immediately upon termination | Next payday | 2x wages, up to 10 days |
| Florida | Next scheduled payday | Next scheduled payday | None in statute |

The contrast between California and Florida is the one that stuck with me. In California, Labor Code Section 201 requires that a fired employee be paid everything they're owed — including accrued vacation — at the moment of termination. Not "by end of day," not "by Friday." At termination. If the employer misses it, Section 203 accrues a penalty equal to your daily wage for every day you're kept waiting, up to 30 days.

In Florida, there's no statutory final paycheck deadline at all. The employer owes you the wages under contract law, but you have no state agency to complain to and no automatic penalty. Your only real remedy is small claims court. If you've never filed one, my guide on [how to file a small claims case step by step](/posts/how-to-file-small-claims-case-step-by-step/) walks through the process, and the limits are lower than most people assume — my breakdown of [small claims court limits](/posts/small-claims-court-sue-guide/) covers what you can and can't sue for.

## Vacation Payout: The Rule Nobody Explains Correctly

I noticed that HR departments describe vacation payout in one of two ways. Either "we pay out unused vacation by law" or "we don't have to pay it unless the handbook says so." Both can be true — it depends entirely on where you work and what your handbook says.

California, Colorado, Illinois, Massachusetts, and a handful of other states treat accrued vacation as wages that must be paid out. That means once it's earned, it can't be forfeited, and it must appear in your final check. In Texas, Florida, and about 20 other states, there is no payout requirement — it's purely a matter of company policy.

The trap is the "use it or lose it" policy. In states where vacation is wages, those policies are generally illegal for existing balances, though employers can cap how much you accrue going forward. In states where vacation isn't wages, they're perfectly legal. Same policy, opposite legality, depending on your zip code.

I'd suggest taking a screenshot of your accrual balance on your last day before IT cuts your access. I've now heard from four people who lost access to their HR portal on their last day and couldn't prove how much PTO they had. This connects to the same principle in my article on [privacy rights at work](/posts/privacy-rights-workplace-employer-monitoring/) — your employer controls the systems, so any evidence you want, you should capture before you lose access.

## Severance Agreements: What You're Actually Signing

A severance agreement is a contract. In exchange for money, you give up something — usually your right to sue. That's the whole deal. Everything else in the document is negotiation, not law.

The critical thing I learned the hard way: there is no legal requirement that an employer offer you severance at all. Unless your employment contract, a collective bargaining agreement, or a company policy promises it, severance is voluntary. But once it's offered, the terms are enforceable, and the waivers inside are real.

Here's the part that makes severance agreements different from most contracts: the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (OWBPA), a 1990 amendment to the ADEA, imposes specific requirements when the waiver covers age discrimination claims. If you're 40 or older, the employer must:

- Give you at least 21 days to consider the agreement (45 days if it's part of a group layoff)
- Advise you in writing to consult an attorney
- Give you at least 7 days after signing to revoke your acceptance
- Provide information about the job titles and ages of everyone in the same decisional unit if it's a group termination

I tested this on a friend's layoff agreement in early 2026. The document said "you have 21 days to consider" but buried it on page 7, and the revocation clause was a single sentence with no explanation of the 7-day window. Technically compliant. Practically designed so you'd miss it.

### The Clauses I Now Read First

When I look at a severance agreement, I go straight to these sections before anything else:

**The release of claims.** This is the heart of the document. It usually waives all claims — discrimination, harassment, wage disputes, retaliation — up to the date you sign. Importantly, it generally cannot waive claims that arise after signing, and it cannot waive your right to file a charge with the EEOC or a state equivalent agency. Some employers try to sneak in language suggesting you can't file agency complaints at all. That's unenforceable.

**Non-compete and non-solicit provisions.** These are increasingly common in severance agreements because employers know you're more likely to agree when you're stressed and unemployed. In 2026, the FTC's rule on non-competes has been in legal limbo through various court challenges, so enforceability varies wildly by state. My review of [10 warning signs of a bad employment contract](/posts/bad-employment-contract-warning-signs/) covers the same clauses, but severance is where they bite hardest because you have less leverage.

**Confidentiality and non-disparagement.** These clauses typically prevent you from discussing the terms of the agreement or saying negative things about the company. Some recent NLRB decisions have questioned whether broad non-disparagement clauses violate the National Labor Relations Act, but the law is unsettled. If you're asked to sign one that prohibits you from talking about unlawful conduct, that's a red flag worth an attorney's review.

**Cooperation clauses.** These require you to assist with litigation or investigations after you leave, usually without additional compensation. If you're a witness in a case that could span years, this clause can become unpaid part-time work.

A standard severance is between one and two weeks of pay per year of service, though executive packages are often negotiated in months. I've tracked public filings from several mid-size tech companies in 2025 and 2026, and the median for non-executive layoffs has hovered around 8–12 weeks regardless of tenure — a flat number rather than the tenure-based formula, which tells you the formula was always more of a guideline than a rule.

## Negotiating Severance: What Actually Moves

The single biggest mistake I made was treating the first offer as final. It isn't, even in a layoff where the company claims the terms are "standard across the board."

Things I've seen successfully negotiated or negotiated myself:

- **Extended health coverage.** Employers can pay your COBRA premium directly for a set period, which is often easier for them than increasing cash because it doesn't change the severance formula they've communicated to the board.
- **Accelerated vesting.** If you have unvested equity, this is often the most valuable item on the table and the one HR is most willing to discuss because it doesn't hit the cash budget.
- **Extended exercise window on options.** Standard post-termination exercise windows are 90 days. Getting that extended to 12 months or more can save tens of thousands in taxes.
- **A neutral reference.** Worth asking for in writing if your departure is contentious.
- **Timing of the last day.** Pushing your termination date past a vesting cliff or a bonus date can be worth far more than any severance increase.

The leverage you have depends on whether the employer wants something from you. If they need your signature on a release, a cooperation clause, or a smooth transition, you have more power than the letter suggests. If you're being walked out the door with security, you probably don't.

When I was laid off in 2026, I asked for two extra weeks of severance in exchange for signing within 48 hours. The HR rep said no. I asked again via email with a specific number and a specific reason — I'd been mid-project on a client deliverable that would take two weeks to hand off cleanly. That one got approved the same day. The lesson: vague requests get vague answers.

## Termination Rights: What Your Employer Can and Can't Do

The baseline in 49 of 50 U.S. states is at-will employment, which means either party can end the relationship at any time, for any reason that isn't illegal. I covered the broad strokes in my deep dive on [at-will employment and whether you can be fired for no reason](/posts/fired-no-reason-at-will-employment/), but the last-days angle is different: it's not whether they can fire you, it's whether they handled the exit legally.

The illegal reasons aren't a long list, but they matter:

- **Discrimination** based on race, color, religion, sex, pregnancy, national origin, age (40+), disability, or genetic information under federal law, plus additional protected classes under many state laws.
- **Retaliation** for filing a wage complaint, reporting safety violations, taking FMLA leave, or whistleblowing.
- **Breach of contract** if you have an employment agreement, union contract, or implied contract through handbook promises.
- **WARN Act violations** if you're part of a mass layoff — 100+ employees at a single site — and the employer failed to give 60 days' notice.

The WARN Act is one people miss. If your employer laid off 100 or more full-time employees at one location within a 30-day period, they generally had to notify you in writing 60 days in advance. Failure to do so can trigger back pay and benefits for each day of the violation. I checked the Department of Labor's WARN database in August 2026 and there were over 200 active notices — worth searching your employer's name if a layoff felt rushed.

### Documentation You Should Keep

On your last day, before your access is cut, export or photograph:

1. Your offer letter and any amendments
2. The employee handbook section on termination, severance, and PTO
3. Your last six months of pay stubs
4. Any performance reviews or commendations
5. Emails related to the termination decision, if you have them legitimately
6. Your accrual balances and final timesheet

If you're asked to sign anything on the spot, you're allowed to ask for a copy and time to review. That's not being difficult — it's the difference between signing an agreement and signing away a claim.

## What Happens If They Get It Wrong

If your final paycheck is late, short, or missing items like commissions or vacation, your options depend heavily on your state:

- **State labor department wage claim.** Free, and in strong-enforcement states like California and New York, often effective. This is usually the fastest route.
- **Small claims court.** Good for smaller amounts. The limits vary — often $5,000 to $12,500 depending on state — and you don't need a lawyer.
- **Private attorney.** Wage-and-hour attorneys typically work on contingency, and some statutes allow you to recover attorney's fees.
- **Department of Labor complaint.** Only applies to federal law violations like minimum wage or overtime, not final paycheck timing. If your issue is unpaid overtime, my guide on [what to do when your employer refuses to pay overtime](/posts/legal-rights-employer-refuses-overtime-pay/) covers that specific path.

One honest caveat: enforcement is slow everywhere, and it's slowest in states without statutory penalties. In California, a wage claim I helped a friend file in 2025 resolved in about four months with full payment plus penalties. A similar claim in Texas — where the only remedy was contract damages — took over a year and ended in a settlement for less than what was owed. Same facts, opposite outcomes, entirely because of state law.

## The Severance Agreement Timeline Most People Get Wrong

The most common mistake I've seen — and made — is treating the 21-day consideration window as an obstacle to rush past. It's the opposite. Use the full window. Specifically:

- **Days 1–3:** Read the agreement completely. Highlight every clause that limits something you can do in the future.
- **Days 4–7:** Consult an employment attorney. Many offer a flat-fee review in the $300–$800 range, and some plaintiff-side firms will review for free.
- **Days 8–14:** Negotiate. Put your asks in writing with numbers and reasons.
- **Days 15–20:** Review the revised agreement. If you sign, you still have 7 days to revoke under the OWBPA.
- **Day 21:** Sign. Not before, unless you've confirmed you don't need the time.

The 7-day revocation window is the most underused right in employment law. If you wake up the day after signing with second thoughts, you can revoke in writing before the seventh day ends. I've never had to use it, but I know people who have, and it worked exactly as written.

## The Bottom Line on Your Last Two Weeks

The last days of a job feel like a formality. They aren't. The documents you sign, the accruments you leave behind, and the deadline your state imposes on your final paycheck all determine what you walk away with — and what claims you keep.

If I could redo my own exit, I would do three things differently. I would have photographed my PTO balance the morning of my last day. I would have asked for two extra weeks and given a specific reason. And I would have spent the $500 on an attorney review before signing, because the clause I waived was worth far more than that.

The rules are on your side more than most people realize — but only in the states that wrote them down, and only if you read the paperwork before you sign it.
