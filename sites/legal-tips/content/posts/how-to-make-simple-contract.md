A friend hired a guy named Marcus to remodel a bathroom on a handshake in March 2024. $11,400, six weeks, cash on completion. No paper. When the tile cracked in August and Marcus stopped answering texts, my friend spent three months and $2,200 in a small claims filing just to prove the deal existed at all. The contract would have cost him forty minutes and a stamp.

That story sent me down a rabbit hole. Over the past year I've formalized a dozen verbal agreements of my own — a freelance retainer, a shared equipment purchase, a subletting arrangement, a lending agreement with a cousin — and I've read through roughly 30 DIY contract templates across sites like Rocket Lawyer, LawDepot, and the free ones baked into Google Docs. This article is what I learned about converting a handshake into something a judge will actually enforce.

Before we get into the mechanics: this is my field notes, not legal advice. Contract law varies a lot by state, and I'm not a lawyer. Where the stakes are real, run the finished draft past an attorney for a flat review fee. What I can give you is the practical operating system I now use every time I shake on something.

## What a Handshake Deal Actually Does (and Doesn't) Do

I want to be clear about one thing up front, because a lot of people misunderstand it: **a verbal agreement is often still a legal contract.** In most U.S. states, if there's an offer, an acceptance, and consideration (something of value exchanged), you've got an enforceable deal even if nobody wrote a word. The Statute of Frauds — a set of statutory rules descended from the English Parliament's 1677 act — only requires writing for specific categories of contracts: real estate sales, contracts impossible to perform within one year, suretyship (promising to pay someone else's debt), marriage-related promises, and goods over $500 in most states under UCC §2-201.

So why bother writing anything? Because the handshake gets you into court and then abandons you there. The problem isn't existence. It's **proof**. When I pulled small claims data from a few county court clerk sites last spring, the recurring theme in the plaintiff-loss writeups was the same: "Could not establish the terms of the agreement." A contract's real job is to create evidence before there's a dispute.

Concretely, a written contract does four things the handshake can't:

- It pins down **exact terms** so "reasonable timeline" doesn't become "I assumed next spring."
- It allocates **risk** — who eats the cost if the tile cracks, the shipment is late, the freelancer walks.
- It establishes a **paper trail** a judge or arbitrator can actually read.
- It creates a **psychological checkpoint** — people behave differently once they've signed.

That last one is undervalued. In my own experience signing four equipment-share agreements with a business partner between 2023 and 2025, the two we actually papered were the two that ran to completion without friction. The two we didn't... weren't.

## The Seven Clauses Every DIY Contract Needs

Most generic "simple contract templates" online are bloated with boilerplate. When I read through my stack of samples, I found the useful content condensed to seven clauses. If you write only these, you'll have something both readable and enforceable.

### 1. The parties (and their legal names)

Full legal names, plus business entity names if any, plus addresses, plus signatures. Not "Dave" — "David Michael Kessler, individually and d/b/a Kessler Woodworks LLC." If it's an entity, get the registered name and state of formation. I've seen two disputes fail on this alone because the plaintiff named the trade name and the judge dismissed for lack of standing.

### 2. The scope of work — written in the negative

Most people write scope as "what will be done." The valuable part is *what will not be done*. Fence installation contracts I've seen succeed all include "does not include removal of existing concrete footings"; the ones that fail are silent. Write both what's in and, if there's any realistic ambiguity, what's out.

### 3. Price, payment schedule, and the change-order rule

A total price, plus a schedule if there are milestones, plus — critically — **how changes are handled**. My current template uses this line: "Any change to the scope requires a written change order signed by both parties. Verbal changes are not binding." This single clause has saved me twice.

### 4. Timeline and deadlines (with dates, not durations)

"Within 30 days of signing" is fine. "Promptly" and "as soon as possible" are not. I once represented myself (in small claims, on a $2,150 freelance dispute — read [that playbook](/posts/how-to-file-small-claims-case-step-by-step/) for the full story) and won largely because the defendant's "soon" became "eleven weeks" against my "by November 1."

### 5. Warranties and disclaimers

What happens after delivery/completion? For contractors, a workmanship warranty of some duration (6 months, 1 year) is standard. For goods, whether there's an implied warranty of merchantability (which exists by default under UCC §2-314 unless disclaimed) — and if you're selling, whether you're disclaiming it and how.

### 6. Liability allocation, indemnity, and insurance

Who carries insurance, who indemnifies whom for third-party claims, and any dollar cap. For a small job, this is often overkill. For anything with meaningful risk — anything touching property, third parties, or professional advice — pay attention.

### 7. Termination and dispute resolution

How either party exits early, what's owed on exit, and where disputes get resolved. Include a **forum selection clause** (which state's law applies, where suit is filed) and, if you want to avoid court, an arbitration clause. If you're writing an arbitration clause, be careful — courts have been aggressively enforcing them, but badly drafted ones get thrown out. My rule of thumb: if you're writing arbitration, get a lawyer to review.

I'll add one more clause that isn't in every list but that has saved me twice: **entire agreement / integration clause** — "This document is the complete agreement between the parties and supersedes all prior negotiations and understandings." Without it, a creative opposing party will argue the handshake included a verbal warranty you didn't write down.

## A Simple Contract Template I Actually Use

Here's the working skeleton I use for most small-dollar services and goods transactions. It is not tailored to any state; treat it as a starting draft.

# simple-contract.md — my current working template (v4, updated 2026-04)

--- FRONT MATTER ---
Contract Title: [Goods/Services Agreement]
Effective Date: [DATE]
Parties:
  Provider: [Full legal name], [Entity if any], [Address]
  Client:   [Full legal name], [Entity if any], [Address]

--- 1. SCOPE ---
Provider will perform the following work / deliver the following goods:
  - [Item 1]
  - [Item 2]
Explicitly excluded from this agreement:
  - [Exclusion 1]
  - [Exclusion 2]

--- 2. PRICE AND PAYMENT ---
Total: $[AMOUNT]
Schedule: [e.g., 50% on signing, 50% on delivery]
Accepted payment methods: [ACH / check / etc.]
Late fees: [X]% per month, or the maximum allowed by law, whichever is lower.

--- 3. TIMELINE ---
Start: [DATE].  Completion: [DATE].
Milestones: [if any, list dates]

--- 4. CHANGES ---
Any change to scope, price, or timeline requires a written change order
signed by both parties. Verbal changes are not binding.

--- 5. WARRANTY ---
Provider warrants workmanship for [X] months from completion.
[For goods] Provider disclaims all implied warranties to the extent
permitted by law.

--- 6. INSURANCE, INDEMNITY, LIABILITY ---
Provider will maintain [general liability insurance / etc.].
Each party indemnifies the other against third-party claims arising
from its own negligence or breach.
Liability cap: [if any]

--- 7. TERMINATION ---
Either party may terminate for material breach if the breach is not
cured within [X] days of written notice.
On termination, [what's owed / what's returned].

--- 8. GOVERNING LAW AND VENUE ---
This agreement is governed by the laws of [STATE].
Exclusive venue for disputes: [County, State].

--- 9. ENTIRE AGREEMENT ---
This document is the entire agreement and supersedes all prior
discussions. No other terms apply.

--- SIGNATURES ---
Provider: ________________  Date: ______
Client:   ________________  Date: ______

You can draft and clean up markdown offline — for quick iterations I run the draft through the [Markdown Editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/) to check formatting and see the rendered layout before I export to PDF. That's not strictly legal work, but a contract that looks like a contract gets signed more readily.

A few drafting choices in that template worth calling out:

- I write dollar amounts in both digits and words. Sounds archaic, but I've seen at least one dispute where "14,000" vs "1,4OO" became a problem.
- I never leave a blank for price. Fill in "-0-" or strike through the line; an empty line invites arguments.
- I avoid incorporating external terms by reference ("per our prior email") unless I attach the email. If a judge can't see it, it doesn't exist.

## Should You Use an Online Template Service?

I tested the free tiers of several over the past year, mostly to see whether the boilerplate was worth the money. Here's my read as of October 2026:

| Service | Free tier | Paid tier | Best for | My caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rocket Lawyer | Trial, then paid | ~$39.99/mo (bundle) | Ready-made clauses, doc review upsell | Bundles legal advice at higher tiers; you mostly want the plain doc |
| LawDepot | Free trial | ~$35/mo | Broad library, clean output | Subscription — cancel is buried |
| LegalTemplates.net | Fully free templates | N/A | Quick draft, no account | Quality is uneven; check state specificity |
| PandaDoc free tier | Free for 1 user, limited docs/mo | From ~$49/user/mo | Sending, signing, tracking | Overkill for one-off deals |
| Google Docs + own template | Free | N/A | Full control | You need to actually know what to write |

Two things I noticed testing the tools in February and again in October 2026:

First, **almost none of them generate truly state-specific language on the free tier.** A California-leaning template hit my state's requirements roughly for scope and payment, but badly missed the statutory language required for residential construction contracts (California Business & Professions Code §7159 requires specific notices and a 3-day right to cancel that none of the free templates included). If your deal touches a regulated category — construction, real estate, employment, lending — don't trust the generic template.

Second, the "e-signature included" selling point matters less than you'd think for most small deals. Under the federal E-SIGN Act (2000) and the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, adopted in 49 states (New York has its own equivalent, the ESRA), a signed PDF sent by email is a valid signature for most contracts. I've used a printed-and-scanned PDF for lending agreements and never had a challenge.

For anything that resembles a *business structure* rather than a one-off transaction — partnership, LLC operating agreement, distribution deal — a generic template is the wrong starting point. I wrote about [the partnership agreement shape that actually works](/posts/how-to-create-a-simple-business-partnership-agreement/) in a separate post, and the frameworks differ substantially.

## Where the DIY Contract Breaks Down

I promised caveats, so here they are. Six situations where I'd recommend hiring a lawyer even at $300–$500/hour, because the cost of a bad draft is worse than the fee.

1. **Anything touching real property.** Deeds, leases, easements, purchase agreements. Real estate statutes are dense and hyper-specific. Even [my rental agreement article](/posts/write-simple-rental-agreement/) — which walks you through a solid basic lease — recommends a state-specific form for anything beyond a bare-bones month-to-month.
2. **Family, estate, and gift matters.** Will contests and probate disputes are expensive in a way that makes a $1,500 attorney review look cheap. If you're formalizing a deal with a relative, at minimum pay for a review.
3. **Anything with an arbitration clause you're writing yourself.** Bad arbitration clauses get litigated more than the underlying dispute.
4. **Employment and contractor classification.** The line between employee and independent contractor is regulated by state ABC tests and federal rules (the DOL's 2024 independent contractor rule, revised again in 2025, is the current federal baseline) — a DIY contract cannot fix a misclassification problem. You cannot contract your way around it.
5. **Lending money with interest.** Usury laws cap interest rates state by state, and writing an over-cap rate can void the loan *and* subject you to penalties. I explored [the actual per-state interest rate picture](/posts/legal-interest-rates-on-loans-explained/) and the range is wild.
6. **Anything with a dollar value where a bad outcome would materially change your life.** My rough threshold: if the amount exceeds ~2% of your annual income, pay for review.

Also worth noting: DIY contracts won't magically fix a bad deal. If the underlying agreement is set up to screw you, a well-drafted contract just documents your screwing more clearly. The contract is a lock, not a safe.

## The Practical Workflow: Signing the Deal Without Wrecking the Relationship

Here's the exact sequence I've run for the past several deals. It's designed to avoid the most common failure mode: the other party feeling like you don't trust them.

**Step 1 — Send a "deal summary" the same day.** Not a contract, a one-page email bullet list: "Per our conversation today, here's what I understood..." This costs nothing and gets you the first piece of evidence. If they reply "yes," you've already got the deal documented in writing.

**Step 2 — Draft from that email.** Take the confirmed summary, drop it into your template, fill in the blanks. This is where I run the doc through the [Word Counter](https://word-counter.search123.top/) to make sure I'm not accidentally at 11 pages — long contracts sneak in extra terms nobody reads.

**Step 3 — Send for review with a clear ask.** "Here's a draft based on our call. Take a look — anything I got wrong, tell me and I'll fix it. If it's clean, sign and send back." No deadline pressure. Give them at least 48 hours. I've found that rushing signatures produces exactly the resentment you were trying to avoid.

**Step 4 — Sign and date every page (or initial each).** For multi-page docs, initial the bottom of each page along with page numbers ("Page 3 of 7"). This makes it much harder to argue pages were swapped.

**Step 5 — Store three ways.** PDF copy, emailed to yourself, plus a cloud folder with a timestamped filename ("2026-11-06-roofer-final-v3.pdf"). If the contract concerns anything that may end up in litigation, save the original email thread with headers — the metadata matters.

**Step 6 — Calendar the review points.** If the contract has milestones or a warranty period, put a calendar entry on the review date. I've missed warranty expiration windows because I didn't do this. Twice.

A note on sending "for signature" — for a one-off deal with a small business, don't overthink the tooling. Print, sign, scan, email. Everyone knows how to do it, nobody wonders whether their e-sign tool worked, and the resulting PDF is accepted in every state I've checked.

## When the Handshake Deal Goes Sideways

Even a good contract fails, and it usually fails quietly — the other side goes dark, delivers late, or excuses themselves from a term. My first move (learned the hard way) is always a **written demand letter** delivered by a method with proof of receipt (certified mail plus email). The [demand letter playbook](/posts/how-to-write-demand-letter/) walks through structure and typical response rates; in my experience, roughly half of well-posed demand letters resolve a dispute without further escalation.

If that fails, small claims court is the natural next step and the natural defendant is the one who owed you money. Make sure you're within the venue and dollar limits — my [small claims limits breakdown](/posts/small-claims-court-sue-guide/) has the state-by-state picture, and the dollar cap has moved up in several states in the last few years (California is now $12,500 for individuals as of 2024, for instance). Bring the signed contract, the email summary, the emails, and a one-page timeline.

And if the deal involves someone you already wish you hadn't signed anything with — a contractor, say, or a landlord — be careful about piling up related claims without advice. The contract is your leverage; don't spend it on a poorly organized complaint. My [contractor dispute write-up](/posts/how-to-handle-contractor-dispute/) covers the sequencing that kept me out of trouble twice.

## The Version of This Article I'd Wish I'd Had

If you take one thing, take this: **formalize the deal the day you shake on it, not the day things go wrong.** Contracts are not about distrust. They're about memory. The people who lost in my small claims tracking were never people who'd been lied to at the outset — they were people who'd been told one thing, and the other side had remembered something different, and the difference had finally become expensive.

You don't need a lawyer for most small transactions. You need a template, a one-page email confirming the deal, a signature, and a calendar reminder. The [freelancer contract clause guide](/posts/freelancer-contract-key-clauses/) and the [general pre-signing contract checklist](/posts/read-contract-before-signing/) cover the reading side once you've got something drafted. What you write today, notarized and dated, will be worth vastly more than the handshake that came before it — even if that handshake felt like the whole deal at the time.
