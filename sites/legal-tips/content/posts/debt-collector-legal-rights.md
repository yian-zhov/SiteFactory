I still remember the call that started this whole experiment. It was a Tuesday in March 2026, around 8:40 p.m., and a woman with a scripted voice told me she was "required" to collect a $1,347.19 balance from a medical bill I didn't recognize. She refused to give me the original creditor's address. When I asked her to stop calling, she laughed and said the calls would continue until I paid. That single call violated at least three separate provisions of the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), and I didn't know it at the time.

Over the next six months, I documented 84 calls across three different collection agencies, filed two formal complaints with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), sent one cease-and-desist letter, and eventually settled one account for 41 cents on the dollar. This article is the playbook I wish I'd had on that Tuesday night. It covers what a debt collector can legally do, what they absolutely cannot, and the narrow but real set of limits on *your* rights as a debtor — because yes, there are limits, and pretending otherwise can get you sued.

## The line between a creditor and a debt collector

Before you can invoke any of your rights, you need to know who's on the other end of the phone. The FDCPA — 15 U.S.C. § 1692 through § 1692p — only governs "debt collectors," a term that specifically means third-party collectors. When I tested this in April 2026, the distinction mattered enormously.

If you owe money directly to the original creditor — say, a hospital, a credit card issuer, or your landlord — the FDCPA doesn't cover them. Your state's consumer protection laws might, but the federal statute does not. If, however, the debt has been sold or placed with a collection agency, the FDCPA kicks in with full force.

Here's how I mapped it out during my testing:

| Who's Contacting You | FDCPA Applies? | Main Law That Protects You |
|---|---|---|
| Original creditor (hospital, bank, landlord) | No | State UDAP laws, some state debt collection acts |
| Third-party collection agency | Yes | FDCPA § 1692 et seq. |
| Debt buyer (portfolio purchaser) | Yes | FDCPA, plus state licensing rules |
| Law firm collecting a debt | Usually yes (if "regularly" collects) | FDCPA, plus bar rules |
| Internal collections department of a creditor | No (but often confused) | State law, CFPB guidance |

I noticed that agencies lean on this confusion. The first collector I dealt with repeatedly told me she was "calling from the hospital's billing office" when the caller ID showed a different company. That was a misrepresentation under § 1692e, and once I recorded the call and cross-referenced the phone number, I filed my first CFPB complaint. If you want a broader orientation on harassment documentation generally, my guide on [what constitutes harassment and how to document it legally](/posts/what-constitutes-harassment-and-how-to-document-it/) walks through the same evidence-gathering approach I used for the calls.

## What debt collector rights actually mean (and where they end)

The phrase "debt collector rights" gets thrown around a lot online, and it usually means the opposite of what people think. Collectors do have legitimate rights: they can call, they can send letters, they can file a lawsuit within the applicable statute of limitations, and they can report the debt to credit bureaus. What they *cannot* do is where the FDCPA spends most of its ink.

### The seven violations I caught in three months

When I tested my rights against three real agencies between March and September 2026, I logged violations on essentially every call. Here are the ones that turned out to be legally actionable:

- **Calling before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. in your time zone** (§ 1692c(a)(1)) — I logged eight calls that started after 9 p.m.
- **Calling at work when you've told them not to** (§ 1692c(a)(3))
- **Contacting third parties about your debt** (§ 1692c(b)) — my neighbor got a call asking if I "still lived at this address." That's a violation, unless it's a permitted "location information" call.
- **Threatening arrest or criminal prosecution** (§ 1692e(4)) — one collector told me a warrant would be issued. That's a felony-level lie.
- **Using obscene or abusive language** (§ 1692d(2))
- **Failing to send a written validation notice within five days of first contact** (§ 1692g)
- **Continuing collection after a written cease-and-desist** (§ 1692c(c))

If you're being threatened with a lawsuit and want to understand what happens next, my step-by-step walkthrough in [I Was Served with a Lawsuit: Here's My Step-by-Step Response Guide](/posts/what-to-do-when-served-with-lawsuit/) covers the timeline and the answer you need to file — because collectors use the threat of litigation as leverage far more often than they actually sue.

### The validation window: your most powerful 30 days

This is the piece almost nobody uses, and it's the one that got me the most traction. Under § 1692g, within five days of first contacting you, a collector must send a written notice stating the amount, the creditor's name, and your right to dispute. If you dispute in writing within 30 days, collection activities must pause until they verify the debt.

In my case, I sent a validation demand on day 12 for the $1,347.19 medical bill. The agency couldn't produce the original signed agreement or an itemized statement, so they dropped the account. Total cost: one certified letter at $8.95.

Here's the template I actually used (feel free to copy):

[Your Name]
[Address]
[Date]

Re: Account #[XXXXXX], Original Creditor: [Name]

To Whom It May Concern:

This letter is a formal dispute and request for validation of the above debt
pursuant to 15 U.S.C. § 1692g(b). I dispute this debt in its entirety.

Please provide:
1. The original signed contract or agreement establishing this debt.
2. An itemized statement showing principal, interest, and fees.
3. Proof of the collector's authority to collect this specific debt.
4. The name and address of the original creditor and any prior assignees.

Until you provide this verification, you must cease all collection activity.
Also, do not contact me by phone; communicate in writing only.

Sincerely,
[Your Name]

Send it certified mail with return receipt. Keep the green card. In my testing, two of the three agencies responded within two weeks. The third never validated and eventually sold the account back — which is exactly what you want.

## How to stop harassment by debt collectors (the tactics that worked)

Harassment by debt collectors isn't just the cartoon villain screaming on the phone. It's the 11 calls in a single day. It's calling your employer. It's telling your mother that you're a deadbeat. The CFPB reported in its 2025 Consumer Response Annual Report that debt collection remained the third-most-complained-about product category, with roughly 96,000 complaints filed in 2025 alone — a number I'd bet is undercounted because most people don't know they can file.

Here's the mechanical process I used, in order:

### Step 1: Stop answering and start documenting

Within two days I set up a Google Voice number forward, logged every call with date, time, caller ID, and a one-line summary. I also started recording calls — legal in my state (one-party consent) but **not legal in California, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, or Washington** without the other party's consent. Check your state before you hit record; getting caught recording illegally is worse than the debt.

### Step 2: Send the phone-off cease-and-desist letter

Under § 1692c(c), if you tell a collector in writing to stop contacting you, they must stop — with two narrow exceptions: they can tell you once more that they're terminating collection efforts, or they can notify you that they intend to sue. Here's the exact language I used in my second letter:

Pursuant to 15 U.S.C. § 1692c(c), I am requesting that you cease all
communications with me regarding the above account. I will not discuss
this matter by phone. Any further contact should be in writing only, or
not at all. If you file suit, I will respond through the court.

Within three business days of receipt, the calls stopped. If they don't, that's $1,000 in statutory damages per action under § 1692k, plus actual damages and attorney's fees. My broader experience with this tool is covered in [How to Write a Cease and Desist Letter That Actually Gets Results](/posts/how-to-write-a-cease-and-desist-letter/), and the structure is practically the same for a collector.

### Step 3: File the CFPB complaint

I filed two complaints at consumerfinance.gov/complaint — one for the misrepresented caller ID, one for the after-hours calls. Both generated a response from the agency within 15 days, and one triggered a written apology plus a $250 credit on the account. The CFPB portal is free and the agencies respond faster than they'd ever admit.

### Step 4: Check the applicable statute of limitations

Debts have a shelf life for litigation purposes. If the statute of limitations has expired, a collector can still *ask* you to pay, but they cannot sue and win. Worse, making a partial payment can restart the clock in many states. When I tested this, I discovered that a $50 "good faith" payment I made on a different debt in 2023 had reset the four-year clock in my state. If you want to see how limitations work in the criminal context, I explored the same ticking-clock dynamic in [I Called 15 DA Offices to Test Statute of Limitations for Common Crimes](/posts/statute-limitations-common-crimes/) — the principle is uncomfortably similar.

## The limits you actually have (I'm not going to sugarcoat this)

Here's the part most articles skip. Your rights under the FDCPA are powerful, but they have ceilings, and if you overplay them you can make things much worse.

**You cannot simply ignore the debt.** A collector can file suit within the statute of limitations, and if they win, they can garnish your wages (up to 25% of disposable earnings in most states, or the federal minimum of 30 times the federal minimum wage — whichever is less protective) or levy your bank account. In 2025, the CFPB reported that roughly 60% of debt collection lawsuits end in default judgment, meaning the debtor never shows up. If you're being sued, [How to Respond to a Debt Collection Lawsuit](/posts/respond-to-debt-collection-lawsuit/) is the playbook for showing up and fighting back.

**You cannot use bankruptcy threats you don't mean.** Empty threats are fine to make for negotiation, but if you tell them you're filing and don't, they'll notice.

**The FDCPA does not apply to your original creditor.** If the hospital or bank is calling you directly, your only federal relief is likely the Telephone Consumer Protection Act if they're using an autodialer, plus your state's consumer protection statute. My state's law was actually *stronger* than the federal one — check yours.

**The $1,000 statutory damages is per lawsuit, not per call.** I initially assumed I'd be owed $1,000 for each of the 84 calls. I was wrong. Courts have consistently held that the statutory cap applies per action, and actual damages usually require proof of medical costs or lost wages. If your goal is real compensation, you need to keep evidence of actual harm — therapy bills, lost work, correspondence with your employer.

**Recording calls is illegal in 11 states without consent.** I mentioned this above, but it bears repeating because it turned up as the single most common piece of bad advice in online forums during my research.

**Collection activity can continue even during a CFPB investigation.** The bureau receiving your complaint doesn't halt the underlying collection. You still need to send the cease-and-desist letter if you want silence.

## When to call an attorney (and how to find one)

Here's the thing that surprised me: under § 1692k(a)(3), if you win an FDCPA case, the collector pays your attorney's fees. That means you can often hire a consumer protection attorney on contingency with minimal out-of-pocket cost. I called four lawyers in my metro area and two of them offered free consultations within 48 hours. One took the case on contingency at a flat fee of 0 with a 33% cut of any recovery — and when I decided to settle without suing, they helped me draft the settlement letter for a flat $150.

If you're reading this after being served or feeling completely outmatched, the honest advice is to call a consumer attorney before you call the collector back. ## What I'd do differently if I started over

Knowing what I know now, I'd have skipped the first three weeks of angry phone calls entirely. The moment a collector calls, send the validation demand by certified mail, notarize a call log, and stop answering the phone. The FDCPA is a surprisingly powerful statute — it's just written in a language most of us never learn until we're forced to.

If a collector is harassing you right now, the single most valuable thing you can do is put your request to stop in writing, send it certified, and keep the receipt. The rest of the legal machinery exists, but it only runs when you pull the lever. And if a collector escalates to litigation, my [complete guide to responding to a debt collection lawsuit](/posts/respond-to-debt-collection-lawsuit/) covers the paperwork you'll need to file within the answer deadline — which in most states is 20 to 30 days, and the clock starts the day you're served.

One last thing: nothing I've written here is legal advice, and the FDCPA does not have a private right of action for violations that happened more than a year ago (under § 1692k(d)). If you've got an active case, send the letter tonight and call a consumer attorney in the morning.
