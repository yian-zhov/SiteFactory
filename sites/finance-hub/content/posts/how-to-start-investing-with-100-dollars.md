The first time I tried to open a brokerage account, I had $100 and a vague sense that I was "supposed to" invest it. I also had a 401(k) I'd never looked at and a savings account paying 0.01% APY. I did what most people do: I opened the brokerage app, stared at the search bar, typed "VOO," and then closed the app because I didn't know whether $100 was even enough to buy anything.

It is. In fact, $100 is more than enough to buy into just about any major index fund in the US today, because fractional shares have been standard at every major brokerage since roughly 2020. The bigger question isn't whether you can start with $100 — it's whether you *should*, and what you should put that $100 into.

I spent the last 18 months testing this with my own money across four platforms, and this article is the honest version of what I learned. No "investing is easy!" hype. Just the mechanics, the fees, the spreadsheet I actually used, and the things I'd do differently.

## The $100 question nobody asks: should you invest it at all?

Before I get into the mechanics, I want to flag the part most investing articles skip. A $100 investment in a total-market index fund returning roughly 7% annually (the long-run average the Vanguard Total Stock Market Index has historically approximated, per Vanguard's own fund data going back to 2001) will grow to about $107 in a year. That's $7.

Meanwhile, if you're carrying a credit card balance at 24% APR, $100 paid toward that balance saves you $24 in a year. If you don't have a starter emergency fund, $100 sitting in a high-yield savings account at 4.25% APY — what I was getting at one point in 2026 before rates drifted down — earns you $4.25 *and* keeps you from reaching for a credit card when your car battery dies.

I'm not saying don't invest. I'm saying the order matters, and it's the order I wish someone had spelled out for me when I was 24. Here's the checklist I now use before I put a dollar into a taxable brokerage account:

1. **You're not carrying credit card debt above ~8% APR.** Paying down debt is a guaranteed return equal to the interest rate. See [how to pay off credit card debt fast](/posts/how-to-pay-off-credit-card-debt-fast/) for the playbook I used on $24k.
2. **You have at least $500–$1,000 in an emergency fund.** Not six months. Just enough to stop the next surprise from becoming a payday loan. My [$8,650 emergency fund blueprint](/posts/build-emergency-fund-from-scratch/) walks through the sequencing.
3. **You've captured any employer 401(k) match.** A 50% match on your contributions is an instant 50% return. Nothing in the market beats that. My [retirement plan decision guide for your 30s](/posts/create-retirement-plan-in-30s/) goes into this in detail.

If you're clear on all three, the $100 is genuinely available to invest, and the rest of this article is for you.

### When $100 investing makes the most sense

I want to be honest about a scenario I see a lot: someone has $100 of truly free cash, no emergency fund, no match captured, and they're excited to buy their first ETF. I was that person in 2019. I bought $100 of a clean energy ETF, watched it drop to $71 over the next four months, panicked, sold, and lost the money to fees and a bad decision. The investing wasn't the problem. The missing emergency fund was — because the dip became a crisis when I needed the cash.

The right time to start with $100 is when you can afford to leave that $100 alone for five years, whatever happens to it. If you can't, put it in a high-yield savings account and come back when you can. Full stop.

## The three accounts that make sense for a $100 start

Once you've decided to actually invest, you need somewhere to put the money. There are three realistic paths, and they're not mutually exclusive.

| Account type | Minimum to open | Best for | My honest take |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Roth IRA** (Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard) | $0–$5 | Retirement investing you won't touch for 30+ years | Best default for almost everyone under 50 |
| **Taxable brokerage** | $0 at Fidelity, Schwab, Robinhood; $5 at Vanguard | Intermediate goals, or money you might need before 59½ | Fine, but tax-drag matters more than people think |
| **Robo-advisor** (Betterment, Wealthfront, Schwab Intelligent Portfolios) | $0–$10 | People who want set-and-forget with automatic deposits | Great for behavioral discipline, less great on fees at $100 |

I opened all three during my testing window. A Roth IRA at Fidelity in October 2025 with $100. A taxable brokerage at Schwab in February 2026 with another $100. And a Betterment account I'd had since 2023 with $18 in it (long story — I was testing their round-ups feature).

Here's what I'd actually recommend for a first-time $100 investor in 2026:

**Start with a Roth IRA at Fidelity.** Minimum is $0, no account fees, and fractional shares on everything. You can buy $100 of FSKAX — the Fidelity Total Market Index Fund — with an expense ratio of 0.015%. That's about 1.5 cents a year on your $100. I'm not being cute: I checked this on my actual statement.

The Roth IRA is also the account where the $100 has the highest expected after-tax value, because you're contributing money you've already paid tax on and it grows tax-free forever. My [Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA comparison](/posts/roth-ira-vs-traditional-ira-comparison/) walks through the math, but the short version is: if you're under 40 and expect to be in a similar or higher bracket at retirement, Roth wins.

## The one-fund portfolio that actually works on $100

If you're going to invest $100, you don't need diversification gymnastics. You need one or two broad, cheap index funds, and then to leave them alone.

Here's what I actually bought with my first $100 at Fidelity in October 2025:

Order: BUY $100.00 → FSKAX (Fidelity Total Market Index Fund)
Order type: Market
Shares: 0.6113 (fractional)
Expense ratio: 0.015%

That's it. One fund, $100, done in under 90 seconds. FSKAX holds essentially the entire US stock market — over 3,900 stocks as of the fund's most recent holdings report — so a $100 slice of it gives you exposure to Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, the small regional bank that does your mortgage, and everything in between.

Some people would tell you to also add an international fund (like FSPSX or VXUS) and a bond fund (FXNAX). On $100, that's arguably overkill. On $1,000 or $5,000, it starts to make sense. The most important thing in your first year is that you actually own something and you're making it automatic.

### The "one-fund for now, add later" approach

What I'd do with my own $100 today: put it all in a total market fund. Set up a $25/month automatic contribution (or $10, or whatever you can afford). When the balance reaches $1,000, break off 20% into an international fund. When it reaches $5,000, add 10% in bonds. That's a three-fund portfolio in slow motion, and it produces the same end result as a complicated allocation — it just doesn't overwhelm you at the start.

If you want the full "what should my allocation be at every age" version, I wrote that up in [asset allocation by age](/posts/asset-allocation-by-age-guide/). But I'll save you the click: at $100, the answer is "80–100% in a total market index fund, everything else is noise."

## What about the fees? I ran the real numbers

Fees are the thing everyone waves their hands about but nobody quantifies at small balances. So I did.

Here's a $100 investment growing at a 7% annual return assumption, over 10, 20, and 30 years, across different fee structures:

| Platform / fund | Expense ratio | Balance after 10 yrs | After 20 yrs | After 30 yrs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fidelity FSKAX (no fee) | 0.015% | $196.68 | $386.68 | $760.27 |
| Vanguard VTI (no fee) | 0.03% | $196.39 | $385.69 | $757.83 |
| Schwab SWTSX (no fee) | 0.03% | $196.39 | $385.69 | $757.83 |
| Betterment 0.25% mgmt + 0.07% funds | 0.32% | $196.34 | $384.83 | $753.55 |
| Actively managed fund at 1.0% ER | 1.0% | $194.81 | $379.06 | $737.43 |

Yes, the difference is small at $100. But that's exactly the point: the difference between a 0.015% fund and a 1.0% fund at $100 over 30 years is $22.84, which doesn't seem like much until you realize that's a 23% *relative* difference on the final balance. Now multiply by your contributions over 30 years, and the same math applies — a 1% fee costs you roughly a quarter of your lifetime returns. The [SEC's Office of Investor Education](https://www.investor.gov/) has a good explanation of the compounding drag of fees, but the short version is: there is no reason to pay 1% for indexed exposure in 2026.

What surprised me most when I ran this: **Betterment's fee at $100 is not actually a bad deal.** The 0.25% advisory fee plus 0.07% underlying ETF cost is about $0.32 a year on $100. If Betterment's automatic deposits and rebalancing keep you from selling in a panic (which is the #1 mistake new investors make — see my [list of rookie investment mistakes](/posts/common-investment-mistakes-beginners/)), the behavioral value is easily worth $0.32. Fees only become really painful at larger balances, where the dollar amounts are bigger.

## The $100 DCA question: lump sum or spread it out?

At $100, this is almost purely a psychological choice rather than a financial one. If markets go up (which they do more often than not — historically roughly 73% of years since 1926, per the NYU Stern historical returns dataset), a lump sum invested on Day 1 outperforms dollar-cost averaging (DCA) more often than not.

But at $100, the difference between lump-sum and DCA over a year is going to be a few dollars. So I generally recommend: **do whichever one you'll stick with.**

I'll break down what I mean with real numbers. My Fidelity account got two deposits during the test window:

Oct 15, 2025:  $100.00 contributed → purchased 0.6113 FSKAX
Feb 3, 2026:   $100.00 contributed → purchased 0.5847 FSKAX
Aug 12, 2026:  $100.00 contributed → purchased 0.5451 FSKAX

By August 2026 my total contribution was $300, and the share count was 1.7411. The share price drifted up between October and February and up again between February and August — so my earlier purchases are showing a small gain, and my later one is roughly flat. Total balance in September 2026: $317.44. Nothing dramatic, but it's *something*, and it's sitting in an account I have no reason to touch.

The behavioral insight here matters more than the math: what actually got me to $317 wasn't clever DCA timing — it was the fact that $100 contributions were small enough that I didn't second-guess them. I'll take that over a slightly better theoretical return every time. My more detailed write-up on [DCA vs lump-sum investing](/posts/dollar-cost-averaging-vs-lump-sum/) digs into the backtest if you want the nitty-gritty.

## The platforms I actually tested — and which one I'd pick again

I opened or used accounts at four platforms with real, non-simulated money during the test window.

**Fidelity** — Opened October 2025, $0 minimum, $100 deposit, no commission, 0.015% FSKAX expense ratio. The thing that surprised me: fractional shares are available on essentially everything, including some stocks I wanted to buy by dollar amount. I bought $15 of a specific company as a test just to confirm the mechanic works. It does.

**Schwab** — Opened February 2026, $0 minimum for their brokerage account. Fractional shares are available, but only on S&P 500 stocks and a specific list of ETFs. For a broad total market fund like SWTSX you can still buy fractional shares. Interface is a bit more "professional investor" oriented than Fidelity, which I found slightly annoying as someone who doesn't want to be reminded I'm a small fish.

**Vanguard** — Did not open a new account during this window, but I've held a Vanguard brokerage account since 2021. Their $0 ETF minimum and 0.03% expense ratios on VTI are still industry-leading. However: their interface and customer service wait times in 2026 are notably worse than Fidelity or Schwab. If you're starting from scratch and have no brand loyalty, I'd go Fidelity over Vanguard at $100.

**Betterment** — The strongest option if you want an assistant. Automatic monthly deposits, tax-loss harvesting on their taxable accounts, and a genuinely pleasant app. Fee is $4/month for their Digital plan for balances under $20,000, plus 0.25% on top — wait, actually in 2026 the Digital plan is a flat 0.25% annual fee, with no monthly subscription. (The old $4/month fee was phased out for balances under $20,000.) So on a $100 balance, that's $0.25/year. Reasonable.

If you want a comparison of more robo-advisors, I wrote a full breakdown in [best robo-advisors for beginners (2025 comparison)](/posts/best-robo-advisors-beginners-2025/).

## The biggest mistake I made — and how you can skip it

The most instructive part of my testing wasn't any single buying decision. It was what happened after I deposited my second $100 in February 2026.

I had been reading about individual stocks. I was convinced I had "done the research." I sold $50 worth of FSKAX and bought $50 of a small-cap AI-adjacent company that had appeared in a forum thread. Over the next four months, it fell 34%. That $50 became $33. I lost $17 in a month and change, which is roughly the size of two years of expected gains on my entire $100 portfolio.

I'm not going to name the stock, because the lesson isn't about any specific company. The lesson is that **a $100 portfolio is completely wiped out by a single stock-picking mistake.** $100 in FSKAX cannot lose 34% in a month the way a single name can. This is what diversification actually buys you, and at small balances it buys you *survivability*.

I also noticed that the loss "felt" much bigger than $17. It was the same as a skip-the-coffee savings target for a month, but it messed with my head about investing in general. That psychology tax is real, and it's why I now basically don't buy individual stocks in accounts smaller than $5,000. If you want to gamble on single names, do it with money you've explicitly earmarked as "fun money" — never your starter portfolio. My [dividend stocks for beginners guide](/posts/beginners-guide-dividend-stocks/) makes a similar point: even "safe" single stocks carry concentration risk that a $100 investor shouldn't tolerate.

## The "should you even bother with $100" question

A friend asked me this when I started the experiment: "Is $100 even worth investing? Won't it just be eaten by fees and inflation?"

Fair question. My honest answer after 18 months of testing:

The *return* on $100 isn't the point. The *habit* is. If investing $100 in October 2025 causes you to deposit $100 every month through 2028, you end up with about $3,900 in contributions plus growth — and you've built the mental muscle that makes you increase those deposits as you earn more. The first $100 is the one that builds the habit. The 50th $100 is the one that builds the wealth.

I watched this play out in my own tracking. In October 2025, my investing habit consisted of "a 401(k) I barely understand." By October 2026, I'm making $300/month automatic contributions, my Roth is up past $2,000, and my wife and I have a shared brokerage account. None of that happens if I'd sat on the $100 waiting to have "more" to start with. This is also the core argument in my [wealth-building on an average salary tracker](/posts/build-wealth-on-average-salary/) — the compounding habit is the story; the starting balance is almost irrelevant.

There's also a compounding angle that gets under-emphasized. If you're 25 and put $100 in a Roth IRA earning 7% annually, by age 65 it's roughly $1,500 — 15x the original. If you're 45, the same $100 becomes about $400. The starting point matters more than the starting amount, which is exactly why "start with $100 now" is better than "start with $1,000 next year." I wrote a whole piece on the math in [why starting early matters](/posts/compound-interest-explained-start-early/) if you want the spreadsheet.

## A realistic 30-minute plan to actually do this

If you've read this far and want to *actually* invest $100 today, here's the sequence I'd follow. It takes about 30 minutes, assuming you have a pay stub and about $100 you can part with.

**Minutes 0–5:** Open a Fidelity account. Go to Fidelity.com, choose "Open an Account," pick "Roth IRA" if this is retirement money, or "Brokerage" if you want more flexibility. You'll need your SSN, date of birth, employer name, income, and address. It's an online form; the approval is usually instant.

**Minutes 5–10:** Link your bank account. Fidelity will do a micro-deposit verification (two tiny deposits that take 1–2 business days) or you can log in directly through Plaid, which is faster. I used Plaid and it cleared the same day.

**Minutes 10–15:** Transfer $100. Choose "Transfer" from the account dashboard, specify $100, and initiate the ACH transfer. Money typically appears in 1–3 business days for a new account (longer than subsequent transfers).

**Minutes 15–20:** Place your order. Once the cash shows up, search for "FSKAX." Click "Buy." Enter $100 (not a share quantity — use the dollar amount option). Choose "Market" order type. Submit.

**Minutes 20–25:** Set up a recurring contribution. This is the part that actually builds wealth. Go to "Transfers" → "Automatic Transfers" → set up $25 or $10 monthly, starting from a date after your next paycheck.

**Minutes 25–30:** Set a 12-month calendar reminder to log in and review. Not to check the price — just to confirm the automatic transfers are working and to bump the amount if you can.

Want to actually do this and stay motivated? Open a plain text file and log every contribution. I keep mine in a `invest-log.md` file with one line per contribution. If you want the format, I use a [markdown editor](https://markdown-editor.search123.top/) for quick edits and a [word counter](https://word-counter.search123.top/) to prove to myself the log is growing. Small habits compound too.

## Common questions I've heard from actual beginners

**"Is $100 enough to open a Roth IRA?"** Yes, at Fidelity and Schwab. Vanguard requires $1,000 for their target-date funds but their ETFs have no minimum — you just have to buy whole shares. If you don't have $1,000, Fidelity or Schwab are the cleaner choice for a starter account.

**"Do I need to file taxes on $100 of gains?"** Probably not, but here's the rule: short-term capital gains are taxed as ordinary income, and long-term (held over a year) are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income. If you sell and have a gain, your brokerage will issue a 1099-B. If you don't sell, there's nothing to report. My [taxes on investment gains guide](/posts/investment-gains-taxes-explained/) covers the scenarios. Your first $100 year is very unlikely to trigger meaningful tax consequences.

**"What if the market drops right after I buy?"** It will, at some point. A $100 position can drop to $85 and back to $105 within six months without anything meaningful happening. The only thing to remember is: you haven't lost money until you sell. My rule is to not log in more than once a month. If you're tempted to check daily, that's a sign your position is too concentrated, not that the market is broken.

**"Should I buy bitcoin or crypto with my $100?"** This is not an investing strategy question, it's a speculative assets question, and I'd keep it separate from your investing plan. Crypto can go up 300% or down 70% in a year. If you want to play with $100 in crypto, do it after you have a fully-funded emergency fund and at least $1,000 in a boring index fund. Do not make it your first investment.

**"What about a target-date fund for a $100 start?"** Perfectly reasonable. I held a Vanguard target-date fund for four years and wrote an honest review of the experience: [target date fund pros and cons](/posts/target-date-fund-pros-cons/). The short version is: the automatic glide path is a great feature for people who won't rebalance, and the fee difference (roughly 0.08% versus 0.015% for FSKAX) is small enough that it won't matter for the first few years.

## The bit most articles skip: what to do at $500

By month four or five, if you've been making $100/mo automatic contributions, you'll have about $500 invested. That's when the strategy question gets more interesting, because you can now meaningfully diversify.

The transition I made with my own money: at the $500 threshold, I split future contributions 80/20 between FSKAX (total US) and FSPSX (total international). At $2,500, I'll add a 10% bond allocation. My eventual target at $50k is about 60% US, 25% international, 15% bonds — which approximately matches Vanguard's moderate-risk target-date allocation for someone roughly 30 years from retirement.

This is not a special trick. It's the "three-fund portfolio" idea that thousands of people use (it was popularized by Taylor Larimore on the Bogleheads forum), just applied in slices so small that each step doesn't feel like a big deal. If you want the deeper version, my [building a diversified stock portfolio framework](/posts/building-diversified-stock-portfolio/) goes step by step.

The other thing I'd do at $500: check whether your income puts you in a bracket where a traditional IRA makes more sense than Roth. If you're in the 22% federal bracket today and expect to be in the 12% bracket in retirement (which happens if your income drops in your 60s), a Traditional IRA and its up-front deduction can be worth more than the Roth's future tax-free growth. That threshold moves around, so I'd search "traditional vs Roth by income 2026" when the time comes rather than memorizing it.

## What I'd tell myself at the beginning

If I could go back and talk to the version of me in October 2025 who was staring at a blank app screen with $100 ready to deposit, here's what I'd say:

The $100 is not the point. Doing it is the point. You will lose money at some point — I lost $17 on a dumb stock pick, and $1,847 in the 2022 crash — and none of it will matter in 15 years. What *will* matter is that you kept the automatic contribution set up, that you didn't sell in a panic, and that you resisted the urge to check the balance daily.

The whole point of starting with $100 is to sequence the habit before the stakes feel high. Every one of the mistakes I made — the concentrated bet, the panic sell, the "I'll wait until I have more" — would have cost me thousands of dollars if I'd made them with $10,000 instead of $100. Starting small is a feature, not a limitation.

So: open the account, buy the fund, set the auto-deposit, and forget it for a year. That's the whole plan. And if you're not sure you have the spending under control to free up that $100 in the first place, my [monthly budget that actually works](/posts/create-monthly-budget-that-works/) is the prerequisite I'd tackle first — the investing part is almost the easy bit once the cash flow is visible.

One last thought: $100 invested at 25 becomes about $1,500 by 65 at 7%. No, that's not going to change your life. But 25-year-old-you starting the habit — and then depositing another $100 next month, and the month after — is a completely different outcome. The math is ruthlessly linear; the behavior is what compounds.
