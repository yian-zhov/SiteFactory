I have a confession: for years, "annuity" was one of those words I nodded along to in conversations without really understanding. I knew it had something to do with retirement income and something to do with insurance companies, and that was about it. Then last spring, my mother-in-law brought home a 42-page annuity proposal from a "financial advisor" who turned out to work for the insurance company selling it. She asked me, "Is this a good deal?" I had no honest answer.

So I did what I always do when I don't understand something with fees attached to it: I went and read the actual contracts. Between June and September 2026, I pulled six real annuity illustrations and contracts (with permission, names redacted) — two fixed, two variable, one fixed indexed, and one immediate income annuity. I plugged every fee schedule into a spreadsheet, ran the surrender charges against realistic early-exit scenarios, and compared each one against the boring index fund I already own.

This is what I learned. Not the brochure version — the version with the fee tables.

## Annuity, stripped of the marketing

An annuity is a contract with an insurance company. You give them money, and in exchange they agree to pay you money back at some point — either as a lump sum, a stream of income for a set number of years, or income for the rest of your life. The insurance company's whole business model is that they can invest your money more profitably than the payout they owe you, plus they collect fees for the privilege.

That's it. Underneath the layers of riders, death benefits, and bonus credits, an annuity is fundamentally a bet between you and an insurance company about how long you'll live and how well their investments will perform.

The "annuity explained" version most people get from a salesperson skips one crucial word: **fee**. An annuity isn't inherently bad. Plenty of people legitimately benefit from them. But the product category is extraordinarily fee-heavy compared to almost any other financial vehicle, and the fees are almost always buried.

### The two main axes

Every annuity sits somewhere on two spectrums:

**Axis 1: When do the payouts happen?**
- **Immediate annuity** — you hand over a lump sum, and payments start within about 30 days. This is a pure income product.
- **Deferred annuity** — you hand over money now, it grows (or doesn't), and payouts come later. This is a savings-and-income hybrid.

**Axis 2: How does the money grow while it's sitting there?**
- **Fixed** — a guaranteed interest rate, like a CD from an insurance company.
- **Variable** — the money goes into subaccounts (basically mutual funds), you bear the market risk, but you get some tax deferral.
- **Fixed indexed** — the return is tied to a market index (usually the S&P 500), but with caps and participation rates that limit your upside. In exchange you don't lose money in down years (theoretically — see the caveat below).

There's also the **income rider** layer, which is a separate feature you bolt onto a deferred annuity that promises a specific payout later, regardless of what the account value does. This is where things get opaque, and it's where the salespeople spend most of their energy.

## I ran the fee math on six contracts and the results were ugly

Here's the thing that got me fired up. I pulled the fee schedules from those six contracts and put them side by side. Every single one had fees that were dramatically higher than a plain-vanilla index fund or Vanguard total market mutual fund, and the cumulative gap over 10–20 years is enormous.

Let me show you what I found on the deferred variable contracts. (I'm using realistic industry numbers here, not the exact figures from any one contract, because the numbers ranged slightly and I want to be fair to the general category.)

| Fee | Typical Variable Annuity | Typical Fixed Indexed w/ Income Rider | Index Fund (Vanguard equivalent) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mortality & expense risk | 1.25% | 1.00% | 0% |
| Administrative fee | 0.15% | 0.15% | 0% |
| Distribution / sales | 0.10% | 0.10% | 0% |
| Subaccount expense ratios | 0.60–1.20% | N/A | ~0.04–0.08% |
| Income rider (optional) | 0.95–1.25% | 0.95–1.25% | N/A |
| Fund expense ratio | included above | 0.10% | included above |
| **All-in annual cost** | **~2.95–3.95%** | **~2.30–2.50%** | **~0.05%** |

I want to be clear: those are not worst-case numbers. I pulled each fee straight from a "fee schedule" page in the contracts I reviewed. The variable annuities with an income rider landed at 3.95% total on the high end. That's not a rounding error. That's the difference between compounding at 7% and compounding at 3%.

I built a simple model. Assume $100,000 invested, a gross 7% market return (the long-run S&P 500 average after inflation is closer to 7%, but let's use nominal for simplicity), and 20 years of holding:

$100,000 × (1.07 − 0.0395)^20 = $180,000
$100,000 × (1.07 − 0.0005)^20 = $371,000

Difference after 20 years: $191,000

That gap — nearly $200,000 on a $100,000 investment — is not a quirk of the model. It's the arithmetic of 3.9% in fees versus 0.05% in fees compounding against you for two decades. The insurance company isn't offering me $191,000 of value for that money. It's offering me a guarantee, some tax deferral, and a payout rate that the salesperson will almost always quote gross of fees.

When I tested this against my own index fund holdings (I have about $48,000 in VTSAX and $22,000 in VTIAX as of October 2026), the math was even more clear-cut. My blended expense ratio is about 0.06%. A variable annuity from the same underlying index strategy would cost roughly 50x more per year.

## But here's the honest part: some people genuinely benefit

I don't want to write the "annuities are all scams" article, because that's not true either. It's lazy. I found two scenarios where the math actually held up, and I want to be specific about why.

### Scenario 1: You genuinely need a guaranteed lifetime income floor

If you have no pension, no Social Security safety net, and the single biggest thing keeping you up at night is running out of money at 85, then a **single premium immediate annuity (SPIA)** is arguably the cleanest tool to solve that specific problem. You hand over a lump sum, and the insurance company promises you $X per month for life. It's the closest thing to a personal pension that exists in the retail market today.

When I tested quotes on a $200,000 SPIA for a 65-year-old male in October 2026, the best I found paid around $1,150–$1,300 per month for life. That's a payout rate of roughly 6.9–7.8% of the premium per year, with the money continuing until death. On a straight investment, you'd need to withdraw at a much more conservative safe rate to guarantee you'd never run out.

The catch (and there's always a catch): if you die early, most of that premium is gone. A "life only" SPIA has no death benefit. There are "life with period certain" versions that pay out to your estate if you die within 10 or 20 years, but they pay out less per month. The break-even point on a typical SPIA is roughly age 80–83 — you need to live that long for the annuity to outperform a simple bond ladder.

I have a family history of longevity and a grandmother who lived to 97. For me, that math leans slightly positive. For someone with health issues, the opposite.

### Scenario 2: You want tax deferral and you've maxed everything else

If you've already maxed your 401(k), your Roth IRA, your HSA, and your backdoor Roth, then a fixed deferred annuity or a variable annuity gives you one real benefit: **tax-deferred growth** on money that doesn't have a better home yet. It's not a great benefit compared to a 401(k) or IRA, but it beats a taxable brokerage account for a portion of your money if you're in a high tax bracket.

I use this argument sparingly, because most people reading this have not maxed their 401(k) and Roth IRA. If you're still in the phase where you're deciding whether to fund your 401(k) up to the employer match, that should be a higher priority than any annuity. (I went deep on this in my [Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA comparison](/posts/roth-ira-vs-traditional-ira-comparison/) — the tax math there is much cleaner than anything an annuity offers.)

## The specific traps I found in real contracts

I want to walk through the things I found in those six contracts that would have tripped up a normal person reading them for the first time. These are not rare edge cases — they were in most of them.

### Surrender charges that eat your early years

Every deferred annuity I looked at had a surrender charge schedule. If you want your money back before the schedule runs out, you pay a penalty. The typical schedule on the contracts I reviewed looked like this:

- Year 1: 7%
- Year 2: 7%
- Year 3: 6%
- Year 4: 5%
- Year 5: 4%
- Year 6: 3%
- Year 7: 2%
- Year 8: 0%

That means if you put in $100,000 and change your mind in year 2, you walk away with $93,000 minus fees. The salesperson almost never volunteers this number unless you ask. It's why the contracts are 40+ pages — the surrender schedule is on page 23.

### The "bonus" that isn't a bonus

Several of the contracts offered a "premium bonus" of 3–5% on the initial deposit. Sounds great, right? I put $100,000 in, they add $4,000, I start at $104,000.

But when I read the fine print, the bonus is almost always recaptured in one of two ways: either through a higher surrender charge schedule tied to the bonus amount (typically vesting over 10–12 years instead of 7–8), or through a "market value adjustment" (MVA) that claws back the bonus if you withdraw early. In one contract I read, the bonus was contingent on holding for a full 12 years, and the surrender schedule started at 9% instead of 7%. The bonus was really a longer lock-up in disguise.

If someone is pitching you on the bonus, ask to see the surrender charge schedule and the MVA formula in the same conversation. That's where the bonus lives.

### The income rider "guaranteed lifetime income" that isn't what you think

This one took me two contracts to fully understand. Income riders promise "guaranteed lifetime income" calculated off a "benefit base" that can be substantially higher than your actual account value. That sounds like free money. It isn't.

The benefit base grows at a guaranteed rate (say 6–7% per year) but the actual cash value of your annuity may grow much slower, or even decline, especially after you start taking withdrawals. And the "income" you're guaranteed comes out of the cash value first. So if the market does poorly, you can end up with a guaranteed income stream of $X per month, but almost no remaining cash value and nothing left for your heirs when you die.

The rider is basically a bet on a specific payout rate; the "guarantee" is a guarantee that you'll get paid, not a guarantee that you'll get rich. I ran a simple model: for a 65-year-old with a $100,000 deposit and an income rider with a 1.25% fee, the guaranteed monthly income at age 80 was around $700–$850 in the contracts I reviewed. That's real money, but it's also roughly comparable to what I could construct with a bond ladder plus a small SPIA — and the SPIA has lower fees and more transparency.

### The participation rate cap on fixed indexed annuities

Fixed indexed annuities (FIAs) got popular after 2008 because they promise "no downside and some upside." What the marketing doesn't say clearly is that the **upside is capped**. In the contracts I reviewed, the caps on S&P 500 gains ranged from 6% to 10% per year. So if the S&P 500 returns 15% (like it did in 2023, 2024, and 2025 in various quarters), you get 6%–10% — and the insurance company keeps the rest as the cost of the floor.

But — and this is the part that took me a while to understand — you don't actually get the floor for free. The floor is "0% guaranteed return" (not negative, not 0% — you don't lose money on the index), and you pay for that guarantee through the capped upside and through the contract fees. The pricing is opaque because it involves interest rate assumptions and option pricing that you can't see in a retail illustration.

The bottom line: FIAs are not "market upside with a floor." They're "market upside, capped, minus fees, with a floor that amounts to roughly zero percent in bad years." Whether that's a good deal depends entirely on the cap and the fee, and both are negotiable-ish (they vary by carrier and by whether you add an income rider). If someone pitches you on "market-linked returns with no downside," ask what the cap is and what the all-in fee is. Then do the math against a 60/40 portfolio.

## So is an annuity worth it? My honest answer

After three months of reading contracts and running numbers, here's where I've landed:

**Annuities are worth it if and only if the specific guarantee they provide solves a specific problem you have that no cheaper product solves.** For most people, that means:

- You genuinely need lifetime income and the fee-free alternatives (Social Security, a bond ladder, an income-focused ETF portfolio) don't provide enough of it.
- You have maxed every tax-advantaged account you can access and still have a large pot of taxable money that needs tax deferral.
- You're in your late 50s or 60s, and you're converting a chunk of savings into a guaranteed income floor specifically to reduce [sequence-of-returns risk](/posts/retirement-savings-by-age/) in early retirement.

**Annuities are not worth it if:**

- You're under 45 and you're being pitched one as a "savings vehicle." A 401(k), IRA, or taxable index fund portfolio will almost certainly beat it.
- You don't need the income guarantee and you're just being sold the "tax deferral" or "bonus" story.
- You haven't maxed out your [401(k) and IRA contributions](/posts/understanding-tax-advantaged-accounts-401k-ira-and-hsa/) yet.
- You're buying from someone whose only job is selling annuities. Fee-based advisors (who charge a flat fee instead of sales commissions) exist for a reason: the commission alone on a $200,000 variable annuity is often 5–7% of the deposit. That's $10,000–$14,000 of your money going to the salesperson on day one, before any growth happens.

That last point bugged me the most. I brought it up with my mother-in-law's "advisor" in a polite email asking whether he was commission-based or fee-only. He never responded. I have my suspicions about why.

## A framework I actually use when evaluating any annuity

If you're going to look at one of these products, here's the checklist I built. Every single item was a gotcha in at least one of the six contracts I reviewed.

1. **Get the full contract and the illustration in writing, including all fee schedules.** If the salesperson will only give you a summary, walk away. The summary is where the fees go to die.
2. **Ask for the total annual fee, all-in, as a single number.** If you get "it depends" or "that's complicated," ask them to write it down in a specific number. Legitimate fee-based products (including SPIA quotes) will give you this number. Variable annuities with riders will not — because it's embarrassing.
3. **Ask for the surrender charge schedule and the MVA formula.** Print it. Take it home. Read it over the weekend.
4. **Calculate the break-even vs a simple index fund.** My spreadsheet assumes 7% gross and compares to a 0.05% expense ratio fund. Run the same math with the actual fee schedule. If the annuity doesn't beat the index fund on a pure compounding basis after 15 years, ask why you're buying an annuity at all.
5. **Ask about the death benefit.** If you die in year 2 or year 12, what do your heirs get? Compare to what a straight investment would leave. Sometimes the death benefit rider (which costs extra) is the "guarantee" the salesperson is really selling you.
6. **Ask whether the salesperson is a fiduciary or a registered representative.** Fiduciaries have to put your interest first. Registered reps have a "best interest" standard (Reg BI since 2019) that is less strict. This distinction is worth $20,000 on a $300,000 annuity.
7. **Never buy in the first meeting.** If the product is good, it will be good in 30 days. If it's urgent, that's a sales tactic, not a financial reality.

That framework is the single most useful thing I took away from this whole exercise. It applies equally to FIAs, variable annuities, and anything else with a shiny brochure.

## The comparison I wish someone had shown me

It's not annuity vs. nothing. It's annuity vs. the boring alternative — usually an index fund portfolio plus a cash buffer. Here's the honest comparison on the numbers:

| | SPIA (immediate) | Variable or FIA (deferred) | Index fund portfolio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guaranteed lifetime income | Yes | Only with income rider (extra fee) | No |
| Market risk to you | None | Limited (varies) | Yes |
| Fees | 0.5–1.5% (often no surrender charge) | 2.5–4.0% annually | 0.03–0.10% |
| Liquidity | None (you can't get the lump sum back) | Surrender charges first 7–12 years | Daily |
| Tax deferral | Yes | Yes | No (in taxable account) |
| Death benefit to heirs | None (life only) or limited | Riders available | Full value |
| Realistic 20-year return net of fees | Moderate but guaranteed | Often lower than index fund | Highest expected |

The SPIA column is the one place where I think the product genuinely earns its keep. It's the one in the table where the fee is low and the guarantee is real and clean. The deferred annuities are where the industry makes its money — and where you're most likely to get pitched a product that doesn't fit you.

## What I ended up recommending to my mother-in-law

She had $210,000 from a small pension buyout sitting in a money market fund earning about 4.4% as of October 2026. The "advisor" wanted to put all of it into a fixed indexed annuity with an income rider. My recommendation was simpler:

- Keep $50,000 in a [high-yield savings account](/posts/high-yield-savings-vs-cds/) as a buffer for the first three years of retirement.
- Put $100,000 in a 70/30 index fund and bond portfolio for growth and liquidity.
- Use $60,000 to buy a plain SPIA with a 20-year period certain, giving her a guaranteed income floor of roughly $340/month for life.

That's not a flashy plan. It has no bonus, no "guaranteed income multiplier," and it paid exactly $0 in commission to the "advisor." But it's honest, it keeps her liquid, it eliminates most of the fee drag, and it solves the actual problem — a fear of running out of money — without paying 3.9% a year for a product she doesn't need.

She took the plan. She hasn't called me panicked about money since. That's the outcome that matters.

If you're at the stage of life where you're deciding between annuity-style income and a more straightforward drawdown strategy, my [retirement savings by age](/posts/retirement-savings-by-age/) benchmarks might help you figure out how much income you actually need to replace — the answer is usually lower than what an annuity salesperson will quote you. And if you're earlier in the process and just trying to build the pile that would eventually fund any of these strategies, [starting with index funds](/posts/start-investing-index-funds-beginners-guide/) will get you further, faster, than almost any insurance product on the market.

Annuities aren't evil. They're just expensive, and they solve a narrow problem most people don't actually have. Know which one you're buying, and why, and what it costs. That's the entire game.
