I don't want to be a landlord. There, I said it. I've watched two friends spend their weekends fixing water heaters, chasing late rent, and dealing with a tenant who painted a bedroom neon orange without asking. I want real estate exposure — the kind that historically beats inflation and throws off income — without the 2 a.m. phone calls.

So in August 2025 I started putting real money behind that idea. Over 14 months I built a $6,500 position split across publicly traded REITs, a real estate crowdfunding platform, and one experiment I regret. This is what I learned, with the actual numbers, fees, and tax paperwork that nobody puts in the marketing brochure.

If you're earlier in your investing journey, some of this will overlap with my [real estate investing for beginners guide](/posts/real-estate-investing-beginners-ways/) — but this article is specifically about the *no-deed, no-mortgage* version.

## What "Investing in Real Estate Without Buying Property" Actually Means

There are really four doors into real estate, and only one of them involves you signing a mortgage:

1. **Publicly traded REITs** — you buy shares of a company that owns income property, on a stock exchange, same as buying Apple.
2. **Real estate crowdfunding** — you invest in a specific property or portfolio through an online platform, usually with a multi-year lockup.
3. **Real estate ETFs and mutual funds** — baskets of REITs, sometimes including international and specialized sectors.
4. **Private REITs and non-traded funds** — sold through advisors or platforms, less liquid, higher fees.

Each has a different liquidity profile, fee structure, and tax treatment. The mistake I see beginners make is lumping them all into "real estate" and assuming they behave the same. They absolutely do not.

Before I go further — none of this is financial advice, and I'm not licensed to give it. I'm a person who tested things and took notes.

## Why I Even Bother With Real Estate Exposure

My core portfolio is index funds. I've written before about how [boring index funds made me $9,200 in my first year](/posts/start-investing-index-funds-beginners-guide/), and I still hold them as the backbone. But real estate has two properties I find genuinely useful:

- **Different return drivers.** REITs are driven by rents, occupancy, and property values — not the same thing as tech earnings.
- **Income.** REITs are legally required to distribute at least 90% of taxable income to shareholders. That's a structural dividend, not a discretionary one.

I'm not going to pretend real estate is uncorrelated magic. In March 2020 and again in the 2022 rate shock, REITs fell *harder* than the S&P 500 because they're rate-sensitive and leveraged. That's an honest caveat most cheerleading articles skip. If you can't stomach a 30–40% drawdown, a REIT-heavy allocation will test you.

## The REIT Route: What I Actually Bought and What It Returned

A REIT is a company that owns and operates income-producing real estate. You buy shares, you get dividends, and you can sell any trading day. That liquidity is the whole appeal.

When I tested this starting in August 2025, I split roughly $4,200 across four positions to spread sector exposure:

| Ticker | Sector | Position Size | My 14-Month Total Return* | Dividend Yield (Oct 2026) | Expense Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| VNQ | Broad US REIT ETF | $1,800 | +11.4% | 3.7% | 0.13% |
| O (Realty Income) | Net lease retail | $1,000 | +9.1% | 5.6% | — (individual stock) |
| PLD (Prologis) | Industrial/logistics | $800 | +14.2% | 3.1% | — |
| VNQI | International REIT ETF | $600 | +5.8% | 4.2% | 0.12% |

*Total return includes reinvested dividends through October 2026. My own brokerage statements, not a marketing sheet.

I noticed that VNQ's 0.13% expense ratio is almost free, while holding individual REITs like O means I have to track their earnings calls myself. For most people, the ETF is the sane default — it gives you ~160 holdings and rebalances itself.

The number that genuinely surprised me: **Realty Income (O) has paid a monthly dividend since 1969** and has raised it over 120 times. A monthly distribution hits my account on the 15th like clockwork. It's psychological, but it made me a better holder than quarterly dividends did.

### The Tax Trap Nobody Warns You About

Here's where it gets annoying. REIT dividends are **mostly non-qualified**, meaning they're taxed at your ordinary income rate, not the lower long-term capital gains rate. In 2025, for someone in the 24% bracket, that's a meaningful difference versus a qualified dividend from a regular stock.

I ran into this hard when I filed this spring. I'd assumed my REIT dividends were "like dividends," and my tax bill came in about $140 higher than I'd budgeted. If you're fuzzy on the mechanics, my [taxes on investment gains guide](/posts/investment-gains-taxes-explained/) covers the qualified-vs-ordinary distinction. The short version: hold REITs in tax-advantaged accounts — a Roth IRA or 401(k) — whenever you can.

That single move — putting my REITs inside a Roth instead of a taxable brokerage — would have saved me roughly $140 in year one and compounds from there.

## Real Estate Crowdfunding: Higher Potential, Lower Liquidity

Crowdfunding platforms let you buy a fractional stake in a specific deal — an apartment complex, a self-storage facility, a data center — often for $500 to $5,000 minimums. I tested two platforms and put $2,000 total into three deals.

Here's the comparison from my notes:

| Platform Type | Example | Minimum | Typical Target Return | Liquidity | My Experience |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equity crowdfunding | Fundrise | $10 | 6–10% (varies) | Quarterly redemptions* | Easy to start, fees are layered |
| Debt/presale | CrowdStreet | $2,500–$25,000 | 8–15% | None — 3–7 year lockup | Opaque underwriting |
| Fractional single-family | Arrived | $100 | Rental income + appreciation | Long hold | Simplest to understand |

*Fundrise's redemption window has been limited at times depending on the fund. Read the fine print.

When I tested Fundrise in September 2025, the onboarding took about 10 minutes and I was invested the same day. But the fee structure was the part that took me an hour to untangle: an annual management fee plus underlying fund expenses plus a possible performance component. My effective all-in cost landed somewhere between **0.5% and 1.5%**, which is 4–10x what VNQ charges.

To be fair, the projected income was higher too — around 7% targeted versus VNQ's 3.7% current yield. But that's a *target*, not a guarantee, and the platform is very clear (buried in the disclosures) that redemptions can be paused.

I noticed that the single most important thing to check on any crowdfunding platform is **whether the deal is equity or debt**. Debt deals pay fixed interest and generally get paid before equity holders if a property sells at a loss. Equity deals are first to absorb losses. This one distinction matters more than the headline return rate.

## ETFs vs Direct REITs vs Crowdfunding: The Honest Scorecard

After 14 months, here's my real, unglamorous take:

**Low-effort, low-cost:** Broad REIT ETFs (VNQ, VNQI, or a low-cost SCHH at 0.07%). You get instant diversification, daily liquidity, and you can put them in a Roth. This is where 80% of people should land.

**More income, more homework:** Individual blue-chip REITs like O or PLD. Higher yield, but you're now doing analysis — and you can be badly wrong on a single property sector. I'm not comfortable concentrating.

**Potential upside, real lockup risk:** Crowdfunding. The returns *can* beat ETFs, but you're illiquid for years, the fees are much higher, and you have far less transparency. Treat this as a small satellite position — I'd cap it at 5–10% of a portfolio, and only money you won't need.

The mistake I made was starting with crowdfunding before I had a solid REIT ETF base. If I did it again, I'd build the VNQ position first, then add crowdfunding with money I could genuinely leave alone.

## How Much Should Real Estate Be of Your Portfolio?

This is where I differ from some advisors. Typical guidance is 5–15% of a portfolio in real estate (above and beyond your primary home). I'm comfortable around 10% — and that's where I sit.

But the number depends hugely on your life stage. My [asset allocation by age guide](/posts/asset-allocation-by-age-guide/) walks through this, but the shortcut: REITs behave like a blend of equity and bond — rate-sensitive in an environment where rates rise. If you're younger and have a long horizon, they can be a nice diversifier. If you're within five years of retirement and drawing income, a big REIT position adds volatility you may not want.

One number I keep coming back to: the Vanguard Total Stock Market Index (VTI) already holds **roughly 3% real estate** in the form of REITs. So before I added anything, I was already mildly exposed. That reframed it for me — I wasn't adding a brand-new asset class, I was *tilted* toward it.

## The Part Where I Admit What Went Wrong

My worst position was a pre-sale debt deal I won't name. It targeted 11% annualized and I put in $800. Nine months in, the developer announced the project was delayed a full year and confirmed it would defer interest payments. That's not a loss yet — but my money is frozen and the promised return is deferred.

The lesson: **"target return" is not a number you can spend.** Crowdfunding deal pages show projections as if they're facts. I now read the full Offering Memorandum — yes, all of it — before investing, and I assume the worst case is the actual case. I also stopped believing a headline return without understanding *how* it's generated (rent? sale? refinance?).

The other thing I'd flag: crowdfunding returns often look attractive in part because they're illiquid. You're being paid a premium to lock up your money. If you might need cash in two years, this isn't the product.

## A Simple Setup That Actually Works (2026 Version)

If I had to rebuild this from scratch today, here's what I'd do:

1. **Maxize tax-advantaged space first** — REITs inside a Roth IRA or 401(k) to avoid the ordinary-income tax hit. See my [Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA breakdown](/posts/understanding-roth-ira-vs-traditional-ira-which-is-right-for/) if you're choosing.
2. **Core: 8–10% of the portfolio in VNQ or SCHH.** Boring, cheap, liquid.
3. **Optional satellite: 2–5% in crowdfunding** with money you can genuinely leave for 3+ years.
4. **Check monthly, not daily.** I stopped opening the app and my holding behavior improved.

Rebalancing matters here too. My target is 10% real estate. If it drifts above 12%, I trim; below 8%, I add. That's mechanical and it works. I use the same logic I wrote about in my [monthly budget that actually works](/posts/how-to-create-a-monthly-budget-that-works/) piece — rules beat willpower.

## Quick Answers to What People Actually Ask Me

**Can I start with $100?** Yes. VNQ trades for around $90–$100 a share in 2026, and fractional shares are available at Fidelity, Schwab, and Robinhood. Fundrise takes $10. That's the whole game.

**Are REITs risky?** They fell around 26% in 2022 and around 40% in 2008. They're equity-like. Don't confuse "real estate" with "safe."

**Do I need a lot of money?** No. The fractional world has genuinely opened this up. My minimum viable position was $100.

**Is crowdfunding a scam?** Not inherently — plenty of legitimate platforms exist and are regulated. But the worst offerings hide behind slick marketing. Always read the OM.

**Should I just buy an REIT index fund and forget it?** Honestly, for most people, yes. If you told me you had $500 and wanted real estate exposure, I'd say VNQ and be done.

## What I'd Tell Someone Starting Today

Real estate is worth owning, and you don't need a down payment, a mortgage, or a tenant to do it. The tools genuinely work — I've held REITs and crowdfunding for 14 months and the math pencils out. But the marketing oversells the smooth parts (income! diversification!) and undersells the sharp ones (tax treatment, lockups, fee drag, and that ugly 2022 drawdown).

My one-line summary: **build the cheap liquid REIT ETF foundation first, put it in a tax-advantaged account if you can, and treat crowdfunding as a small high-effort experiment — never the core.** Do that, and you get the exposure without the neon-orange bedroom and the 2 a.m. water heater call.
