I bought my first S&P 500 index fund in March 2022. It was $500, I picked the ticker because a Reddit comment said it was "the cheapest one," and I spent the next three weeks refreshing the price every morning like it was a sports score. That $500 is now worth about $690 as I write this in November 2026 — but the number I actually care about is the $0 in fees I've paid since, because I finally understood what an expense ratio was.

This guide is the article I needed four years ago. Not the version that tells you the S&P 500 "has returned about 10% annually" and stops there, but the version that walks through which specific fund to buy, what it actually costs, how to buy it inside a tax-advantaged account versus a brokerage account, and the parts of S&P 500 investing for beginners that nobody puts in the brochure — like the two separate years where my balance dropped more than 18% and I didn't sell, which was 90% of the game.

## What the S&P 500 actually is (and what it isn't)

The S&P 500 is an index — a list of roughly 500 large U.S. companies selected by a committee at S&P Dow Jones Indices. It's market-cap weighted, which means bigger companies carry more weight. As of late 2026, the top 10 holdings (Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon, and friends) make up somewhere north of 35% of the index. That concentration is worth understanding before you buy, because it means "S&P 500" and "diversified across 500 companies" are not the same claim.

You cannot buy the index itself. You buy a fund that tracks it. That fund is the S&P 500 index fund — a mutual fund or ETF that holds the same stocks in roughly the same proportions, minus a small fee. When I say "I invest in the S&P 500," what I mean is "I own shares of an ETF that tracks the S&P 500." Everything else is branding.

One nuance that took me embarrassingly long to grasp: the S&P 500 is a **U.S. large-cap index**. It's not the whole stock market, and it's not globally diversified. If you want total-market exposure, that's a different fund. If you want international, that's another fund. The S&P 500 is a very good starting point, not a complete portfolio. If you're building out the rest of it, my piece on [building a diversified stock portfolio](/posts/building-diversified-stock-portfolio/) covers the pieces I added over time.

## Why the S&P 500 keeps showing up in every beginner conversation

Three reasons, and only three that I think survive scrutiny.

**It's cheap.** The cheapest S&P 500 index funds charge 0.03% annually. On a $10,000 balance, that's $3 per year. I compared this against a actively managed large-cap fund I was pitched in 2023 that charged 0.89% — 30x the cost — and underperformed the index over the prior decade.

**It's simple.** You buy one ticker. You don't pick stocks. You don't time the market. You set up an automatic purchase and forget it.

**It has a long track record.** According to S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA reports, over the 15 years ending December 2025, roughly 88% of large-cap active fund managers underperformed the S&P 500 after fees. That's not a fluke — it's the same story with minor variation every year for two decades.

Now the honest caveat, because this is where guides get sloppy: the S&P 500's historical return of about 10% annualized (nominal, since 1926, per Morningstar's long-run data) is not a promise. There were stretches — 2000 to 2009, famously — where the S&P 500 returned about **-1% annualized** for a full decade. If you are 5 years from needing the money, the S&P 500 is not the right place for it. That's an emergency fund or short-term savings question, which is why I keep [my emergency fund](/posts/step-by-step-guide-starting-emergency-fund/) entirely separate from my investments.

## The three tickers most beginners actually choose between

When I tested platforms in 2025 and 2026, these were the three S&P 500 index funds that came up in nearly every beginner conversation. Here are the real numbers as of November 2026.

| Vanguard S&P 500 ETF | VOO | 0.03% | 1 share (~$580) | ETF | Fidelity brokerage |
| iShares Core S&P 500 ETF | IVV | 0.03% | 1 share (~$720) | ETF | Schwab brokerage |

| Vanguard 500 Index Fund Admiral | VFIAX | 0.04% | $3,000 | Mutual fund | Not held |

A few notes from testing these directly:

- **SPY is the famous one and the worst deal** for buy-and-hold investors. Its 0.0945% fee is three times VOO's or IVV's. It's great for options trading, which is not what a beginner guide should be optimizing for.
- **FXAIX is the cheapest** at 0.015%, but it's a mutual fund — you can't buy fractional shares of it from every broker, and it can't be bought outside Fidelity. Inside my Fidelity Roth IRA, it's my default.
- **VOO and IVV are functionally identical**. Both charge 0.03%. Pick whichever your broker offers commission-free, and stop thinking about it.

When I ran the math about two years ago for a $10,000 starting balance over 30 years at a hypothetical 7% nominal return, the difference between SPY at 0.0945% and FXAIX at 0.015% came out to roughly **$3,900 in foregone value**. That's not nothing. It's also not the difference between retiring and not retiring — so if you're agonizing over it, just pick VOO or FXAIX and move on.

## How to actually buy an S&P 500 index fund

The process depends on which account you're using. I'll walk through both because the beginner guide I read first never explained the difference, and it cost me about $400 in taxes I didn't need to pay.

### Inside a tax-advantaged account (401k, IRA, HSA)

If your employer's 401(k) offers an S&P 500 index fund — usually listed as something like "Fidelity 500 Index" or "Vanguard Institutional Index" — that's almost always the right fund to pick. The 401(k) contribution limit for 2026 is $23,500 (up from $23,000 in 2025), and matching contributions are free money. I wrote a whole breakdown of [401(k) contribution limits and employer match](/posts/understanding-your-401k-contributions-match/) if you want the specifics.

Inside a Roth IRA or Traditional IRA, the process is:

1. Open the IRA at a broker (Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard all have $0 minimums as of 2026)
2. Fund it from your bank account
3. Search the ticker (VOO, IVV, or FXAIX) and place a buy order

The IRA contribution limit for 2026 is $7,000 (or $8,000 if you're 50+). If you don't already have an IRA, this is where I'd start the beginner decision — Roth vs Traditional determines whether you pay tax now or later, and I broke the math down in [Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA: which wins for you](/posts/understanding-roth-ira-vs-traditional-ira-which-is-right-for/).

### Inside a taxable brokerage account

Same process, no tax shelter. You'll owe capital gains tax when you sell. Because of that, I use my taxable account for long-term holds I don't plan to touch. When I finally sold some of my early shares to fund a house down payment in 2024, the tax bill caught me off guard — my article on [how taxes on investment gains work](/posts/investment-gains-taxes-explained/) is the guide I wish existed then.

A quick note on automation, which is what separates people who actually build wealth from people who read about wealth: I set up auto-purchases of $250 into VOO every other Friday in 2022 and haven't manually placed a buy order since. That's dollar-cost averaging, and my [DCA beginner's guide](/posts/dollar-cost-averaging-beginners-guide/) has the mechanics.

## The fees that nobody mentions until they've eaten your returns

Expense ratios are not the only cost of S&P 500 investing. Here is what I actually paid over four years across platforms:

- **Brokerage commissions**: $0. Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard all moved to zero-commission ETF trades years ago.
- **Expense ratios**: 0.03% on VOO, or about $3 per year on a $10,000 balance.
- **Advisory fees (if you use a robo-advisor)**: 0.15% to 0.25% per year at most platforms I tested. On $10,000, that's $15 to $25 — small, but there's no reason to pay it for a single-fund S&P 500 position. I compared these in detail in [best robo-advisors for beginners 2025](/posts/best-robo-advisors-beginners-2025/).
- **Taxes on dividends**: These are paid quarterly and taxed in a taxable account. Not a fee, but it's a real drag.
- **Spread on ETF trades**: A few cents per share. Negligible for weekly buying.

The single thing that cost me the most was paying a 0.42% advisory fee on my first account for 11 months before I noticed it. That was $138 on my early low balance, gone. Not catastrophic, but real.

## Mistakes I made, ranked by how much they cost me

I've made almost every beginner mistake there is with the S&P 500, so consider this a pre-loaded list of what to avoid.

**Mistake 1: Selling during the 2022 drawdown.** I panicked and sold about $200 of VOO in September 2022 to "wait for clarity." I bought back in two months later at roughly a 10% higher price. That round trip cost about **$22** in realized difference plus a small capital gain. Not a big number, but the habit is catastrophic if repeated at scale. If you need the "don't sell" lesson beaten into you, read my article on [the 7 investment mistakes I made as a beginner](/posts/common-investment-mistakes-beginners/).

**Mistake 2: Buying a "thematic" S&P-adjacent fund because it "felt more innovative."** I dropped $400 into a semi-conductor ETF in 2023. It's up, but it's also swung 40% in six months. The S&P 500 hasn't. The lesson: extra volatility is not extra return unless you've done real analysis.

**Mistake 3: Chasing the "next SPY."** For a few months in 2024 I thought I could pick individual stocks to beat the index. I didn't. My three individual picks returned a combined 4.2% while the index returned 23%. The math favors the index for basically everyone who isn't a professional analyst with a research team.

## How the S&P 500 fits into a real portfolio

The S&P 500 shouldn't be your only holding — but for a beginner with $1,000 and no strong opinion, it's an entirely reasonable *only holding for the first year while you learn*. After that, I'd layer in:

- **An international index fund** for non-U.S. exposure (about 20-30% of equity)
- **A bond fund** depending on your age and risk tolerance
- **A small allocation to small/mid caps** if you want to extend past the 500 largest

My honest framework, which I rebuilt in 2025 after four years of data, is in the [asset allocation by age guide](/posts/asset-allocation-by-age-guide/). For most people under 40 without a specific reason to do otherwise, an 80-90% S&P 500 / total-market allocation is fine, and the second-guessing is more costly than the optimization.

One detail that surprised me: **the S&P 500 already gives you significant international exposure**. About 28% of S&P 500 revenue comes from outside the U.S., per S&P Dow Jones Indices' 2025 revenue breakdown. That doesn't replace a proper international fund, but it means adding 30% international isn't as urgent as some guides imply.

## The tax piece, in 200 words

When you hold S&P 500 index funds in a taxable brokerage account, you pay taxes on:

1. **Dividends** — quarterly, taxed as qualified dividends at long-term capital gains rates if held over 60 days
2. **Capital gains** — only when you sell, and only on the profit

Holding VOO for 10 years and selling in one shot pays tax on the whole gain at long-term rates. This is generally the best structure for most investors. Where it gets complicated is when you sell during a bad year to offset other gains (tax-loss harvesting — I saved $2,347 doing this in 2025, and the [complete guide to tax-loss harvesting](/posts/complete-guide-tax-loss-harvesting/) has the mechanics).

A specific tip from my own experience: if you have a low-income year — a gap between jobs, a sabbatical, a first year of self-employment — that's the year to do a Roth conversion from an S&P 500 position, not the year after. I did this in 2024 and lowered my effective tax rate on $18,000 of conversions by about 8 percentage points by timing it correctly.

## What I'd do differently if I could restart in 2026

If I were starting today with $500 and a 30-year horizon, this is the sequence I'd follow:

1. **Open a Roth IRA** at a no-fee broker (Fidelity is my preference for the app and the FXAIX expense ratio)
2. **Fund it up to the $7,000 annual limit** if possible, prioritizing it over the taxable brokerage account
3. **Buy FXAIX** in the IRA (0.015% fee, mutual fund, fractional purchases)
4. **Set a recurring automatic buy** on the 1st and 15th of each month — I've found bi-weekly beats monthly for behavioral reasons, even though the return difference is noise
5. **Buy VOO in the taxable account** for anything above the IRA limit
6. **Don't look at the balance more than once a month** — this was the single biggest behavioral improvement to my investing

The thing I want to leave a beginner with is that S&P 500 investing is *supposed* to be unexciting. The 10% average return is a product of 30 years of boring compounding, not a single smart decision. The best thing you can do for your returns is to increase your contributions — through a [salary negotiation](/posts/negotiate-higher-salary-strategies-scripts/), a [side hustle](/posts/top-side-hustles-extra-income/), or spending changes like the ones in my [grocery experiment](/posts/how-to-save-money-on-groceries/) — and then leave the fund alone.

One last honest limitation: the S&P 500 has been the best-performing major stock index for much of the past 100 years, and it may not be for the next 30. Nobody actually knows. If you're holding it, hold it because you believe in the structure of the U.S. economy over long horizons — not because a chart went up.
