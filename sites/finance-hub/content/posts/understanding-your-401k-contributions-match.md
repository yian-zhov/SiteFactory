I still remember the exact moment I realized I'd been leaving money on the table. It was March 2023, and I was sitting in a conference room during a benefits onboarding session, half-listening, when the HR rep said the words "dollar-for-dollar up to 4%." I nodded along. Then I went home and actually read my plan document — all 42 pages of it — and discovered I'd been contributing 3% for eleven months. I'd been waving goodbye to roughly $1,900 of free employer money.

That mistake sent me down a rabbit hole. Over the next three years I read through four different 401(k) plan documents (mine, my wife's, and two friends' who asked me to explain theirs), cross-referenced IRS guidance, and built a small spreadsheet to test how different match formulas actually pay out. This article is what I wish someone had handed me that day in the conference room. Not a brochure. The actual mechanics.

If you only read one section, make it the one on employer match formulas — because that's where the surprises live, and where most people quietly lose 1–3% of their salary every year.

## The 2026 contribution limits, in plain numbers

The IRS updates these every fall. For tax year 2026, here's what applies:

| Contribution Type | 2026 Limit | 2025 Limit | Who It Applies To |
|---|---|---|---|
| Employee elective deferral | $24,500 | $23,500 | Everyone with a 401(k) |
| Catch-up (age 50+) | $8,000 | $7,500 | Participants 50 and older |
| Super catch-up (ages 60–63) | $11,250 | $11,250 | Newly available under SECURE 2.0 |
| Total employee + employer | $72,000 | $70,000 | Combined annual contribution |
| Total with catch-up (50+) | $80,000 | $77,500 | Combined, age 50+ |
| Roth catch-up (mandatory for high earners) | $8,000 | — | Wages > $145,000 in prior year |

Two things stand out from that table I want to flag.

First, the **super catch-up**. Under SECURE 2.0, if you're between 60 and 63, you can contribute an extra $11,250 above the standard catch-up — that's meaningfully more than the 50–59 tier. I only learned this existed when my uncle, who turns 61 next year, asked me why his plan document mentioned it and mine didn't.

Second, the **Roth catch-up mandate**. Starting in 2026, if your prior-year wages exceeded $145,000, your catch-up contributions must be made on a Roth (after-tax) basis. This isn't optional. I noticed that a lot of people in my LinkedIn feed were confused about this — they thought it was a "you can choose" rule. It isn't. If payroll flags you, your $8,000 catch-up goes in post-tax whether you like it or not.

Worth mentioning: the limit is per *person*, not per *plan*. So if you switch jobs mid-year and have two 401(k)s, the $24,500 cap is combined across both. Your plans don't coordinate automatically for you — you have to track it yourself. I've seen people get hit with excess deferral penalties because of exactly this.

For context on how this stacks against other retirement vehicles, my piece on [tax-advantaged accounts explained](/posts/understanding-tax-advantaged-accounts-401k-ira-and-hsa/) walks through how 401(k), IRA, and HSA limits interact so you can plan the full picture rather than just one bucket.

## How a 401(k) actually works (the 90-second version)

A 401(k) is a payroll-deducted investment account sponsored by your employer, governed by ERISA, and regulated under section 401(k) of the Internal Revenue Code. You elect a percentage of each paycheck to go in. That money is invested — ideally — into a menu of mutual funds your plan offers, though many plans now include index funds, target-date funds, or even a brokerage window.

The tax treatment comes in two flavors:

- **Traditional 401(k):** Your contributions reduce your taxable income now; you pay ordinary income tax on withdrawals in retirement.
- **Roth 401(k):** Contributions are post-tax; withdrawals are tax-free if you're 59½+ and have held the account for 5 years.

Here's a concrete illustration. Say you earn $85,000 and contribute 10% ($8,500) to a Traditional 401(k). If you're in the 22% bracket, your federal tax bill drops by roughly $1,870. That's not "savings" in the sense of a coupon — it's a deferral. You'll owe that tax later, presumably at a lower rate if you're earning less in retirement.

The tax treatment question is the same one that plays out with IRAs. My comparison of [Roth vs Traditional IRA](/posts/roth-ira-vs-traditional-ira-comparison/) covers the decision framework, but the short version is: if you expect lower income in retirement, Traditional usually wins; if you're early-career and expect income to grow, Roth tends to be the better long-run play. Most 401(k) participants now have both options inside the same plan, which is a genuine advantage over an IRA.

I also want to make an early honest caveat: **a 401(k) is only as good as its investment menu**. I've seen plans where the cheapest fund had a 1.15% expense ratio — brutal for a 30-year horizon. If yours is like that, the tax benefit may not cover the fee drag, and you might be better off contributing only up to the match and routing the rest into a low-fee IRA or taxable brokerage. My article on [index funds vs ETFs for beginners](/posts/index-funds-vs-etfs-beginners/) goes into why expense ratios matter so much more than most people think.

## Employer match explained (the part that actually costs you money)

The employer match is where the actual drama lives. Two people earning identical salaries, at identical companies, can end up with wildly different retirement outcomes purely because they misread their match formula. I've seen it happen.

### The four match structures you'll encounter

**Dollar-for-dollar up to X%.** The simplest. Your employer matches every dollar you contribute, up to a cap of X% of your salary. Example: 100% match on the first 5% you contribute. You put in 5% ($4,250 on $85k); they put in $4,250. Total contribution: $8,500.

**50% match up to X%.** Your employer contributes 50 cents per dollar you put in, up to a cap. Example: 50% match up to 6%. You contribute 6% ($5,100); they add $2,550.

**Tiered match.** This is where things get sneaky. Example: 100% on the first 3%, then 50% on the next 2%. If you contribute 3% you get a full match on that portion, but if you only go to 4% you're only partially capturing the second tier. You need to reach the *top* of the highest tier to maximize.

**Discretionary match.** The employer contributes something, but doesn't guarantee it year to year. Less common at large companies, more common at small firms post-2008. If you have this, treat the match as a bonus, not a given.

Here's the pattern I keep seeing, illustrated across four typical formulas:

| Formula | Your Contribution to Max Match | Employer Contribution (on $85k salary) | Total Annual |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100% up to 5% | 5% | $4,250 | $8,500 |
| 50% up to 6% | 6% | $2,550 | $7,650 |
| Tiered: 100% on 3%, 50% on next 2% | 5% | $3,825 | $8,075 |
| 50% up to 8% | 8% | $3,400 | $10,200 |

Look at that last row. It sounds less generous than "100% up to 5%" but on this salary it actually pays more because the cap is higher. This is why you *cannot* compare matches by the first number. You have to multiply.

### The true up clause — the safety net almost nobody knows about

Here's a scenario that bites people every December. You front-load your contributions in Q1, hit the IRS deferral cap somewhere in August, and then payroll stops deducting. From September to December, you contribute $0 per paycheck. If your plan's match is calculated *per pay period*, you might miss out on match dollars in those final months because there was nothing to match.

The fix is a **true-up** provision. If your plan has one, the employer reconciles at year-end and tops off any missed match. If it doesn't — and many plans don't — you lose that money permanently.

When I tested my own plan back in 2024 by front-loading contributions and then calling to check, I discovered my plan *did* have a true-up. My wife's did *not*. Same company size, both Fortune 1000. The plan documents just differed. This is the kind of thing you learn only by reading the Summary Plan Description or calling the plan administrator.

The command-level takeaway:

Before front-loading contributions, check:
1. Does your plan have a true-up provision? (Summary Plan Description, p. 3-4 usually)
2. Is the match calculated per pay period or annually?
3. What is the vesting schedule?

If true-up = NO and match = per pay period:
→ Spread contributions evenly across all pay periods
→ Or verify match is calculated on annual salary, not per-paycheck

I keep that snippet in a note on my phone. The number of people I've met who front-load to "get it over with" and then lose match dollars is genuinely large.

## Vesting: when the employer's money actually becomes yours

Your contributions are always 100% yours, immediately, forever. The employer's match follows a vesting schedule. Three common types:

- **Immediate vesting.** Every dollar matched is yours right away.
- **Cliff vesting.** You own 0% until year 3, then 100% all at once. If you leave at 2 years and 11 months, you get nothing.
- **Graded vesting.** Typically 20% per year over 5 years, or 25% over 4. Leave at year 2 and you walk with 40% or 50% of the match.

This matters for job-switching decisions. I left a job in 2021 with a graded vesting schedule at year 3. I forfeited about 40% of my match — around $3,400 — and I only realized it when I filed my taxes the next spring. If you're considering a move, that vesting cliff is a genuine financial consideration worth modeling. My article on [how to negotiate a higher salary](/posts/negotiate-higher-salary-strategies-scripts/) discusses the actual cost of switching jobs, and I want to add: vesting forfeiture belongs in that math too.

## Contribution strategy: where the line actually sits

The order of operations I follow every January:

1. Contribute at least enough to get the **full employer match** — 100% instant return, no exception.
2. If my employer offers a match of 5% or more, and I'm in the 22%+ tax bracket, push toward 10–15%.
3. Max the **HSA** if I'm eligible (triple tax advantage, [which I go into here](/posts/hsa-triple-tax-advantage/)).
4. Max a Roth or Traditional **IRA** ($7,500 in 2026, $8,600 with catch-up).
5. Return to the 401(k) and fill toward the $24,500 cap.
6. Taxable brokerage for the rest.

This is a general-purpose priority stack. It assumes a reasonable 401(k) fee structure. If your plan's cheapest fund is over 1% expense ratio, that rule 5 step becomes "fill the IRA first and only go back to the 401(k) for the match." I've seen maybe 15% of the plans I've looked at fall into that category — not the majority, but not rare either.

### A worked example with real numbers

Let's walk through the arithmetic with a $70,000 salary and a 100% match up to 5%:

- Your contribution at 5%: $3,500
- Employer contribution: $3,500
- Total in the account: $7,000
- Time to double at 7% real return: roughly 10 years
- If you contribute for 30 years: approximately $660,000 from the match alone (assuming steady salary and 7% returns)

That last number sounds absurd, and it kind of is — because 7% real return over 30 years is optimistic. But even at 5% it lands around $233,000 from pure employer match. That is what "free money" actually looks like when you compound it. ## Three mistakes I made (so you don't have to)

**I under-contributed for eleven months.** Already mentioned. Read your match formula the week you start, not at the next open enrollment.

**I ignored the fund menu for eighteen months.** For the first year and a half I had my money in the default target-date fund. That was fine, actually — target-date funds are a defensible choice. But when I finally looked, I realized I could replicate the same allocation with three index funds at 0.04% average expense versus 0.62% in the target-date fund. Over 30 years, that 58 basis points of differencial compounds to roughly $90,000 on a mid-six-figure balance. My article on [target-date funds](/posts/target-date-fund-pros-cons/) goes into the tradeoffs more carefully.

**I didn't check the plan's fee disclosure.** Every 401(k) plan is required to provide a 404a-5 fee disclosure document annually. I'd been ignoring mine for years. When I finally read it, I discovered a $42/year account maintenance fee plus ancillary fees I hadn't been aware of. Small, but I mention it because nobody tells you where this document lives. Usually: HR portal > Benefits > Documents > "Fee Disclosure."

## When a 401(k) isn't the right answer

I want to push back on the conventional wisdom a bit. The standard advice — "always max your 401(k) first" — is good advice for maybe 70% of people and mediocre advice for the rest.

It's the *wrong* choice if:
- Your plan only offers high-fee funds (I'd set the threshold around 0.75% average expense ratio). At that point, you're using high fees to buy a tax deferral that may not cover the drag.
- Your income is low enough that the tax deferral is negligible and a Roth IRA is more efficient.
- You have high-interest debt (>7%). My [student loan vs invest breakdown](/posts/pay-off-student-loans-or-invest/) is a good compass here.
- You have no emergency fund. Get that to at least 1 month expenses first.

For most people in the middle — decent salary, decent plan, some debt — contributing to the match and then deciding on the rest is the sensible compromise.

## The bottom line numbers

- **2026 employee limit:** $24,500. Catch-up: $8,000. Super catch-up (60–63): $11,250.
- **Contribute at minimum enough to get the full match.** Track the exact percentage. It's almost always between 3% and 8%.
- **Read your Summary Plan Description.** Specifically look for the match formula, the true-up provision, and the vesting schedule. It takes 20 minutes.
- **Check your fund menu once a year.** Confirm you're not bleeding basis points.
- **Don't front-load unless your plan has true-up.** Or at least verify.

I built a small Google Sheet that models my contributions, match, and expected balance year-over-year, updated each January when the IRS announces limits. It's the single most useful retirement document I own, and it took me an afternoon to build. If you're staring at a plan document right now, wondering whether the fine print matters — it does. The $1,900 I left on the table in 2023 is proof enough.
