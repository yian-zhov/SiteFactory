I sat at my kitchen table on a Tuesday night in September 2026 with a spreadsheet, two Zillow tabs, and a mortgage calculator open on my phone. That was the third time in four years I'd done this ritual. The first two times, I talked myself out of buying. This time, the numbers finally pointed the other way — but not for the reason I expected.

What I've learned from running this math repeatedly is that the rent vs buy question almost never gets answered by the thing people think it will. It's not about "throwing money away on rent." It's not about pride of ownership. It's a five-number test, and if you run it honestly, the answer is usually clearer than the endless internet debate suggests.

Let me walk you through exactly how I decide, with the actual numbers from my last three runs.

## The "Rent Is Throwing Money Away" Myth, Quantified

I want to start here because it's the belief that ruins more financial decisions than any other in this category.

Rent is not wasted money. It is the price of a place to live for one month, bundled with maintenance, property taxes, insurance, and mobility. When you buy, you don't stop paying for those things — you just start paying for them in lumpy, unpredictable chunks that you also have to manage yourself.

Here's what my own rent looked like in 2026: $2,150/month for a two-bedroom, and my landlord replaced the water heater in March (I paid $0), patched the roof in July (I paid $0), and handled a nasty ant invasion in the kitchen (I paid $0). When I modeled buying the identical unit, the mortgage payment alone was $2,680 at a 6.4% rate — before taxes, insurance, HOA, and maintenance.

That gap of roughly $530/month is not "equity I'm missing." It's money I still have, sitting in a high-yield savings account earning 4.3% as of October 2026. The question is whether that gap, invested, ever catches up to the equity and appreciation a house builds. Sometimes yes, sometimes no. You have to run it.

If you're still working out where your monthly surplus goes, my [50/30/20 budget breakdown](/posts/fifty-thirty-twenty-budget-rule-explained/) is where I'd start — you can't sensibly answer rent vs buy until you know your actual housing ceiling as a percentage of take-home pay.

## The Five Numbers That Actually Decide This

Forget the generic "you should buy if you'll stay 5+ years" advice. That rule is a shorthand for a calculation, and it's been wrong for me twice. Here's what I actually plug in.

### 1. The Price-to-Rent Ratio

Divide the purchase price of a home by the annual rent for a comparable place.

| Price-to-Rent Ratio | What It Usually Means | My Default Lean |
|---|---|---|
| Under 15 | Buying is often cheaper long-term | Buy if stable |
| 15–20 | Genuinely close; depends on other factors | Run the full math |
| 20–25 | Renting usually wins unless you stay 10+ years | Lean rent |
| Over 25 | Strong rent signal in most markets | Rent |

For the condo I looked at in April 2026: $510,000 price, $27,600 annual rent for a comparable unit = ratio of 18.5. Right in the gray zone. For the house I looked at in September: $640,000 price, $26,400 annual rent = ratio of 24.2. That second one was a much weaker buy signal, even though it *felt* like the "real" house I wanted.

I noticed that my emotional desire to buy the second property was inversely correlated with how good a buy it actually was. That's not a coincidence — the nicer house is nicer because it's more expensive relative to the rent it could command.

### 2. Total Monthly Ownership Cost

This is where most rent vs buy calculators lie to you. They show you the mortgage payment and stop. Here's the real formula I use:

Total Monthly Ownership Cost =
  Principal + Interest
  + Property Tax (annual ÷ 12)
  + Homeowners Insurance (annual ÷ 12)
  + HOA Fees
  + Maintenance Reserve (1% of price ÷ 12)
  - Tax Savings (marginal rate × deductible interest & tax ÷ 12)

For the $640,000 house with 20% down ($128,000), a 6.4% 30-year loan of $512,000:

P&I:                $3,202/mo
Property tax:         $611/mo  ($7,332 annual in my county)
Insurance:            $158/mo
HOA:                    $0/mo
Maintenance (1%):     $533/mo
Gross:              $4,504/mo

Tax savings (24% bracket, standard deduction already used elsewhere):
  Deductible interest year 1 ≈ $32,400
  Deductible property tax = $7,332 (capped at $10k, fine)
  Total deductions ≈ $39,732
  Tax savings ≈ $9,536/yr ≈ $795/mo

Net cost:           $3,709/mo

Compare that to renting the equivalent for $2,200/month. Buying costs me **$1,509/month more in real cash flow**. The equity portion of the mortgage is only $470 of the P&I in year one — the rest is interest. So of that $1,509 gap, roughly $470 is going into my own pocket as principal, and about $1,039 is genuinely consumed by ownership costs.

That $1,039 is the number to beat. Renting costs $2,200 in cash. Buying consumes $1,039 more per month in real, unrecoverable expense *plus* locks up $128,000 in a down payment. The down payment alone, at 4.3% in a high-yield account (I use the ones I documented in my [HYSA vs CD comparison](/posts/high-yield-savings-vs-cds/)), would earn roughly $458/month tax-free — which is another cost of buying.

### 3. The Break-Even Horizon

Break-even is the month you've held the home long enough that the equity, appreciation, and saved rent have overtaken the transaction costs and the opportunity cost of your down payment.

Standard transaction costs: 2–3% to buy (closing costs), 6–8% to sell (agent commissions, concessions, transfer taxes). Together, that's roughly 8–10% of the purchase price round-trip. On a $640,000 house, that's $51,200–$64,000 you need to earn back just to break even.

I ran the actual break-even with these assumptions: 3% annual appreciation, 6.4% mortgage, 4.3% opportunity cost, 2.5% rent inflation.

| Years Held | Cumulative Cost of Buying | Cumulative Cost of Renting | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | $81,200 | $84,100 | Rent (barely) |
| 5 | $134,600 | $139,900 | Rent (barely) |
| 7 | $188,900 | $195,400 | Buy |
| 10 | $271,000 | $285,600 | Buy |
| 15 | $412,000 | $471,200 | Buy (widely) |

Break-even landed at about **6.5 years** in this scenario. Not 5, not 10. That's a wide window and the exact number moves a lot with appreciation assumptions.

If you're serious about building wealth through homeownership, it's worth reading my [real estate investing for beginners](/posts/real-estate-investing-beginners-ways/) piece — it covers why "my home is my investment" is a harder argument than people assume.

### 4. Your Actual Down Payment (Not the Advertised One)

The 20% down payment myth deserves its own section. Real down payment requirements in 2026:

- Conventional loan: 3% down (with PMI) for first-time buyers
- FHA: 3.5% down (with PMI + up-front fee)
- VA: 0% down (with funding fee)
- Jumbo (over ~$766,550 in most counties): typically 10–20% down

PMI is the silent killer. On a $640,000 house with 3% down ($19,200), PMI ran about $290/month in the quotes I pulled. You pay that until your loan-to-value drops to 80% — which at 3% down takes years unless you refinance or the market appreciates aggressively.

When I ran the 3%-down scenario, break-even stretched from 6.5 years to **9.1 years** because of PMI. That's a massive swing. If you can't stomach 20% down, that's not necessarily a dealbreaker — but it does change the math dramatically.

If you're building the down payment from scratch, my [down payment saving playbook](/posts/how-to-save-for-a-house-down-payment-in-5-years-or-less/) walks through the exact timeline I used to accumulate $48,000.

### 5. Local Market Reality Check

National numbers are useless. Rent vs buy in San Francisco and rent vs buy in Memphis are different questions entirely.

I use three data sources when I run this:

1. **Zillow Observed Rent Index (ZORI)** — updated monthly, check the specific metro
2. **Case-Shiller Home Price Index** — 20-city plus local, updated monthly with 2-month lag
3. **Redfin Data Center** — granular neighborhood-level data, updated every two weeks

As of the data I pulled in October 2026: national rent growth was 3.1% year-over-year (ZORI), home price appreciation was 2.7% (Case-Shiller 20-city, June data), and mortgage rates sat at 6.4% for a 30-year conventional. The spread between rent growth and appreciation has narrowed — which means the "appreciation will bail me out" argument is weaker than it was in 2021.

If appreciation runs at 2.7% instead of the 3% I modeled, break-even moves out to about 7.6 years. If it runs at 5%, break-even drops to 4.8 years. Appreciation assumption is the single most sensitive variable in this entire calculation — be careful with it. I default to 3%, which is roughly the long-term national average over the past century when you account for inflation.

## The Costs Nobody Puts in the Brochure

I want to be honest about the downsides, because the internet is stuffed with cheerleading.

**Closing costs are real and immediate.** My last quoted closing cost on a $640,000 purchase was $14,200 — 2.2% of the price, paid at signing, gone forever. That includes lender fees, title insurance, escrow, appraisal, recording fees, and prepaid interest.

**Maintenance is lumpy.** The 1% rule of thumb is a floor, not a ceiling. My friend's house needed a $19,000 HVAC replacement and a $14,000 roof in the same year. That's a 5% maintenance year. If you're running tight, this will break you.

**Illiquidity.** Selling a house takes 60–120 days and $40,000+ in commissions. If you lose your job and need to relocate in three months, you are not selling without a loss. Renting gives you a 30-day exit.

**Opportunity cost is invisible but real.** That $128,000 down payment, invested in a boring index fund over 10 years at 7% nominal, grows to roughly $252,000. The house's appreciation, on the same money over 10 years at 3%, would generate value growth of about $220,000 on the full $640,000 — but the portion attributable to your down payment is different math entirely. Anyone who tells you the house wins here is usually comparing apples to a very specific crop of oranges.

**Property taxes never stop.** Even after you finish the mortgage, you'll pay property tax and insurance forever. Many renters forget this. A paid-off house still costs me around $11,000/year in my market in taxes and insurance. Renting a comparable place would cost $26,400/year. The gap is smaller than people think once the mortgage is gone.

**Rent control and lease options.** In some markets, especially older cities with rent stabilization, long-term renters can pay drastically below market. My mother-in-law has been in a rent-controlled unit for 18 years and pays 42% below current market. If you're in a placid rent-controlled situation, buying is almost certainly a worse financial decision unless you're buying for reasons other than money.

## When Renting Actually Wins (Not Just Financially)

There are four situations where I unambiguously recommend renting even if the math is currently neutral:

1. **You're likely to move in under 5 years.** Almost every case. The transaction costs eat you.
2. **Your income is volatile.** Freelancers, commission salespeople, startup employees — a mortgage is a fixed obligation that doesn't care about your Q3.
3. **Your emergency fund isn't fully stocked.** If you don't have six months of expenses saved before buying, you're renting from the bank with a much worse landlord. My [emergency fund blueprint](/posts/build-emergency-fund-from-scratch/) walks through the exact numbers.
4. **You're paying off high-interest debt.** Buying a house while carrying $18,000 of credit card debt at 24% APR is a math mistake. Debt first, house second.

There's a fifth: **You're early in your earning years and expect substantial raises.** Renting gives you the flexibility to live closer to work, or to move to a cheaper market if remote work becomes available. Buying locks you into one geography and one commute.

## When Buying Wins

Buying wins clearly when:

- **You're in a market with price-to-rent under 15.** In those markets, buying is genuinely cheaper than renting from day one in many cases.
- **You'll stay 10+ years.** The math compounds favorably over time even in less-favorable markets.
- **You can put down 20% and keep a fully funded emergency fund afterward.** The PMI and vulnerability disappear.
- **You're buying for reasons beyond money.** Stability, community, the ability to renovate, pets, children in a specific school district. These have real value even if they don't show up in a spreadsheet.
- **Your income is stable and rising.** You'll inflate away the mortgage payment as your salary grows.

## The Framework I Actually Use at the Kitchen Table

Here's the exact sequence I run every time, and it takes about 40 minutes:

**Step 1 — Rent the same thing you'd buy for one month, in your head.** Not a smaller apartment. The *same size, same neighborhood, same amenities*. This is the fair comparison, and most people skip it.

**Step 2 — Pull the price-to-rent ratio.** Under 15, proceed with strong buy bias. Over 20, proceed with strong rent bias. 15–20, run the full math.

**Step 3 — Build the total monthly ownership cost.** Not just P&I. Everything. Be honest about maintenance.

**Step 4 — Compute break-even.** Use conservative appreciation (2.5–3%). If break-even is longer than the time you're confident you'll stay, rent.

**Step 5 — Check the 5-year plan.** Career stability, relationship status, family plans, location. The financial math is necessary but not sufficient. If any of those five answers is "I don't know," default to renting for another year.

Then re-run this once a year. That's it. It's not glamorous, but it's honest.

## A Few Tools That Help

I use [Google Sheets](https://sheets.google.com) for the main model — there's a financial function `PMT` that computes principal and interest in one cell:

=PMT(rate/12, years*12, -loan_amount)

For a $512,000 loan at 6.4% over 30 years, that returns $3,202.11 — which matches my lender's quote to the penny. I keep a copy of my working spreadsheet template linked from my [net worth tracking piece](/posts/track-net-worth-importance/), because this all ties back to the balance sheet.

When I'm comparing mortgage rate quotes, I use the [Word Counter](https://word-counter.search123.top/) to sanity-check my rate lock comparison emails — not because word count matters for mortgage decisions, but because it's a quick way to make sure I'm not accidentally sending a half-finished email to a loan officer. That sounds silly, and it is. But it happened once and I'm not proud of it.

For the amortization schedule, I use Bankrate's calculator and cross-check against my own formula. The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/) own calculator is also free and more trustworthy than most commercial sites.

## The Bottom Line

The rent vs buy decision is not about what's universally smarter. It's about your specific five numbers and your specific five-year plan. In my case, the first two times I ran it, the numbers said "keep renting" — and I did, and that was correct, because I moved both times. The third time, September 2026, the math finally flipped because I found a $480,000 property in a market with a price-to-rent ratio of 14.8, and I'd been in the same city for four years with no plans to leave. That's the pattern that produces a yes.

Not the existence of a Zestimate. Not the mild panic that rent is going up. Not a friend's success story.

Just five numbers, run honestly, on a Tuesday night. That's how I'd do it if I were you, and it's how I did it three times before I finally got a "buy" back.
