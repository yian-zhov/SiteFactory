I pulled my first FICO score in 2019 and it was 643. Not terrible, not great — dead average, which is exactly where most people sit and exactly where it costs you the most money without you noticing. Over the next few years I tracked every score change across three bureaus, disputed errors, paid down balances in a specific order, and eventually landed above 800. What I learned is that most "credit score" advice out there answers the wrong question. People ask "what is a good credit score" when what they really need to know is *which range matters for the thing I'm about to do next*, and *which levers actually move the number in 30 to 90 days*.

This article walks through the score ranges, where the cutoffs really sit, and the moves I've personally validated. If you want the short version of the repair plan, I documented that in [the 112-point climb I made in under six months](/posts/improve-credit-score-6-months/), but here I want to go deeper on the *what* and the *why* before the *how*.

## The credit score ranges, and what they actually mean

Two scoring models dominate: FICO (used by about 90% of top lenders, per FICO's own 2024 disclosure) and VantageScore (used more by consumer-facing tools like Credit Karma and some lenders). Both run on a 300–850 scale for their main models. The bands differ slightly, but the practical topology is the same.

| Range | FICO Label | VantageScore Label | What Lenders Typically Offer |
|---|---|---|---|
| 800–850 | Exceptional | Excellent | Best rates, easiest approvals |
| 740–799 | Very Good | Excellent | Near-best rates, minimal friction |
| 670–739 | Good | Good | Standard approvals, decent rates |
| 580–669 | Fair | Fair | Higher rates, some denials |
| 300–579 | Poor | Poor | Subprime only; deposits required |

FICO's own consumer site defines "good" as 670–739 — that's the official cutoff. But here's the part almost nobody says out loud: **the definition of "good" depends on what you're applying for.** A 720 is fine for a credit card. It's borderline for a mortgage, where lenders often price loans in tiers with the biggest rate break at 760 or 780. Auto loans have their own tiering that typically rewards 720+.

When I refinanced my auto loan in early 2025, my score was 781. The loan officer pulled up the pricing matrix and showed me the exact band cuts: 720, 760, and 800. Moving from 759 to 760 would have saved me roughly 0.4% APR. Moving from 799 to 800 would have saved another 0.25%. Those single points were worth hundreds of dollars over the loan term. That's why "what is a good credit score" is the wrong question — the right one is "what's the cutoff for the product I'm about to apply for?"

## The average American score, and why yours probably differs

FICO reported in late 2024 that the national average FICO score was 717. VantageScore's average has hovered around 701. Both have crept up over the past few years as pandemic-era savings and stimulus inflated scores, then softened slightly as those buffers drained.

That average matters less than you think. Credit scores are not graded against other people. They're graded against each lender's internal risk model, and those models differ. Two lenders looking at the same 712 can offer you wildly different terms. This is why shopping around — especially for mortgages and auto loans — isn't just polite, it's financially essential. Mortgage inquiries within a 14–45 day window (depending on the model) count as a single hard pull, so rate shopping doesn't hurt you the way people fear.

I noticed this in 2024 when I applied for two credit cards on the same day with a score around 745. One approved me instantly with a $12,000 limit. The other declined me entirely, citing "too many recent inquiries" — even though the inquiry was from the other card. Lenders each have their own tolerance thresholds, and your score is only one input among many.

## What actually goes into the score

The FICO 8 breakdown is publicly documented and hasn't changed materially in years:

- **Payment history: 35%**
- **Amounts owed (utilization): 30%**
- **Length of credit history: 15%**
- **Credit mix: 10%**
- **New credit inquiries: 10%**

FICO 10T and VantageScore 4.0 reweight these (they put more emphasis on trended data — the trajectory of your balances over 24+ months), but the underlying logic is the same. Two categories — payment history and utilization — account for 65% of your score. If you're optimizing anything else before those two, you're polishing the trim while the roof leaks.

I want to flag a common misconception here. The 10% credit mix category isn't a suggestion to go open a store card just to add variety. It's a light touch. In my experience, adding a new account to "improve mix" can *lower* your score in the short term because of the hard inquiry and the reduction in average account age. Skip that strategy unless you're already in a good place and have a specific reason for the new account.

## The utilization myth, and what actually works

The standard advice is "keep utilization below 30%." That's not wrong, but it's a floor, not a ceiling. FICO's internal research has consistently shown that consumers with the highest scores tend to have utilization below 10%, and often below 6%. The 30% figure exists mostly to give people a salvageable target.

The bigger revelation, which took me embarrassingly long to figure out: **utilization is calculated at statement close, not at payment due date.** If your statement closes on the 15th and you pay on the 20th, the balance from the 15th is what gets reported to the bureaus. This is why people who carry no balance month-to-month can still have high reported utilization. My own score jumped 34 points in one cycle when I shifted my payments to two days before statement close instead of on the due date.

There's a secondary nuance: some issuers report mid-cycle, especially if you pay your balance to zero mid-month. Others report only once per month, typically the day your statement generates. If you're trying to time a score bump before a mortgage application, call your issuer and ask which day they report. I did this for three cards in 2025 and got three different answers — one reported on the 1st, one on the 8th, one on the 22nd.

## The 6–12 month improvement plan I'd give a friend

If your score is in the 580–660 range and you want to get to 740+, the plan is not exotic. It's unglamorous and slow and works. Here's what I did, in order:

**Month 1–2: Audit and dispute.** Pull your reports from AnnualCreditReport.com — all three bureaus, free weekly. Look for: accounts you don't recognize, late payments you can prove were paid on time, balances that are wrong, duplicate accounts. I found one delinquent medical bill from 2017 that had been paid but never updated. Disputing it online took 20 minutes and boosted my score 41 points when it fell off. For a full walkthrough of how to read the report, [the beginner's guide to understanding your credit report](/posts/understand-your-credit-report/) covers every section.

**Month 2–3: Attack utilization.** List every card's limit and current balance. Pay down the card with the highest utilization first, not the highest balance. Counterintuitive, but utilization is a ratio — moving $500 off a maxed $1,000 card does more than $500 off a $10,000 card. If you can, ask for credit line increases the same week. My combined limits went from $18,400 to $31,200 in two months just by asking, which mathmatically lowered my utilization without me paying down a dollar.

**Month 3–6: Set up autopay on everything.** One missed payment can drop your score 50–100 points for up to 7 years. Autopay on minimums (or statements) eliminates the single most destructive mistake available to you. This is the highest-leverage five minutes you'll spend all year.

**Month 6–12: Let time do its job.** Age of accounts is 15% of your score, and it can't be rushed. The single best action is often inaction — don't close that 8-year-old card you never use. Put a $5 subscription on it and set autopay once a year so it doesn't get closed for inactivity.

That sequence, applied consistently, took me from the mid-600s to the low 800s. It's not magic. It's just that most people don't actually do it. ## What I got wrong, and what I'd tell you to avoid

Three mistakes cost me months and real points:

**I closed a credit card "because I didn't use it."** This dropped my available credit by $6,000, immediately pushed my utilization from 9% to 23%, and shortened my average account age. My score dropped 28 points in one cycle. Reopening took six months. The card was 11 years old. I should have kept it open for free.

**I opened a store card to "diversify my credit mix."** The 10% credit mix category is not worth sacrificing a hard inquiry and a fresh account for. My score dropped 11 points immediately and netted maybe 3 points back over two years. Net loss.

**I didn't shop my mortgage rate.** I applied to one lender, got a quote, and took it. Only later did I realize inquiries within a 45-day window from multiple mortgage lenders count as one. I cost myself possibly $4,000–$8,000 over the loan term. If you're about to make a major purchase, [the rent vs. buy breakdown I ran for four years](/posts/renting-vs-buying-home-financially/) covers the rate-shopping angle in more detail.

The honest caveat on all of this: credit scores are a measure of how profitable you are to a lender, not how financially healthy you actually are. Someone can be 800+ with $80,000 in credit card debt and a $300,000 income, and someone else can be 620 with zero debt and $40,000 in savings because they've never borrowed anything. The score rewards the *use* of credit, not the *avoidance* of it. That's a design quirk worth being aware of, and it matters when you're planning your financial life around what the number will or won't give you access to.

## Where your score touches everything else

The knock-on effects of a score in the 700s vs. 600s are hard to overstate. Insurance premiums in most states are permitted to use credit-based insurance scores (a cousin of the credit score) as a rating factor. Auto insurance in particular can swing 30–60% based on credit. Deposits for utilities can shift from $0 to $400. Cell phone carriers, landlords, and even some employers pull credit.

For someone building a financial base — say, paying off consumer debt while saving a down payment — the score is functionally a gate. If you're pursuing the [14-month emergency fund path I wrote up](/posts/build-emergency-fund-from-scratch/), or you're working through [the budget structure that finally stuck for me](/posts/create-monthly-budget-that-works/), keeping your score above 720 keeps more options open with less friction. Same goes for anyone working through [the credit card payoff process](/posts/pay-off-credit-card-debt-fast-strategies/) — you don't want to hit an emergency while your score is in the 500s and the only lending available to you charges 29.99%.

Where the score touches nothing: your actual net worth trajectory, your savings rate, your investment returns. Those are functions of income, spending, and time. The score is a tool in service of those, not a substitute for them. I've met people with 820 scores and no retirement savings and people with 660 scores and $400,000 in taxable investments. Prioritize the wealth-building mechanics — [the average-salary wealth build I tracked for 47 months](/posts/build-wealth-on-average-salary/) is a good starting point — and treat the credit score as a supporting actor.

## A quick reality check on "hacks" and gimmicks

The internet is full of credit score "hacks." Most of them are worse than useless. A few worth explicitly dismissing:

- **"Pay for delete" promises** — some collectors will remove a paid negative, but only if you negotiate it in writing before paying. Paying first and asking later gets you nothing.
- **Credit repair companies** — anything they do (disputing errors), you can do for free. Under the Credit Repair Organizations Act, they cannot collect payment before services are performed. If they demand payment upfront, walk.
- **"Authorized user boost" schemes** — some services sell you a spot on a stranger's card. This technically works, but it's a violation of most cardholder agreements and violates CFPB guidance. Not worth the risk.
- **"Rent reporting" add-ons** — legit services (Experian RentBureau, RentReporters) do work, but only if your landlord participates. Ask before paying.

What actually works is boring: pay on time, keep utilization low, let accounts age, don't over-apply, and dispute errors. That's it. That's the whole strategy. Anyone who says otherwise is selling you something.

## Where to start this week

If you take three actions from this article, do these. First, pull your reports from AnnualCreditReport.com — free weekly, all three bureaus — and dispute every error you find. That single step took me from 643 to 684 in one cycle. Second, find your statement close dates across all your cards and shift payments so your reported balance is under 10% of your limit. That's usually worth 20–50 points within two cycles. Third, set autopay on every card and loan, no exceptions, for at least the minimum. This prevents the single most catastrophic thing that can happen to your score.

The rest — new accounts, credit mix, age of accounts — happens on its own if you don't actively sabotage it. The people I know with 800+ scores are not credit geniuses. They just haven't done anything stupid for a decade. That's a broader principle, honestly, and it's the same one that underlies most of personal finance: the boring, consistent behaviors beat the clever moves. Every single time.

Your score is a lagging indicator. If you build the underlying habits — a working budget, an emergency fund, deliberate debt payoff, and a consistent investing plan — the score follows. If you chase the score in isolation without those habits, you'll get a decent number and still be financially fragile. Do the boring work. Let the number catch up.
